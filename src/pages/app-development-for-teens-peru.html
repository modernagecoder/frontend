School and apps

Do Peruvian secondary schools teach app development?

Not as a subject. The national curriculum treats digital skills as something every area should use, and the secundaria timetable has no computing course. App building happens outside school, in a pilot, or at home.

What the curriculum asks for

MINEDU's national curriculum, the CNEB, lists competency 28, about working in virtual environments created by technology, among the competencies that cross all areas rather than belonging to one. A regular-day secundaria timetable fills 35 hours a week with Matemática, Comunicación, Inglés, sciences and Educación para el Trabajo; no slot in it is called computing or programming.

So a student can finish 5° de secundaria having used many apps for schoolwork without once seeing how one is put together.

Where Peru is testing more

A thousand students in the last two years of secundaria in Lima Norte are learning Python in the Fábrica de Programadores, a MINEDU pilot that ends with the PCEP certification exam. And the OPI, the Peruvian computing olympiad run by UTEC, costs nothing to enter for any secundaria student.

Both are good signs, and both are about programming in general. Neither teaches a teenager how to design screens, store a user's data or publish to a phone, which is the gap an app course fills.

Our Spanish-language page for the same teenagers, crear apps: curso para adolescentes, looks at how Peruvian teens actually go online and teaches a sign-up form lesson. Here we look at something parents ask us about just as often: the age rules.

The age-14 line

At what age can a teenager in Peru have their own app accounts?

In Peru the number that matters is usually 14, not 13. Google sets 14 for Peru, and Peru's data protection regulation lets young people from that age give consent for their own data in some cases. Here are the lines, from each organisation's own page.

Why this matters in an app course

Our teen app course uses Firebase for sign-in and storage, and Firebase works with a Google account. In Peru, a 13 year old is below Google's line for managing an account alone, so a parent sets it up through Family Link or creates a project the teen works inside. We explain this at the free class rather than in week six.

It also matters for the apps teenagers build. If a 16 year old writes an app for classmates in 2° de secundaria, those users are under 14, and Peru's regulation expects a parent's consent for a digital service aimed at them.

What we do in class

Teenagers learn to design an app that asks for as little as possible: a nickname instead of a full name, no phone number, no photo unless the feature truly needs one. When an app must know a user's age, they learn to compute it correctly, which is the lesson further down this page.

Parents of under-14s give consent for their child to join our classes, and we never ask a child to type in their parents' work or money details, which the regulation also protects.

The age rule is the minimum, not the method. Even where a tool is allowed, we teach teenagers to write the feature themselves first, then ask the assistant to review it, not the other way round. The Peruvian computing olympiad makes the same point in its own way: UTEC's 2025 rules banned generative AI for solving the problems. A teen who can only build with a chatbot open cannot build when it is closed, and contests, exams and interviews close it.

A lesson teens enjoy

How does an app work out how old someone is?

Most first attempts subtract the birth year from this year. That is wrong for about half the year. Here is the lesson, with three invented users and a date of 9 November 2026.

The task

An app for teenagers in Peru must decide whether a new user may sign in alone, at 14 or over, or needs a parent first. Three users try on 9 November 2026: Lucía, born 9 November 2012; Mateo, born 25 December 2012; and Valeria, born 29 February 2012.

Before running anything, the mentor asks the class for the right answers. Lucía turns 14 today. Mateo will not be 14 until Christmas. Valeria has a birthday that only exists in leap years.

Why it matters

With the age-14 line from the table above, a wrong age is not a small bug. It decides whether a parent is asked for consent at all.

It is also a classic test of whether a student checks their own code. Version 1 looks correct, runs without errors and gives three plausible answers. One of them is wrong.

The fix is one comparison. If this year's birthday has not happened yet, the person is one year younger than the subtraction says. Python compares pairs of numbers element by element, so (today.month, today.day) < (born.month, born.day) asks exactly "is today earlier in the year than the birthday?".

The last two lines start the liveliest discussion of the lesson. In 2026 there is no 29 February, so when does Valeria turn 14? Version 2 says 1 March, because 28 February comes before 29 February in the comparison. Some systems choose 28 February instead. Neither is a bug; it is a decision, and good code makes the decision visible with a test, so nobody changes it by accident.

  1. Predict first. Without running it, what does Version 1 say for someone born on 31 December 2012, checked on 1 January 2027?
  2. Write the tests. Turn the four extra lines of Version 2 into checks that stop the program if any answer changes.
  3. Move it into the app. In the React Native course the same rule is written in JavaScript. Translate it, then run the same four tests.
  4. Ask less. Could the app work with "Are you 14 or over?" and no birth date at all? Discuss what you gain and what you lose.

We wrote the code, the three users, the dates and the printed output for this page; it ran under Python 3 on 9 November 2026. The threshold of 14 follows the line Google publishes for Peru.

Language of the classes

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. English is the one teaching language in all our live online classes, and our mentors in India are used to teenagers who are still growing into it.

How it feels for a Peruvian teenager

Mentors speak slowly, repeat instructions in other words, and give a student time to answer. A teen who replies half in Spanish is understood and given the English phrase. After a few weeks most stop noticing, because the class is mainly about the code on the screen.

App development is a good subject to learn in English: JavaScript keywords, error messages, React Native documentation and nearly every tutorial are in English already.

What the English figures suggest

EF's English Proficiency Index 2025 scores Lima 555, in its high band. The same edition scores Peruvians aged 18 to 20 at 469, in the low band, which suggests many young people leave school before their English is comfortable.

An hour of app building in English twice a week is extra listening and speaking practice that a teenager does not experience as a lesson. Parents tell us this is half the reason they chose it.

If you are not sure whether your teenager's English is ready, the free class answers that. The mentor checks how comfortably they follow, and tells you honestly. For a broader look at teen coding in Peru, our page on clases de programación para adolescentes covers games, competitions and AI too.

App development for teens in Peru: questions and answers

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. English is the only teaching language in every live online class. Mentors keep a calm pace, rephrase when a teen looks unsure and soon get used to each accent; if a student answers partly in Spanish, the mentor replies with the English word. JavaScript and its error messages are in English in any case.

How old must my child be to start app development?

Our teen app course starts at 13. A younger child, or a 12 year old in 1° de secundaria, usually starts with our coding courses for children and moves across later.

Does my teenager need any coding experience?

No. The teen app course starts from zero. If they already know some JavaScript or Python, the mentor moves faster through the early weeks.

Can a 13 year old in Peru have a Google account for the course?

Google lists 14 as the minimum age in Peru to manage your own Google Account. For a 13 year old, a parent creates and manages one with Family Link, or sets up the project the teen works in. We explain it at the free class.

Which AI tools will my teenager use?

Only tools whose terms allow their age, such as GitHub Copilot from 13 with a mentor reviewing, or ChatGPT with a parent's permission. We do not use tools set at 18, such as Claude or Replit, in teen classes.

Will my teen just copy code from AI?

No. They write each feature themselves first and use an assistant, where allowed, to review it afterwards. Mentors ask them to explain every line they hand in.

What time are classes?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India runs 10 hours 30 minutes ahead of Peru in every season, so a 17:00 class in Peru is 03:30 the following day for the mentor.

Does my teen need an iPhone or an Android phone?

Either works. With Expo, teens test their app on whatever phone the family has. A computer is needed to write the code; a phone alone is not enough.

How much does it cost?

Fees appear here in US dollars and the card is charged in dollars, which your bank changes into soles. Opened from India, the page shows rupees instead. No card is needed for the free first class.

Will it help at school?

It sits alongside school, not inside it: secundaria has no computing course, and we award no Peruvian grade. What it builds is logical thinking, English and a project portfolio, useful for university applications and contests like the OPI.

Is there a certificate?

Passing the final exam earns a certificate of completion that we issue ourselves. MINEDU and schools have no part in it, so treat it as a record of the course; the app your teenager built is the better proof.
