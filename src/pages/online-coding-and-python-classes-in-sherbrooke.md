## Where coding meets a Sherbrooke student at school and at CEGEP

Sherbrooke follows the Québec Education Program set by the Ministère de l'Éducation. We read the English versions of the ministry documents on 22 October 2026. Words in quotation marks come from the ministry; everything else is our summary.

### The 2026 Digital Competency Framework

The ministry's framework is "organized into 12 dimensions" and names, as a guiding principle, the "integration of AI as a component of digital technology". In our reading, one of its elements does ask students to build or adapt a digital tool with programming, and computational thinking appears among its sample themes.

### Secondary IV and V math

In Secondary Cycle Two students choose between "Cultural, Social and Technical (CST), Technical and Scientific (TS) and Science (S)". The program says "All of these options allow students to enroll in a preuniversity program." Our [guide to the three options](/quebec-secondary-4-math-cst-ts-science) goes further.

### CEGEP and the R score

Quebec describes a pre-university CEGEP program as one "that requires two years of full-time study", and a technical program as three years. It adds that "Universities use the R score to analyze applications". Our [CEGEP computer science page](/cegep-computer-science-preparation) covers preparation.

What the framework does not do is put a weekly programming course on every timetable. Computing in Quebec is spread across subjects and competencies, so how much code a Sherbrooke student actually writes depends a great deal on the school and the teacher. A learner who wants to be fluent by CEGEP, especially one aiming at a science or computer science program, usually needs time outside school to write programs long enough to go wrong in interesting ways.

## How long are Sherbrooke's bike paths? Python, a city file and a map that lies a little

The Ville de Sherbrooke publishes its bike path layer, "Pistes cyclables", as open data under CC BY 4.0. Our students count it in Python and then discover that the length column is not in ground metres. Every figure below is our own calculation from the file downloaded on 22 October 2026.

**Our count of the Ville de Sherbrooke file (1,257 rows), by TYPEVOIE. "Map km" adds up the SHAPE__Length column; "ground km" applies our latitude correction. English glosses are our translations**

| TYPEVOIE in the file | Our gloss | Segments | Map km (Web Mercator) | Ground km (our correction) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bande cyclable unidirectionnelle | One-way painted lane | 514 | 160.6 | 112.8 |
| Piste cyclable bidirectionnelle | Two-way separate path | 353 | 150.3 | 105.6 |
| Chaussée désignée | Designated shared roadway | 177 | 55.1 | 38.7 |
| Accotement revêtu | Paved shoulder | 88 | 49.5 | 34.7 |
| Piste cyclable unidirectionnelle | One-way separate path | 125 | 29.5 | 20.7 |

This is the Python that prints the right-hand columns. It needs nothing beyond the standard library and the CSV in the same folder:

```
import csv
import math

SCALE = math.cos(math.radians(45.4))   # Sherbrooke sits near latitude 45.4 N

counts = {}
km = {}
with open("pistes-cyclables.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        kind = row["TYPEVOIE"]
        metres = float(row["SHAPE__Length"]) * SCALE
        counts[kind] = counts.get(kind, 0) + 1
        km[kind] = km.get(kind, 0) + metres / 1000

for kind in sorted(km, key=km.get, reverse=True):
    print(kind, counts[kind], "segments", round(km[kind], 1), "km")
```

### 1. The naive sum

Students first add up SHAPE__Length and get 445.0 km. It looks like an answer. Then they read the dataset description, which gives the coordinate system as WGS 1984 Web Mercator, WKID 3857, and ask what a "metre" means on that map.

### 2. Measure it a second way

From the GeoJSON copy of the same layer, they convert every vertex back to latitude and longitude and add great-circle distances. That gives 312.2 km on the ground, about 0.70 of the map figure, across latitudes 45.274 to 45.518.

### 3. Explain the gap

Web Mercator stretches distances by roughly one over the cosine of the latitude. At 45.4 degrees north the cosine is 0.7022, so multiplying by it brings the map lengths within a fraction of a kilometre of the careful measurement in every row of the table.

### What the file still cannot tell you

A one-way lane painted on both sides of a street appears as two segments, so lane kilometres are not street kilometres. The file says nothing about surface, width, winter clearing or how busy a path is. Three rows have an empty network name, and 818 rows belong to the "Réseau utilitaire". A student who writes those limits down beside the number has done real data work.

|---|---|---|---|
| Beaver Computing Challenge | Our summary: Grade 10 or below in three levels; no coding needed | 9 to 20 November 2026 | English only |
| Canadian Senior Mathematics Contest | Includes "CÉGEP students" | 18 November 2026 | See CEMC |

## An AI tool would have given you 445 km

Which is the point of learning to check.

Ask a chatbot to total the Sherbrooke bike path file and it will write a tidy script that sums SHAPE__Length and prints 445.0. The code runs. The answer is wrong by more than 130 km, because the question of what a map metre means never came up. Nothing in the output warns you.

We teach Python so that learners ask that question themselves: what unit is this, where did it come from, does the result make sense against something I know? Older students use AI tools under the age rules we follow, but as a draft to inspect, never as an answer to hand in.

Quebec's own framework talks about integrating AI into digital competency; integration starts with knowing when the tool is confidently wrong. The longer argument is in [why coding is still worth learning in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
