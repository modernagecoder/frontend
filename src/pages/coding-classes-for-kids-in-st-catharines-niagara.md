## From Grade 1 to Grade 8: both ends of the Ontario coding ladder for Niagara children

Schools in St. Catharines and across Niagara teach the Ontario curriculum. The overall coding expectation, C3, uses the same words in every grade from 1 to 8; what changes is the detail. Below, the Grade 1 and Grade 8 wording as the ministry&rsquo;s site gave it on 22 October 2026, each paired with what happens in our lessons.

- **Grade 1, in brief**: The first rung (our summary, not the ministry&rsquo;s wording): children write and run short programs whose steps happen one after another. Our answer: Six-year-olds program a partner first: two steps forward, turn, clap. Then the same steps as blocks. Order matters, and they find that out by getting it wrong.
- **Grade 8, C3.2**: Children &ldquo;read and alter existing code involving the analysis of data in order to inform and communicate decisions, and describe how changes to the code affect the outcomes and the efficiency of the code&rdquo;. Our answer: Reading someone else&rsquo;s data program is a skill of its own. We hand twelve-year-olds a working script, change one line together, and ask how the answer, and the decision it supports, would shift.
- **Grade 8, C3.1**: Code &ldquo;that involves the analysis of data in order to inform and communicate decisions&rdquo;. Our answer: The top of the ladder. A twelve-year-old who has worked with real files is ready for it: read data, summarise it, and say what it means.
- **Grade 8, notes**: &ldquo;Data can be stored in lists, or input into a program, in order to find solutions to problems and make decisions.&rdquo; Our answer: Lists come first in our Python course for exactly this reason. Once a child can loop over a list, any file becomes a list of rows.
- **Grade 8, notes**: &ldquo;By combining mathematical and coding concepts and skills, students can write programs to draw conclusions from data.&rdquo; Our answer: Drawing conclusions is the hard part, and it is not coding. Our parks project ends with children writing one honest sentence about what the numbers show.

One Grade 8 example in the ministry&rsquo;s notes is pleasingly practical: &ldquo;use a sample of a schoolyard cleanup and apply an appropriate scale to make predictions about the entire cleanup&rdquo;. It is proportional reasoning done by a program, and it is the same skill as turning counts into percentages, which is what the Niagara Falls project below asks for. Between Grades 1 and 8 the ladder adds repeating events, nested events, conditions, efficiency and sub-programs; our [Ontario coding guide for Grades 1 to 8](/ontario-coding-curriculum-grades-1-to-8) quotes each rung.

## Most parks, or most land? Two kinds of percentage from the City of Niagara Falls parks file

We looked for parks data across the St. Catharines - Niagara census metropolitan area. The City of St. Catharines publishes a parks layer, but its record carries no licence, so we did not use it. The City of Niagara Falls, a municipality in the same metropolitan area, publishes its parks under an open government licence. So the numbers here describe Niagara Falls only, and we worked them out ourselves from the copy we downloaded on 22 October 2026.

**City of Niagara Falls Community Parks and Trail Lands: our calculation of each park type&rsquo;s share of the 109 parks and of the 441.61 hectares in the file**

| Type | Parks | Share of parks | Hectares | Share of land |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NEIGHBOURHOOD | 61 | 56.0% | 91.29 | 20.7% |
| CITY WIDE | 26 | 23.9% | 220.68 | 50.0% |
| COMMUNITY | 22 | 20.2% | 129.64 | 29.4% |

Neighbourhood parks are more than half of all the parks but only about a fifth of the land. City-wide parks are under a quarter of the parks yet half the land. Both statements are true at once, and which one you quote depends on the question you were asked. Both shares come out of one short Python program, which twelve-year-olds in our classes write themselves:

```
import csv

parks = {}
land = {}
with open("niagara-falls-parks.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        kind = row["Type"]
        parks[kind] = parks.get(kind, 0) + 1
        land[kind] = land.get(kind, 0) + float(row["Area_HA"])

total_parks = sum(parks.values())
total_land = sum(land.values())
for kind in parks:
    print(kind, round(100 * parks[kind] / total_parks, 1), "% of parks,",
          round(100 * land[kind] / total_land, 1), "% of land")
```

### Two dictionaries, one loop

parks counts each type; land adds up each type&rsquo;s hectares. Both fill in the same pass through the file, and the totals come from adding each dictionary&rsquo;s values.

### Share means divide

A share is part divided by whole, times 100. The same type gets two different shares because the whole is different: 109 parks in one case, 441.61 hectares in the other.

### A decision to inform

This is the Grade 8 idea in small. If a town wanted more parks within walking distance, which share matters? If it wanted room for sports fields? Children argue it out, using the numbers.

### Why the shares add up to 100.1

Add the rounded park shares: 56.0 + 23.9 + 20.2 = 100.1. The land shares, 20.7 + 50.0 + 29.4, also make 100.1. Nothing is wrong. Each share was rounded to one decimal place on its own, and the small rounding gains happened to pile up. Unrounded, they make exactly 100. Children who meet this once stop being alarmed when a newspaper chart adds to 99 or 101.

The City scores its own file, too. The dataset description shows an &ldquo;Open Data Quality Rating (Beta): 89.1/100&rdquo; as of 19 October 2026, with separate scores for Completeness (82.4), Validity (88.2), Recency (92.3), Uniqueness (100.0) and Understandability (90.8). A class can discuss what each word means for a data user; we do not average the scores ourselves. In our download, 90 records are dedicated parks, 15 dedicated trails, 3 non-developed and 1 a dog park, and 35 parks are under one hectare. We print no park names, since several honour people. ## Why should a Niagara child learn to code when AI can do it?

Because deciding what a number means is still a person&rsquo;s job.

An AI tool could produce the parks table in seconds. It could also write either headline, that most Niagara Falls parks are neighbourhood parks or that most park land is city-wide, without saying that both are true. A child who has calculated both shares knows that the choice of percentage is a choice of story, and knows to ask which one is being told.

Under-13s in our classes do not use adult chatbots. Their teacher shows them how AI learns from examples, and where it slips, through hands-on activities. Most of every lesson is their own reasoning: setting out the steps, trying them, checking the result against common sense.

Ontario ends its elementary coding path with using data to inform decisions, and that is precisely the part of the work that stays human. The longer argument is in [why coding is still worth learning](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
