## How much water fits inside Waterloo&rsquo;s water mains? A geometry lesson on city data

The City of Waterloo publishes the pipes that carry its drinking water as open data. Students read the file, model each pipe as a cylinder and then argue about what the model leaves out. Every count, sum and volume below is ours, not the City&rsquo;s.

The dataset is called Water Distribution Mains. The City&rsquo;s description says it represents the pipeline system that conveys municipal drinking water through a network of distribution mains within the City of Waterloo, and it adds a caution that teachers should read aloud: these records are based upon available information, compiled from a variety of sources, some of which has not been verified. We queried the layer on 22 October 2026 and received 8,118 rows, each one a length of pipe with a diameter, a length in metres, a material, an install date and an owner.

Across the eleven sizes our sums come to 480.55 km of pipe and a modelled 26,180 cubic metres, about 26.2 million litres. Those two totals are ours, built row by row from the City&rsquo;s file; the City publishes neither. The surprise in the table is the 150 mm row. Pipes of that size make up 39.6 percent of the length but only 12.8 percent of the volume, while the 300, 600 and 750 mm pipes together hold 64.5 percent of it. Volume grows with the square of the diameter, and a table makes a Grade 8 student feel that in a way a formula on its own rarely does.

The Python that produces the table reads the nine downloaded pages, skips one placeholder row and adds up length by size before applying the cylinder formula. It uses only the standard library:

```
import json, math
from collections import defaultdict

length_by_size = defaultdict(float)
for offset in range(0, 9000, 1000):
    with open(f"mains_{offset}.json", encoding="utf-8") as page:
        for feature in json.load(page)["features"]:
            pipe = feature["attributes"]
            if pipe["DIAMETER"] == 999:
                continue  # a placeholder, not a size
            length_by_size[pipe["DIAMETER"]] += pipe["LENGTH_M"]

total = 0
for size, metres in sorted(length_by_size.items()):
    radius = size / 2000  # millimetres to metres, then halved
    volume = math.pi * radius ** 2 * metres
    total += volume
    print(size, round(metres / 1000, 2), "km", round(volume), "m3")
print("modelled volume:", round(total), "cubic metres")
```

### 1. Name the model

A main is treated as a cylinder, so its volume is pi times the radius squared times the length. Students write down what that assumes: a perfectly round pipe, full of water, with an inside width equal to the diameter in the file.

### 2. Fix the units first

The file gives lengths in metres and diameters in what are almost certainly millimetres, since 150, 200 and 300 match standard pipe sizes. Dividing by 2,000 turns a diameter in millimetres into a radius in metres in one step, and students must explain why it is 2,000.

### 3. Distrust the extremes

Sort the file and odd values appear. 303 rows are shorter than one metre, the longest is over 2.5 km, and each oddity is a question for the student, not a number to quietly delete.

### Three things in the file that are not what they seem

One row, OBJECTID 1015, gives a diameter of 999, a value no other pipe shares and almost certainly a placeholder, so we leave it out and say so. 47 rows carry an install year of 1850 and no row has any year from 1851 to 1907; 10 of those 47 are listed as PVC, so the date column for those rows needs checking before anyone draws a history from it. And the Shape__Length column is measured in degrees of latitude and longitude, not metres, so only LENGTH_M is used. Finding those three is the real lesson.

Other columns support a second session. The City is listed as the owner of 7,765 rows, the Region of Waterloo of 194, and 159 are shared. By material, the file lists 4,871 rows of polyvinyl chloride, 1,922 of cast iron and 1,174 of ductile iron. Grouping install years by decade shows cast iron dominating the 1950s and 1960s and PVC taking over from the 1980s, a neat two-way table for MDM4U students. ## Worked problem: scale factors and a power function from one pipe

Written by us for Grade 9 to Grade 12 students, using sizes that appear in the Waterloo file. It starts with arithmetic and ends in the language of MHF4U.

**Question.** A 100 m length of 300 mm main is modelled as a full cylinder. (a) How much water does it hold? (b) How much would a 150 mm main of the same length hold, and why is the answer not half? (c) Write the volume of a 10 m section as a function of its diameter and find the diameter that holds exactly 1 cubic metre.

**Step 1, convert before you calculate.** A diameter of 300 mm is 0.3 m, so the radius is 0.15 m. The volume is pi times 0.15 squared times 100, which is 7.0686 cubic metres. One cubic metre is 1,000 litres, so the pipe holds about 7,069 litres.

**Step 2, reason with the scale factor.** Halving the diameter halves the radius, and the radius is squared, so the cross-section shrinks by a factor of one quarter. The 150 mm main holds 1.7671 cubic metres, exactly a quarter of 7.0686. A student who answers &ldquo;half&rdquo; has used a linear rule on a quadratic relationship, the single most common error we see in Grade 9 measurement.

**Step 3, build the function.** For a 10 m section, V(d) equals pi over 4 times d squared times 10, with d in metres. It is a power function of degree 2, defined here only for positive d. Setting V(d) equal to 1 and solving gives d squared equals 4 divided by 10 pi, so d is about 0.3568 m, or roughly 357 mm. That diameter is not in the file, and noticing that is part of the answer: real pipes come in set sizes, so an engineer would choose the next size up.

**Step 4, check the claim in the data.** If volume grows with the square of the diameter, one kilometre of 600 mm pipe should hold as much as sixteen kilometres of 150 mm pipe, because 600 divided by 150 is 4 and 4 squared is 16. Our table agrees in spirit: 20.75 km of 600 mm pipe hold 5,868 cubic metres, far more than the 190.18 km of 150 mm pipe at 3,361.

**Try it yourself.** (1) Find the volume of the longest single row in the file, 2,545 m of 750 mm pipe. (2) Write the inverse of V(d) for a 10 m section, so that it gives the diameter for any volume, and state its domain. (3) In one sentence, explain why the modelled total is probably larger than the water really inside the pipes. Answers are discussed in the free lesson.

## Can a Waterloo student just ask an AI to do the math?

It can produce an answer. It cannot tell the student whether the answer means anything.

Hand an AI assistant the mains file and ask how much water the pipes hold, and it will multiply happily. It will not notice that 999 is a placeholder, that one column is in degrees, or that a diameter in millimetres must be converted before it is squared. Each of those slips changes the answer by orders of magnitude, and only a student who understands the formula sees the problem.

So our teachers ask for a prediction before every calculation and an explanation after it. Older students meet AI tools under the age rules we follow, and use them to check work they have already done, never to do the work. Marked school assignments always stay the student&rsquo;s own.

The point of Grade 9 modelling, and of every contest CEMC writes, is judgment about whether a number makes sense. The longer argument is in [our case for learning to think before using AI](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
