## Build a score for towns, then watch the weights pick the winner

Three census shares, scaled so that the lowest town scores 0 and the highest 1, then combined with weights that add up to one. The learner runs all 66 combinations in steps of a tenth.

| Indicator | Skerries | Rank alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Households with broadband | 94.2% | 13th |
| Adults with an honours degree or higher | 37.2% | 7th |
| Journeys taking under 15 minutes | 37.9% | 26th |

### Equal weights

Give each indicator a third of the score and Skerries comes fourth of the 53 towns. On its own that looks like a finding about Skerries. It is really a finding about equal weights.

### Every weighting

Across the 66 weightings Skerries ranks anywhere from 3rd to 26th. It is in the top five under 19 of them and the top ten under 47. Put all the weight on short journeys and it falls to 26th.

### Four winners

The town in first place changes too: Maynooth under 38 of the weightings, Shannon under 18, Letterkenny under 6 and Portmarnock under 4. Each could headline its own "number one town" list without a single figure being wrong.

| Pair of indicators | Correlation |
| --- | --- |
| Broadband and degrees | 0.70 |
| Broadband and short journeys | minus 0.74 |
| Degrees and short journeys | minus 0.50 |

### A league table is a set of weights, dressed as a fact

When indicators pull in opposite directions, as broadband and short journeys do here, the weights decide the order. Across all 53 towns the median gap between a town's highest and lowest position is 33 places. Any ranking built this way should come with its ingredients, its weights and a note of how far the order moves when the weights change. Without those three, it is an opinion that happens to be arithmetic.

The learner computes each share for every town, rescales each so the lowest town scores 0 and the highest 1, and writes a function that takes three weights and returns the ranked list. A double loop generates every weighting that adds to one in steps of a tenth and records where each town lands. From that record come the numbers on this page: Skerries' highest and lowest position, how often each town is first, and how widely each town's rank swings. The final exercise is to write the paragraph that would have to accompany a published version of the table.

Reading any ranking

## Three questions for every list of places

The same questions work for rankings of towns, schools, universities or products.

### What went in?

A different trio of indicators would produce a different table. Choosing them is already a view about what matters.

### How was it weighted?

Equal weights sound neutral but are a choice like any other. Here they put Skerries fourth; other defensible weights put it twenty-sixth.

### How much does it move?

A result that survives most reasonable weightings is worth reporting. One that flips with small changes is a result about the weights.

What this page does not claim

The score is made up for teaching and says nothing about where anyone should live. The three indicators were chosen to show the effect of weights, not to measure quality of life, and the towns at the bottom of any weighting are deliberately not named.

## Ask an assistant to rank towns for a family move

It will give a confident answer. Ask what it weighted and how.

An AI assistant asked to rank towns will usually produce a list with reasons, drawing on whatever rankings it has seen. Every such list rests on weights, and on this page four different towns come top from the same three census figures depending on the weights alone. A list that does not show its weights cannot be checked.

A learner who has run the 66 weightings asks the assistant to name its criteria, state their weights and say which towns would change places if the weights moved. The answer that comes back is less exciting and far more useful.

Rankings are easy to produce. Showing how much of a ranking is choice is what makes it honest. The longer argument is in [why coding repays the time it takes](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
