## If, then, else: the Ontario Grade 5 coding children in Ajax and Pickering meet

Public schools in Ajax and Pickering follow the Ontario curriculum. On 22 October 2026 we pulled the Grade 5 math text straight from the ministry&rsquo;s curriculum service. Words in quotation marks are the ministry&rsquo;s; beside each is the way our teachers bring it to life.

- **Grade 5, C3.1**: Children write and run code &ldquo;that involves conditional statements and other control structures&rdquo;. Our answer: A conditional is a fork in the road. We draw it as a fork first, with yes on one side and no on the other, before a single block is placed.
- **Grade 5, notes**: &ldquo;A conditional statement evaluates a Boolean condition, something that can be either true or false.&rdquo; Our answer: We play true-or-false out loud: the number is bigger than 10, the sprite is touching blue. Children learn that a condition is a question with only two answers.
- **Grade 5, notes**: &ldquo;Conditional statements, like loops, can be nested to allow for a range of possible outcomes or to implement decision trees.&rdquo; Our answer: Our playground project uses exactly this: three possible answers, so two questions asked in order. Children see why the order of the questions matters.
- **Grade 5, suggested situations**: The ministry lists &ldquo;determining the minimum and maximum values from a set of numbers&rdquo; and a guessing game whose code decides &ldquo;if guess was too high, too low, or correct&rdquo;. Our answer: The guessing game is a class favourite, and we build it in Scratch and then in Python. Finding the smallest and largest value comes up again in the Ajax data below.

Grade 5 is also where the ministry&rsquo;s notes start distinguishing a single equals sign, which stores a value, from a double one, which compares two values. Children who skip that distinction write programs that look right and quietly do the wrong thing, which is why we slow down here. By the end of the year most of our Grade 5 students can explain, in their own words, why age less than 20 and age less than or equal to 20 give different answers for a playground built in 2006. Our [guide to Ontario coding from Grade 1 to Grade 8](/ontario-coding-curriculum-grades-1-to-8) shows the years either side.

## How old are the playgrounds in Ajax? Sorting the Town&rsquo;s file with if and else

The Town of Ajax publishes its playgrounds as open data, a point file of play sites with an INSTALL year for each one. Pickering&rsquo;s open data hub, when we searched it on 22 October 2026, listed mostly Durham Region and conservation layers and no City of Pickering parks or playground file, so this project uses Ajax only. All figures in this section are counts we made ourselves from the Ajax download of that date.

**Ajax playgrounds by age in 2026, our calculation (2026 minus the INSTALL year) from the Town of Ajax Playgrounds file; 73 rows have TYPE Playground**

| Age band in 2026 | Playgrounds | What the code asks |
|---|---|---|
| Under 10 years (installed 2017 or later) | 9 | age &lt; 10 |
| 10 to 19 years | 33 | otherwise, age &lt; 20 |
| 20 years or more (installed 2006 or earlier) | 31 | everything left over |

The file has 81 rows: 73 playgrounds, 3 skate parks, 3 splash pads and 2 sets of exercise equipment. Install years run from 1999 to 2020, and 42 of the 73 playgrounds went in during the 2000s. The youngest is 6 years old in 2026 and the oldest 27. Written as Python, the Grade 5 decision tree looks like this, and children of 10 or 11 type it line by line in our class:

```
import csv

bands = {"under 10 years": 0, "10 to 19 years": 0, "20 years or more": 0}
ages = []
with open("ajax-playgrounds.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        if row["TYPE"] != "Playground":
            continue
        age = 2026 - int(row["INSTALL"])
        ages.append(age)
        if age < 10:
            bands["under 10 years"] += 1
        elif age < 20:
            bands["10 to 19 years"] += 1
        else:
            bands["20 years or more"] += 1

print(bands)
print("youngest", min(ages), "oldest", max(ages))
print("mean", round(sum(ages) / len(ages), 1))
ages.sort()
print("median", ages[len(ages) // 2])
```

### Skip what does not fit

The first if checks the TYPE column and uses continue to jump past skate parks, splash pads and exercise equipment. Only the 73 playgrounds reach the age questions.

### Ask in the right order

Is the age under 10? If not, is it under 20? If neither, it must be 20 or more. Swap the first two questions and every playground under 10 also passes the under-20 test first, so the counts go wrong.

### Mean and median

Our run printed a mean of 17.6 years and a median of 18. The two are close here, and children work out why: most install years cluster in one decade, with no extreme values pulling the mean.

### A date that is not what it seems

Age here means years since the INSTALL date in the file. It is not the age of every swing or slide: parts can be replaced without the file changing. Most rows were last edited in 2013 or 2014, and the Town&rsquo;s record of the dataset was modified on 9 May 2021. A good data scientist asks how old the data is before trusting what it says about age.

Some columns are never printed in our classes. Location names can name schools or people, and one column records a staff account, so we leave them out and teach why. The NAME column is safer and interesting: among the playgrounds it says Main 54 times, Senior 18 times and Junior once, which a class can turn into a pie chart. The most common install year is 2006, with 9 playgrounds. Data used under the Town of Ajax - Open Data Licence v.1.0, which allows commercial use. ## Why should an Ajax or Pickering child code when AI exists?

Because every AI system is, underneath, a pile of decisions someone had to understand.

In the playground project the hard part was not typing. It was deciding which question to ask first and noticing that an install year is not the same as the age of a slide. A tool can write the if-else; it cannot tell you whether the question was the right one. Children who have built decision trees by hand notice when an answer does not add up.

Adult chatbots stay out of our classes for children under 13. Those children learn what AI is through activities their teacher runs, such as sorting cards into rules and seeing where the rules fail. The rest of the lesson they reason for themselves, step by step, and test what they built before believing it.

Ontario puts if-then thinking into Grade 5 math, and that same thinking is what lets a person question an answer from a machine. The longer argument is in [should children still learn to code](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
