Modern Age Coders vs Oxford Learning

Oxford Learning and Modern Age Coders answer different questions a parent might be asking. Oxford Learning is a network of tutoring centres across Canada that works on how a child learns: it begins with an assessment, then builds a customised program that can cover math, English, French, science, reading, writing or study skills, mostly at small tables in a centre. Modern Age Coders teaches two subjects, mathematics and coding, live online, and goes as deep in them as the student wants to go. This page compares the two from Oxford Learning's own website as read on 22 October 2026, explains what Oxford Learning says about its cost, and says plainly when a centre that covers every subject is the more useful choice.

Two different promises

Oxford Learning's promise is broad: better thinking and learning skills that help in every class. Its assessment looks at concentration, memory and reasoning as well as subject knowledge, and the program that follows can touch several subjects.

Ours is narrow on purpose. We promise nothing about grades, but we do commit to teaching a student mathematics, or coding, properly: why each method works, where it comes from, and how to use it on a problem the student has not seen before.

Why that is not a contest

A child who is behind in reading, disorganised with homework and anxious about tests across the board needs something different from a child who is fine everywhere except quadratics. The first need fits a learning-skills centre; the second fits a subject specialist.

Many families need one in one year and the other later. The sections below are meant to help you tell which need you have now.

How it works

An Oxford Learning program, step by step, as it describes it

Oxford Learning sets out its process in some detail. Read in order, its pages describe four stages.

  1. The Dynamic Assessment. Oxford Learning calls it a proprietary combination of licensed testing activities, taken in an assessment room away from the classroom. It measures academic and cognitive skills to understand "not only where a student stands academically, but also why a student is at that level".
  2. A customised program. Education coordinators use the results to choose a starting point, areas of focus and teaching methods. Oxford Learning says each student's program is individualised.
  3. Classes at a small table. Regular programs seat three students with one teacher, mixing one-on-one teaching with independent work. Centres generally run after school from Monday to Thursday and on Saturday mornings.
  4. Lesson Review meetings. Parents and centre staff set goals together and check them at the next meeting, from sight words to quadratic equations to remembering homework.

Virtual Table, the online option

For families who cannot reach a centre, Oxford Learning runs Virtual Table. It describes the sessions as live, with screen sharing, and says "students work from workbook materials that meet their specific learning needs".

Parents receive the link on the day: Oxford Learning says "a private link and class password is emailed to parents the day of the class". New students are placed with an online placement guide.

The breadth of the menu

Oxford Learning's menu runs from Little Readers for preschoolers to a University and College Success Program, with French, SAT, ACT and SSAT preparation along the way. Its math page offers programs "for every age and grade level, from Grade 1 to Grade 12".

That breadth is the point of a learning-skills centre: one place can follow a child across subjects and years.

A worked example

Finding the real misconception behind a wrong answer in Grade 9 or 10 algebra

This example is ours, written for our classes, and is not taken from Oxford Learning or any curriculum document. A student writes (x + 3)² = x² + 9. Marking it wrong is easy. Finding out why the student believes it, and making sure the belief is gone, is the teaching.

  1. Ask for the reasoning. The student usually explains that the square applies to each part separately, the way 2(x + 3) becomes 2x + 6. That is the real misconception: treating squaring like multiplying by a number.
  2. Test the rule with a number. Try x = 1. The left side is (1 + 3)² = 16. The student's rule gives 1 + 9 = 10. One number is enough to show the rule is false.
  3. Notice what zero hides. With x = 0 both sides give 9. A student who checks only with zero would wrongly decide the rule works, so we choose test numbers on purpose.
  4. Build the right rule from a picture. A square with side x + 3 splits into four pieces: an x by x square, two x by 3 rectangles and a 3 by 3 square. Its area is x² + 3x + 3x + 9, which is x² + 6x + 9. The missing 6x is the two rectangles the student forgot.
  5. Check the new rule the same way. At x = 1: 1 + 6 + 9 = 16, which matches. At x = 5: 25 + 30 + 9 = 64, and (5 + 3)² = 64.
  6. Ask three questions that separate a slip from a belief. Expand (x + 5)², (2x + 1)² and (x - 4)². A student who has truly changed their idea gets the middle term each time: 10x, 4x and -8x.

What the output shows

The expression and the expanded form agree on every row. The student's rule agrees only at x = 0, which is exactly why zero is a poor test number.

The row for x = -3 is a bonus: the expression is 0 there, but the student's rule gives 18, a gap no one can miss.

Why this is subject depth

A general study skill, such as checking your work, helps here. But knowing which numbers to test, and having the area picture ready, comes from teaching the subject itself.

The same idea returns later, in factoring trinomials in Grade 10 and in the binomial expansions of senior math, so getting it right now saves work in every later grade.

Deciding

Four steps to decide

A short order that works whichever way you lean.

  1. Name the need. Write down the one or two things you most want to change this year. If they span subjects or study habits, start with a learning-skills centre. If they are about math or coding, start with a subject course.
  2. Ask Oxford Learning the cost questions above, and ask about the assessment and the cancellation policy.
  3. Book our free first class. The teacher works through a few problems with your child and tells you what they found.
  4. Compare what your child can explain afterwards. The option that left your child able to explain more, in the area you most care about, is usually the one to choose.

Oxford Learning and Modern Age Coders: what parents ask

Is Modern Age Coders part of Oxford Learning?

No. Modern Age Coders is independent and has no relationship with Oxford Learning. What this page says about Oxford Learning is taken from its own website, read on 22 October 2026.

How much does Oxford Learning cost?

Oxford Learning does not publish a figure. Its pages say every location charges a competitive hourly rate that varies by city and province, and that a program price depends on the program and how often the student attends. Ask your nearest centre for the full monthly total.

Does Oxford Learning offer payment plans?

Its pricing page says monthly payment plans are available through iFinance, and adds that they may not be available at all locations. Oxford Learning also states a 30-day cancellation policy.

How many hours does Oxford Learning recommend?

Its FAQ recommends about 100 hours of instruction for maximum cognitive gain, notes that two 1-hour classes a week come to about 8 hours a month, and says it generally takes about a year to reach 100 hours.

How many students are in an Oxford Learning class?

Oxford Learning says its regular programs run at small tables with three students to one teacher, and that its small group programs and workshops usually have no more than 8 to 10 students. Our batches have 15 to 20 students, or we teach one to one.

Does Oxford Learning tutor online?

Yes. Its Virtual Table program connects students with a tutor live over screen sharing, using workbook materials, and Oxford Learning says the sessions are not pre-recorded. All of our classes are live online.

Does Oxford Learning teach coding?

We found no coding or programming program on the Oxford Learning pages we read in October 2026. Modern Age Coders teaches coding from age 6, alongside mathematics.

Is there an Oxford Learning alternative for math only?

Modern Age Coders teaches mathematics live online from Grade 1 to university level, in a group or one to one, with the reasons behind each method taught first. We do not cover reading, writing, French or general homework help.

What does the Oxford Learning assessment involve?

Oxford Learning describes its Dynamic Assessment as a set of licensed testing activities in a separate room, taking up to two hours for older students, that looks at cognitive skills as well as school subjects. Our first class is a free lesson that also places the student.

When are your live classes for Canadian students?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. We fix the time by your province's clock.
