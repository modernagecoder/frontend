## Dhofar is ten wilayats, not one survey location

Oman's [Governorates System](https://bur.gov.om/assets/pdf/en/governorate-system.pdf) lists ten Dhofar wilayats: Salalah, Taqah, Mirbat, Rakhyut, Thumrait, Dhalkut, Al Mazyunah, Muqshin, Shalim and the Hallaniyat Islands, and Sadah. It identifies Salalah as the governorate centre.

That administrative structure matters when a dashboard claims to describe Dhofar. A sample from the centre may describe a defined Salalah population. It cannot automatically represent people in the other nine wilayats.

The current [Dhofar page on the unified government portal](https://gov.om/en/dhofar-governorate) lists wali leadership for the wilayats separately. It publishes a Sunday-through-Thursday business week for the governorate office. Those hours belong to government administration, not Modern Age Coders.

Oman's [Foreign Ministry regional guide](https://www.fm.gov.om/en/about-oman/state/oman-by-region/) places Dhofar in the far south. It describes borders with Al Wusta, the Arabian Sea, Yemen and Saudi Arabia. It gives an approximate regional area of 99,300 square kilometres and names mountains, plains, coastal settlements and the Empty Quarter among the region's physical settings.

An approximate governorate area is not a city footprint. A coastal household, an inland settlement and a mountain community may have different access conditions and schedules. A regional survey needs a sampling design that makes those differences visible.

The same tourism guide names beaches, deserts, fertile plains, green mountains and archaeological monuments. Those features are not survey measurements, but they demonstrate why the governorate should not be modelled as one uniform setting.

The source also describes military, administrative, residential, storage and reception spaces. If a student models heritage uses, each value needs a time field. A building can remain in one location while its purpose changes.

These are public context facts, not evidence of household connectivity or coding demand. They establish that the target geography contains different settlement patterns. The student's survey must collect the evidence for its own question.

## The Dhofar sampling-bias coding project

The learner opens a synthetic dashboard with a confident headline: 80 percent of Dhofar learners report reliable study connectivity. The arithmetic is correct because 72 positive answers divided by 90 responses equals 80 percent.

The learner then groups the source rows by wilayat. All 90 records say Salalah. No resident of Taqah, Mirbat, Rakhyut, Thumrait or another Dhofar wilayat had any chance to enter the sample.

This is sampling bias. The failure concerns the frame from which people could be selected. It exists even if every selected person answers. Non-response is a separate risk and is held constant in this exercise so the geographic coverage problem remains clear.

| Teaching stratum | Selected responses | Synthetic positive responses | Displayed rate | What may be concluded |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Salalah | 90 | 72 | 80.0% | Result for this synthetic Salalah sample only |
| Taqah | 30 | 18 | 60.0% | Separate teaching stratum |
| Mirbat | 25 | 16 | 64.0% | Separate teaching stratum |
| Thumrait | 35 | 21 | 60.0% | Separate teaching stratum |
| Rakhyut | 20 | 8 | 40.0% | Separate teaching stratum |
| Five synthetic strata combined | 200 | 135 | 67.5% | Still not a population estimate without a defensible design |

The second table is not presented as a real Dhofar estimate. It demonstrates that the answer can move when previously excluded strata appear. Combining the rows does not automatically create a representative result. Selection probabilities and population evidence still matter.

A bigger Salalah sample does not solve the problem. Raising its size from 90 to 9,000 could reduce random sampling error for the selected Salalah frame. It would not give a resident elsewhere a chance of selection.

The repair begins before data collection. The learner writes the target population, names required geographic strata, identifies a defensible frame and documents the selection method. Each record stores wilayat, eligibility, selection probability, response status and collection time.

| Required check | Question answered | Failure prevented |
|---|---|---|
| Target population | Who does the claim intend to describe? | Scope expands after analysis |
| Sampling frame | Who could be selected? | Excluded places stay invisible |
| Geographic stratum | Which wilayat supplied the record? | Capital is mistaken for region |
| Selection method | How was the person chosen? | Convenience is called random |
| Eligibility | Who qualified for the question? | Wrong records enter denominator |
| Response status | Did the selected person answer? | Sampling and response failures blur |
| Weight evidence | What controls support adjustment? | Guessed weights imply accuracy |
| Limits | Where can the result transfer? | One rate looks universal |

Weights are not decorative multipliers. They depend on relevant, dated population controls and the actual survey design. If those inputs do not exist, the dashboard reports unweighted sample descriptions and states the limitation.

Every percentage appears beside its numerator and denominator. Small strata receive wider uncertainty and more cautious language. A quality report may say that the observed synthetic sample has a certain rate while refusing to describe unobserved people.

The interface also exposes collection dates. Khareef, school terms, university assessments, work patterns and other timing differences may change who is reachable. A one-week convenience sample should not silently become an annual regional claim.

All responses, rates and counts in this project are invented. They do not measure actual internet access, education provision, household opinion or demand in Salalah or any other Dhofar wilayat.
