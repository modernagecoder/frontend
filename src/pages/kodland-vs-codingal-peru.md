## Why do Peruvian parents compare Kodland and Codingal?

Because both appear when a parent searches for online coding classes for children, and on the surface they look alike: live classes, a free trial, projects, certificates. Underneath, the language and the shape of the class differ.

### The language question

One site is written in Spanish and the other in English. For many Peruvian families that single fact decides more than any feature list.

### The format question

Small group, one to one, or both. Each format changes how much a child talks, how fast they move and what a month costs a family.

### The fit question

Ages, subjects and equipment. A school can be right for a 9-year-old and wrong for a 15-year-old, or the other way round.

## What should a Peruvian family ask that neither page answers?

Mostly practical things: the hour in Peru time, the teaching language when a child is stuck, the equipment at home, and how to stop. Keeping notes on every school in the same shape makes the gaps obvious, and that is the idea behind this short Python lesson.

### Notes as dictionaries

Write each school's facts as a Python dictionary: one key per question, one value per answer. A second dictionary, blank, holds every question you care about, each set to "not stated". Merging the two should give a full row with the gaps still marked.

Python 3.9 and later merge dictionaries with the | operator. When both sides have the same key, **the right-hand side wins**. That one rule decides whether your notes come out right.

### The bug, and the fix

In the program, wrong = school_a | blank puts the blanks on the right, so every real answer is overwritten by "not stated". The output shows it: wrong: not stated | not stated.

Swapping the order, blank | school_a, keeps the facts and fills only the missing keys. A list comprehension then names the gaps, which become your questions for the trial class. School A and School B here are invented, not Kodland or Codingal.

**Merging notes on two schools** (Python)
```python
blank = {'language': 'not stated', 'ages': 'not stated',
         'format': 'not stated', 'peru page': 'not stated'}

school_a = {'language': 'Spanish', 'format': 'small group or 1-1'}
school_b = {'language': 'English', 'ages': '6 to 18', 'format': '1:1'}

wrong = school_a | blank      # the right-hand side wins every clash
right = blank | school_a

print('wrong:', wrong['language'], '|', wrong['format'])
print('right:', right['language'], '|', right['format'])

for name, facts in [('A', school_a), ('B', school_b)]:
    row = blank | facts
    gaps = [k for k in blank if k not in facts]
    print(name, row['language'], row['ages'], 'gaps:', gaps)
```

**What it prints** (Output)
```output
wrong: not stated | not stated
right: Spanish | small group or 1-1
A Spanish not stated gaps: ['ages', 'peru page']
B English 6 to 18 gaps: ['peru page']
```
Copied unchanged from running the script under Python 3.14; both schools and every fact about them were made up for this lesson.

1. **Predict.** Before running it, say which line prints "not stated" twice, and why.
2. **Add a key.** Put 'hours': 'not stated' in blank and watch both schools gain a gap.
3. **Count.** Print how many questions each school has answered, using len(facts).
4. **Explain.** In one English sentence, say what "the right-hand side wins" means for a | b.

This is the kind of lesson our teen students meet early: a small, real problem, one surprising result, and a rule they will reuse for years. Merging settings, configuration files and API responses all follow the same right-hand-wins rule.

## How should a family in Peru choose between online coding schools?

With one checklist for every school, Kodland, Codingal, us or anyone else, filled in after a trial class rather than from the homepage.

1. **Language.** Which language is every lesson in, and what happens when your child does not understand?
2. **Format.** Group or one to one, how many children per teacher, and can you switch later?
3. **Hours on your clock.** Ask for the class time in Peru time, written down.
4. **Ages.** Ask which age range applies to the specific course, not the school as a whole.
5. **Equipment.** A computer, a camera, a headset; check what the course truly needs. In INEI's survey for April to June 2026, only 36.4% of homes across Peru owned even one computer.
6. **Thinking, not copying.** In the trial, does the teacher ask your child to predict and explain, or mainly to copy?
7. **Progress you can see.** How will you know, after a month, what your child can now do alone?
8. **Stopping.** How do you pause or stop, and what notice is needed?

Online habits differ by age. INEI's figures for April to June 2026 put internet use at 92.5% among teenagers of 12 to 18, but 68.2% among children of 6 to 11. A teenager can usually manage a live class alone; a younger child often needs an adult nearby for the first few weeks, whichever school you choose.

## What should you see in the first three months at any school?

A simple yardstick that works for Kodland, Codingal, us or anyone else. These are our suggestions, not any school's promise.

**Signs of real progress, month by month (our suggestions)**

| When | Your child can | Ask the teacher | A warning sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | Show you the trial project and name one part | What did my child find hardest? | Nothing to show |
| Week 4 | Change a program without step-by-step help | What should we practise at home? | Every project identical |
| Week 8 | Explain a bug they fixed | Is the level still right? | Your child has stopped talking about it |
| Week 12 | Start a small project of their own | What comes next, and why? | Only copying from the screen |

### For parents

Ask for the same three things at every school: something to see, something explained, and a next step.
If the language is the problem rather than the coding, say so early; a change of format often fixes it.

### For teens

Keep every project in one folder with a sentence about what it does. It becomes your record.
If a class feels too slow, ask to move level. Good schools expect that question.
