# Coding Classes in Texas

## The rule most Texas families are never told

Under **19 TAC 74.12**, the Foundation High School Program's two Languages Other Than English credits may be selected from:

> "(i) any two levels in the same language, including comparable AP or IB language courses that do not count toward another credit required for graduation; or (ii) two credits in computer programming languages, including computer coding, to be selected from Computer Science I, II, and III, AP Computer Science Principles, AP Computer Science A, IB Computer Science Standard Level, and IB Computer Science Higher Level."

Clause (ii) is an alternative of equal standing, not a hardship exemption. A student choosing it needs no reason and no permission.

**A separate provision is routinely confused with it.** That one covers a student who has already begun a language and is unlikely to complete the second credit. It requires agreement from the LOTE teacher or another designated LOTE teacher, the principal or designee, and the parent, or from the student's ARD committee, or from their Section 504 committee. Families told "you need sign-off" have usually been quoted this second provision when they qualify under the first.

**Check the current text.** The version quoted is published as effective until 1 August 2025, and TEA issued an August 2025 update to the Chapter 74 Subchapter B graduation rules. Ask your school counsellor for the text in force for your child's cohort.

## The Texas student data project

Each market gets a project on local data containing a specific way of being wrong. Texas gets observation bias.

Texas records more tornadoes than any other state and the NOAA Storm Prediction Center database runs back to 1950. A student plots counts per year, sees the line climbing, and draws the obvious conclusion. It is wrong.

The count measures tornadoes **that somebody noticed and reported**. Three things changed and none was the weather: population spread into places a tornado would previously have touched down unseen; Doppler radar was deployed nationally through the 1990s; and in 2007 the Fujita scale was replaced by the Enhanced Fujita scale, producing a large jump in reports of the weakest tornadoes.

**The trap.** NOAA's own assessment is that removing the F and EF0 category from the database nearly eliminates the annual frequency trend. The apparent rise sits almost entirely in the tornadoes hardest to spot and easiest to miss before radar. Strong tornadoes, which nobody in 1955 could have failed to notice, show no comparable climb.

**The generalisable method.** When you suspect a trend is an artefact of collection, find a subset where the collection method did not change. If the trend vanishes there, the trend was about the observer. If it survives, it is real.

Students pull current records themselves rather than using numbers printed on a web page.
