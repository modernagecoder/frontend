## Most students learn AI from tutorials. In Sohar it is running down the road.

The applied end of machine learning is unglamorous and enormous. Predicting when a bearing will fail before it fails. Deciding which container moves next. Spotting the defect a tired human eye misses on the fourteenth hour of a shift. Forecasting a load so that a plant does not run a furnace hotter than it needs to. None of that is speculative technology. It is ordinary industrial software, and it is what a very large share of working machine learning engineers actually do.

A student in a port and industrial city has an advantage when they meet those problems, and it is not access. It is intuition. They already know that a crane has a queue, that a shift has a handover, that a delay propagates. Those are the mental models the mathematics is describing.

**What we do not claim:** we have no arrangement with any company in Sohar, we place no students into any of them, and nothing here should be read as a route into a particular employer. The industrial context is a teaching resource and nothing more.

What it does change is the project a student picks. A learner who has watched lorries queue is far more likely to build something that models a queue than one who has only seen a tutorial about handwritten digits. Projects chosen from a student's own surroundings get finished, because the student can tell when the answer is wrong.

## Four projects a teenager here can actually reason about

None of these needs a company's data. Every one can be built from figures a student generates, simulates or collects themselves, which is deliberate: waiting for permission to use somebody's real dataset is how a student project dies.

1. **Predict when something breaks before it breaks.** Simulate machines with accumulating wear, log the readings, train a model to flag the ones about to fail. Then the interesting part: what does a false alarm cost, and what does a missed failure cost? Those numbers are not equal, and the moment a learner internalises that they stop optimising accuracy and start optimising consequence.
2. **Decide what moves next.** Given a queue of items with different sizes, deadlines and priorities, in what order should they be handled? A fourteen year old can write a rule that beats first come first served within an afternoon, and can also discover that their rule collapses when one item is enormous. That is the lesson.
3. **Find the defect in the picture.** Image classification on photographs the student takes themselves rather than a tidy academic set. Bad light, odd angles and unbalanced classes each teach more than a clean dataset would.
4. **Forecast a load, then argue with your own forecast.** A time series, a baseline that simply repeats yesterday, and a model that has to beat it. Most beginner forecasts do not beat yesterday, and finding that out is the most useful hour in the course.

**Why we do not start with robotics here.** Not because it is bad. Because in a city whose economy runs on process and logistics, the more useful early intuition is about systems that decide rather than machines that move. A robot that follows a line teaches control. A model that predicts a failure teaches uncertainty, and uncertainty is the harder and more transferable idea.

## The five rungs

| Rung | Roughly | What the student actually does |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Python by hand | Age 9 to 12 | Real syntax rather than blocks: loops, functions, files, errors read and fixed |
| 2. Data | Age 11 to 14 | A messy file loaded, cleaned, plotted, and the poisonous row found |
| 3. Machine learning | Age 12 to 16 | A model trained, its error stated in a real unit, compared against a baseline |
| 4. Agents | Age 13 to 18 | A program given a goal that picks its steps, calls tools, and fails loudly |
| 5. GitHub and the write-up | Any | The code published with an honest account of what it does and where it breaks |

The agent layer is where this goes next: once a student can train and measure a model, the question is what should happen when the model says something. ## How the classes run

Oman holds Gulf Standard Time all year and India, where the teaching operation sits, holds Indian Standard Time all year. The difference is a permanent **thirty minutes**, so a slot agreed once never has to be renegotiated because a season changed.

| Slot, Oman time | Who it is for |
|---|---|
| 16:30 to 18:00 | Primary age children, straight after school |
| 18:30 to 20:00 | The main teenage block, after dinner and school work |
| 20:30 to 22:00 | University students and working adults |

Ninety minutes per session, twice a week, screens shared both ways, five to eight students or one to one, in English. Work is committed to GitHub during the term rather than handed in at the end of it.
