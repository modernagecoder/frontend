The Muscat load factor model, or what happened at the airport when the flights went away

Every market we teach in gets a first project built on its own data, because an argument you can have with your own family is an argument you remember. In Muscat that project is the airport. Muscat International sits in the Wilayat of Al Seeb, the same wilayat the Royal Decree chose for the AI zone, and in 2025 it handled 13,157,559 of Oman's 14,939,209 air passengers, which is 88.1 per cent of the national total. The finding the model has to surface

At Muscat International, international flights fell 4.5 per cent, from 86,797 to 82,913, while international passengers rose 1.3 per cent, from 11,681,790 to 11,838,223. Divide one by the other and passengers per flight moved from 134.59 to 142.78. The headline says growth. The ratio says consolidation: fewer aircraft, fuller aircraft. A student who reads only the passenger column reports the wrong story, and reports it confidently.

The same derived column across the 2025 frame ranks the country in a way no press release does: Salalah domestic 164.37, Muscat international 142.78, Salalah international 138.88, Muscat domestic 137.34, Sohar domestic 121.65, Duqm domestic 98.53, and Sohar international 3.55.

That last figure is the teaching moment. Sohar international carried 390 passengers on 110 flights in 2025, down from 44,897 on 495 flights. At 3.55 passengers per flight that is not a commercial schedule, and a regression fitted with the row still in it is dragged by something that does not describe scheduled air travel at all. Ten seconds of plotting exposes it. No amount of staring at the table does.

The same article prints a first quarter total of 2,857,817 against 2,929,495 a year earlier, a fall of 2.4 per cent. The three monthly figures as printed add to 2,858,447, which is 630 more than the printed quarterly figure. We do not tell students where that gap comes from, because we do not know. We tell them what the page prints, and we make them write the discrepancy down instead of quietly choosing the number they prefer.

Three rows cannot carry a regression and the class is told so plainly. What three rows can carry is the first habit of anyone who works with data for a living: add the parts, compare them with the whole, and report the difference.

  1. Prove the dataset before trusting it

    The student types in the fourteen rows, sums the seven 2025 rows and confirms 14,939,209 passengers and 104,510 flights against the published national totals. Then the harder half: three 2024 cells are not printed anywhere, and they are recovered from the printed percentage changes and the printed total. Sohar domestic passengers of 18,247 in 2025 are described as a fall of 21.8 per cent, which gives 23,331 for 2024, and that is exactly the residual when the six printed 2024 figures are subtracted from 14,537,674. Nothing is modelled until the recovery closes to the digit.

  2. Plot before fitting

    A scatter of flights against passengers, coloured by airport, with segment as the marker shape. Six points suggest a line. Sohar international lands on its own, nowhere near it. The student sees the outlier before any model has been asked to average it away, which is the whole reason the plot comes first.

  3. Add the derived column and make a decision

    Passengers per flight is one line of pandas and it changes the story. Muscat international moves 134.59 to 142.78 across the two years. The student then decides, in writing, whether Sohar international belongs in the training set at all, and defends the decision either way. Both answers are acceptable. Not deciding is not.

  4. Split honestly, and find out why that is hard here

    Rows are held back before anything is fitted. Fourteen rows makes this uncomfortable, and that discomfort is the lesson: a single random split of eighty and twenty would be meaningless at this size, so leave one out cross validation is the honest choice. Understanding why is worth more than the model that follows.

  5. Fit the regression and read the coefficient out loud

    A linear regression of passengers on flights, with airport and segment one hot encoded. The flights coefficient is then stated in real units, as extra passengers per extra flight. Then the hard question, which most courses never ask: can this model reproduce what actually happened at Muscat, where flights fell and passengers rose?

  6. Measure the error in passengers, not in R squared

    Mean absolute error reported in passengers is a number a parent who flies out of Muscat can judge. The student reports it in passengers and as a percentage of the row it belongs to, then refits without the Sohar international row to see how much of the error was one bad row rather than a bad model.

  7. Write the limitations paragraph and publish it

    Fourteen annual rows. One airport carrying 88.1 per cent of the traffic. A 2026 shock the model was never shown. A 630 passenger discrepancy in the published source. All of it goes in the write up, published at a link, with arithmetic anyone in Muscat can re-run. That link is worth more in a university application than a certificate, because it can be opened and checked.

One more question sits at the end of the project, and it is deliberately left open. In May 2026 Indian nationals were the largest passenger group at Oman's airports, with 175,022 movements made up of 76,515 arrivals and 98,507 departures, a net outflow of 21,992 in a single month. Omani nationals were second at 80,732 and Pakistani nationals third at 45,956. Does that departure surplus line up with Muscat's Indian schools closing for summer on 31 May, with Eid travel, with labour contract cycles, or with none of them? Published reporting attributes the 2026 decline to regional tension in Middle East aviation, and says nothing about schools. The student has to argue it from the data they can get, and then state plainly what the data cannot settle. That sentence, the one admitting the limit, is the hardest sentence in the course to write.

The distinction

Using AI and building AI are two different educations

We teach both, in that order, and we tell parents which of the two holds its value. Prompting is a product skill, and products are rewritten every year. Modelling is a mathematical skill, and the mathematics has not changed since anyone reading this was at school.

Using AI tools

  • Type a prompt, read the reply, reword it and try again
  • Learn the menus of whichever product is fashionable this year
  • Success means an answer that looks plausible on screen
  • Nothing transfers when the tool is retired or the price changes
  • Genuinely worth a few weeks of anyone's time, and no more
  • We teach it properly, including how to catch a confident wrong answer

Building AI

  • Find the data, prove it adds up, and defend every choice made about it
  • Pick a model, and know what it is assuming on your behalf
  • Train it, then state the error in units a non specialist can check
  • Work out whether the failure is the data, the features or the model
  • Put it in front of real inputs and watch it degrade
  • Rests on statistics, linear algebra and code, none of which expire

The distinction is sharper in Muscat than in most cities, because the governorate is building AI capacity rather than only buying it. Read what follows and you will find a decree zoning land, seven data centres, a research centre, an open data platform and a national language model. Not one of those is staffed by a generation trained only to prompt.

Why now, in Muscat

A Royal Decree put an AI zone in Al Seeb, and the rest of the city is already wired for it

Royal Decree No. 50/2026, issued on 30 April 2026 by His Majesty Sultan Haitham bin Tarik, establishes an Artificial Intelligence Special Zone in the Governorate of Muscat, in the Wilayat of Al Seeb, on approximately 104,000 square metres. The decree instructs the Board of Directors of the Public Authority for Special Economic Zones and Free Zones to appoint the party that will manage, operate and develop the zone, in coordination with the Ministry of Transport, Communications and Information Technology. Target industries reported are semiconductors, robotics, integrated AI systems and software development, with special economic zone incentives, exemptions and a lighter regulatory setup for the companies inside it. The zone sits inside the Eleventh Five Year Development Plan for 2026 to 2030 and Oman Vision 2040. On the day of the decree, The National reported combined chip sector investment of RO 14.63 million, USD 38 million, involving GSME, Lumotive and Movandi.

Above the zone sits the national frame. On 22 September 2024 His Excellency Said bin Hamoud al Maawali, Minister of Transport, Communications and Information Technology, announced the National Programme for Artificial Intelligence and Advanced Digital Technologies, running to the end of 2026 as an upgrade to the 2022 Executive Programme. Its three pillars are adopting AI across economic and developmental sectors, localising AI technologies, and governing AI applications with a human centred vision. The initiatives listed under it are a National Open Data Platform, a National Centre for AI Research and Development, an AI Studio, an Omani national language model, a Centre for the Fourth Industrial Revolution with the Ministry of Economy and the World Economic Forum, and quantum computing work. The stated targets are to raise the digital economy from 2 per cent of GDP in 2021 to 10 per cent by 2040, and to enter the top 50 of the Oxford Insights Government AI Readiness Index.

The skills pipeline underneath it is measured rather than promised. At the five year Makeen forum held on 27 April 2026, the ministry reported more than 11,000 trainees since the programme launched in September 2022, 139 programmes across more than 30 technology fields, over 27,000 registrations and more than 2,425 micro credentials awarded. It reported a 50 per cent employment rate in the technology bootcamp track, 2,790 Omanis supported into jobs, 4,949 Omanis entering sector professions, IT sector Omanisation raised to 45.5 per cent, and 150 or more Omani semiconductor engineers with five start-ups behind them. The next phase targets 10,000 jobseekers over five years, with new specialised programmes in AI agents and quantum computing.

Read that as a parent in Bousher or Darsait and one pattern stands out. Every item is a building project. An open data platform needs people who can work with data. A national language model needs people who understand tokenisation, training and evaluation. A semiconductor start-up needs engineers, and the ministry has counted them. None of it is a shortage of AI users. Muscat has those already, in every office and every classroom.

A child who is twelve today is twenty two in 2036, four years short of the Vision 2040 horizon, and applying for exactly these roles against a national policy that pushes employers to hire and train nationals. That is the whole reason this page exists, and the reason we will not sell a prompt engineering course as an AI education.

How the classes run

Timed to a Sunday to Thursday week, which is the part most providers get wrong

Muscat runs on Gulf Standard Time, UTC+4, with no daylight saving at any point in the year. Against Indian Standard Time that is exactly one hour and thirty minutes behind, so 18:00 in Muscat is 19:30 in India. The clock is the easy half. The week is the half that catches people out: the Omani working and school week runs Sunday to Thursday and the weekend is Friday and Saturday, which is the opposite shape to an Indian family's. A Muscat student's free evenings are Sunday through Thursday, and their free mornings are Friday and Saturday.

What a session actually is

Ninety minutes of live video with a teacher who knows your child's name and remembers what they got stuck on last week. Screens are shared in both directions, so the teacher watches the code being written and interrupts at the moment the mistake happens rather than marking it three days later. Batches are five to eight students, or one to one if you prefer.

Between sessions there is a task rather than a worksheet: something to build, break and bring back. Questions during the week go to the same teacher, not to a support queue. Timings shift around Ramadan and the Eid holidays rather than running through them.

The Muscat school year, and when to start

Muscat's Indian schools run an April to March academic year rather than an August to June one. Indian School Darsait's published Calendar of Events for 2026 to 2027 has the new year opening on 6 April 2026, summer vacation beginning 31 May, and reopening on 12 July for Classes IX to XII and 20 July for the younger classes under a Board circular issued on 31 December 2025. Renaissance Day falls on 23 July 2026. Half yearly examinations begin 20 and 21 September, the second term opens 6 October, and National Day holidays fall on 25 and 26 November 2026. Winter break runs 20 to 31 December, school reopens 3 January 2027 with Pre Board II beginning the same day, annual examinations run through February, the last working day is 4 March 2027 and the next academic year begins 4 April 2027. Several examination windows are printed as tentative in the calendar itself.

Two moments in that year are the natural ones to start something new: early April and the middle of July. Two are best avoided: the June travel exodus and the annual examinations in late February. Outside those, admission is rolling and a student can join in any week.
