# Robotics vs coding for kids in Peru

In Peru, "robotics" is often the first word a parent reaches for when they want a child to learn technology. It is a good instinct: robots are exciting, physical and easy to show off. But every robot that does something clever is running a program, and that program is where most of the thinking lives. This page explains the difference between robotics and coding, what real robotics competitions expect of children, how to choose for your child, and what we teach.

## What is the difference between robotics and coding for kids?

Robotics is the whole machine: a body, motors, sensors and a program. Coding is the program. A robot without code is a model; code without a robot can still run games, apps, websites and AI.

### Sense

A robot reads the world through sensors: light, distance, touch, a camera. In code, each reading is just a number arriving many times a second.

### Decide

The program compares those numbers with rules: if the line is to the left, steer left; if something is close, stop. This is the part that takes real thinking.

### Act

Motors turn, wheels move, an arm lifts. The action is only as good as the decision before it, which is why the decision is where children learn most.

### What robotics adds

Building teaches mechanical sense: gears, balance, why a robot tips over on a slope. Children see their code act on the real world, which is motivating, and team builds teach cooperation.

It also brings costs and limits: parts to buy, a kit that must be at home or at a centre, and lessons where much of the time goes on assembly rather than on the program.

### What coding adds

Coding goes deeper into the decide step and much further beyond it. The same if, loop and variable that drive a robot also build games, apps and data projects, and lead to Python, Java and AI.

It needs only a computer. INEI found a computer in 36.4% of Peruvian households in the second quarter of 2026, so a family that has one already has everything a coding class needs.

### Notice the word autonomous

In RoboMission, the WRO explains, "the field is set up randomly each round", so "the robot needs to be able to make its own decisions during the run". Nobody steers it with a remote. Every decision is a line of code written before the run.

Future Engineers goes further: a car-like robot must drive itself round a track. That is a problem in control, the subject of the lesson further down this page.

### What that means for a parent

A child who reaches the senior categories will spend far more hours on the program than on the build. In our view, the teams whose robots cope with surprises are usually the teams with strong coders.

Learning to code first is therefore not a detour from robotics. It is the part of robotics that takes longest to learn and transfers to everything else.

## How does a robot follow a line? A lesson in code, no robot needed

Here is a lesson we teach to children of about 10 to 12, simulated in Python. A small robot starts 10 millimetres to the right of a black line and must steer back onto it. The numbers and the simple model are ours; real robots add speed and friction, but the idea is the same.

### The obvious idea

Most children first write: if the robot is right of the line, turn left by a fixed amount; if it is left, turn right. In our model a fixed turn moves the robot 3 millimetres sideways each step.

Before running it, the mentor asks: "Where will the robot be after eight steps?" Children usually say "on the line". The program disagrees.

### What actually happens

The robot reaches 1 millimetre right of the line, turns 3, and lands 2 millimetres left. Then it turns 3 back and lands 1 right. It never settles: it wobbles across the line for ever.

Real line-followers built this way do exactly that, zig-zagging along the tape. The child has found the problem with their own eyes before anyone names it.

**Version 1: always turn by the same amount** (Python)
```python
def fixed_turn(offset, turn=3.0, steps=8):
    path = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        offset = offset - turn if offset > 0 else offset + turn
        path.append(round(offset, 1))
    return path


print("fixed turn of 3:", fixed_turn(10.0))
```

**What Version 1 prints** (Output)
```output
fixed turn of 3: [7.0, 4.0, 1.0, -2.0, 1.0, -2.0, 1.0, -2.0]
```
Exact output of our run with Python 3. Distances from the line in millimetres, one per step; negative means left of the line.

The better idea: **steer by how far off you are**. Far from the line, turn hard; close to it, turn gently. In code the correction is the error times a number called the gain. Engineers call this proportional control, and it is the first idea behind self-driving robots like those in the Future Engineers category.

**Version 2: steer in proportion to the error** (Python)
```python
def proportional(offset, gain, steps=8):
    path = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        offset = offset - gain * offset
        path.append(round(offset, 1) + 0.0)
    return path


for gain in (0.3, 0.6, 1.6, 2.2):
    print(f"gain {gain}:", proportional(10.0, gain))
```

**What Version 2 prints** (Output)
```output
gain 0.3: [7.0, 4.9, 3.4, 2.4, 1.7, 1.2, 0.8, 0.6]
gain 0.6: [4.0, 1.6, 0.6, 0.3, 0.1, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0]
gain 1.6: [-6.0, 3.6, -2.2, 1.3, -0.8, 0.5, -0.3, 0.2]
gain 2.2: [-12.0, 14.4, -17.3, 20.7, -24.9, 29.9, -35.8, 43.0]
```
Exact output of our run. A gain of 0.3 creeps back slowly; 0.6 settles within six steps; 1.6 overshoots and swings before settling; 2.2 swings wider every step and runs away.

Children then explain the four rows in their own words, in English. Too gentle is slow but safe. A bit stronger is ideal. Too strong overshoots. Much too strong is worse than doing nothing, because each correction is bigger than the error it fixes. **A correction bigger than the mistake makes the mistake grow**, and that sentence is as true of a robot as of many things outside robotics.

1. **Ages 8 and 9.** On a paper number line, start a counter at 10 and move it 3 towards zero each turn. Where does it end up after eight turns?
2. **Ages 10 and 11.** Run both versions. Find, by testing, the largest gain that still settles without crossing the line at all.
3. **Ages 12 and up.** Change the gain from 2.2 to 2.0 and predict the result before running. Explain why 2 is the turning point in this model.

## At what age should a child start robotics or coding?

Coding can start at 6 with blocks. Competitive robotics, by the WRO's own rules, starts at 8, and the senior categories run to 19. A child who codes from 6 or 7 arrives at 8 already thinking in steps and conditions.

### A sensible order

**6 to 8:** blocks, sequences and simple conditions on screen. **9 to 11:** typed Python, simulated sensors, games. **12 and up:** control, data and AI, and a robotics team if the child wants one.

Teenagers who want robotics competitions benefit most from solid Python and some mathematics: angles, rates, averages of noisy readings. Our [coding classes for teens in Peru](/coding-classes-for-teens-peru) page sets out those paths.

## How do I know my child is learning to think like a robot programmer?

By how they plan and explain, not by what they assemble. Our own stages:

**Our stages in control and decision logic**

| Stage | Can do | Can explain |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Exact steps | Guide a character round a grid with no wasted moves | "Forward two, turn right, forward one." |
| 2. Repeat | Use a loop instead of copying the same step | "Repeat four times makes a square." |
| 3. Sense and decide | React to a number with if and else | "If it is dark, the light turns on." |
| 4. Keep checking | Loop over readings and decide each time | "It checks again every step." |
| 5. Control | Correct in proportion to an error | "Big error, big turn; small error, small turn." |
| 6. Test the edges | Find the setting where a system fails | "Above 2 it runs away, so I keep it below." |

### For parents

Ask your child to explain a decision their program makes. If they can say the rule in a sentence, they understand it.
Stage 5 is the steering lesson. It is often the point where children see that maths is useful.

### For young engineers

Predict what your program will do before you run it, especially at the extremes.
When something wobbles or overshoots, ask whether your correction is too big.
