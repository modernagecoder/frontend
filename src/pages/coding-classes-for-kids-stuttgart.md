## When does a Stuttgart child first learn computing at school?

In grade 5, in a subject that is only now being built. Everything below comes from the Gesellschaft für Informatik, which tracks the rules of all sixteen Länder.

### A new subject, starting in grade 5

Since the 2025/26 school year, Baden-Württemberg has a compulsory subject called Informatik und Medienbildung, computing and media education. The Informatik-Monitor 2026/27 says it is being introduced across secondary school types, beginning in grades 5 and 6 and growing year by year after that.

At Werkrealschule, Hauptschule, Realschule and Gemeinschaftsschule the plan is four lessons a week in total over the years of Sekundarstufe I. That puts the Land among the four that meet the short-term target of four compulsory hours, alongside Hamburg, Sachsen and Schleswig-Holstein.

### A curriculum still being written

The same monitor is frank about one gap: "Hierfür liegt noch kein Bildungsplan vor", there is no curriculum for the subject yet. A consultation is planned in the 2026/27 school year and the new Bildungsplan is to take effect in August 2027. Until then, teachers in grades 5 to 7 lean on older plans and a reading guide.

Primary school, Grundschule, covers grades 1 to 4 in Baden-Württemberg, and the new subject does not reach into it.

**Computing in a Stuttgart school career, from the 2026/27 rules**

| 5 and 6 | All secondary school types | Informatik und Medienbildung, compulsory, new since 2025/26 |

School rules are set by the Land, so they are the same in Stuttgart, home to 612,663 people on 31 December 2024 according to Destatis, as in Esslingen, Ludwigsburg, Böblingen, Fellbach or Waiblingen. Our classes are online, so a family anywhere in the Stuttgart region joins the same groups.

## Which Stuttgart bike counter grew faster?

Stuttgart publishes yearly totals from its two permanent bicycle counters, free for anyone to reuse. Our older children use them to learn a trick that grown-up analysts use every day: when two things are very different in size, compare how fast they grow, not how much.

### The two counters

The city's open data file lists two fixed counters. The first has counted since 1 July 2012 on the König-Karls-Brücke in Bad Cannstatt, on main cycle route 1. The second, in Stuttgart-Süd on the Böblinger Straße, has counted since 10 December 2013.

The file gives one total per counter per year from 2013 to 2022. For the Böblinger Straße in 2013 it says NA, not available, which makes sense: that counter only ran for the last three weeks of the year.

### The question

Which counter grew faster between 2014 and 2022? A child looks at the numbers and says the bridge, because its totals are much bigger. The first program agrees. The second program shows why that answer is unfair.

Children work from a short file, stuttgart_bikes.csv, holding three columns: year, counter and count. Rows marked NA are skipped on purpose, so that one empty year cannot crash the program.

**Version 1: how many more bikes?** (Python)
```python
import csv

counts = {}
with open("stuttgart_bikes.csv", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        if row["count"] == "NA":
            continue
        counts[(row["counter"], int(row["year"]))] = int(row["count"])

for counter in ["König-Karls-Brücke", "Böblinger Straße"]:
    first = counts[(counter, 2014)]
    last = counts[(counter, 2022)]
    print(counter, "grew by", last - first)
```
It prints: König-Karls-Brücke grew by 384076 and Böblinger Straße grew by 203508.

The bridge added 384,076 bikes a year, the Böblinger Straße 203,508. So the bridge grew faster? Not so quick. The bridge started in 2014 with more than three times as many bikes, so adding a lot was easy for it. To compare fairly we turn each counter's 2014 total into 100 and scale every other year the same way. This is called an index, and it is how prices, sales and many other numbers in the news are compared.

**Version 2: give both counters the same start** (Python)
```python
for counter in ["König-Karls-Brücke", "Böblinger Straße"]:
    base = counts[(counter, 2014)]
    index = []
    for year in range(2014, 2023):
        index.append(round(100 * counts[(counter, year)] / base))
    print(counter, index)
```
It prints: König-Karls-Brücke [100, 113, 110, 112, 134, 137, 189, 154, 152] and Böblinger Straße [100, 104, 108, 117, 147, 154, 195, 178, 195].

Now the picture flips. By 2022 the bridge stands at 152, about half as many bikes again as in 2014, while the Böblinger Straße stands at 195, almost twice its 2014 level. The smaller counter grew faster. Both lines jump in 2020, the highest year for the bridge, and the Böblinger Straße beats its own 2020 total in 2022 by only 450 bikes. The file does not say why the numbers moved, so we teach children not to invent a reason the data does not give.

1. **Ages 6 to 8, with a ruler.** Draw two towers of bricks, one of 3 and one of 9. Add 2 bricks to the small one and 3 to the big one. Which tower grew more? Which one changed more, compared with how tall it was?
2. **Ages 8 to 10, in Scratch.** Store both lists of totals and make a sprite say which counter added more bikes. Then divide each year by the first year and multiply by 100 before you compare.
3. **Ages 10 to 12, in Python.** Run Version 1, write down your answer, then write Version 2 yourself before you look at ours.
4. **Going further.** Choose 2018 as the base year instead of 2014. Does the faster counter change? Explain why the base year matters, and why 2013 cannot be the base year for the Böblinger Straße.

## Is there a contest my Stuttgart child could enter this school year?

One good choice for ages 8 to 12 is the Känguru der Mathematik, a multiple-choice maths contest written at school, which moves to a later date in 2027.

### What it is

The Känguru der Mathematik is an individual contest of 75 minutes. Pupils in grades 3 and 4, and in grades 5 and 6, each face 24 problems; older pupils get 30. In March 2026 more than 920,000 pupils from more than 13,000 schools took part, according to the organisers.

It is a maths contest, not a coding one, but the thinking overlaps a great deal: spotting a pattern, ruling options out, working backwards from an answer.

### When it runs in 2027

Normally the contest falls on the third Thursday of March. For 2027 the organisers moved it to 8 April 2027, since the March date would land right before Easter holidays in nearly all Länder.

Online registration opens on 1 December 2026 and closes on 31 January 2027. Ask your child's school early whether it takes part.

None of the contest's own problems appear in our lessons. Mentors invent fresh puzzles of a similar kind and train the habits behind them, such as checking each answer option in turn rather than guessing. Children who enjoy those puzzles usually enjoy the Känguru, and a child who later wants a computing contest can try the Informatik-Biber, which runs in schools from 9 to 20 November 2026. We are not connected with either organiser.
