Video game design for kids

Ask a child what they want to make and the answer is usually enormous: an open world, twenty weapons, a boss that changes shape. Game design is the skill of turning that dream into something a stranger can pick up and enjoy in the first thirty seconds. It is about rules, choices, difficulty and fairness, and it can be practised with paper and a pencil before a single line of code exists. This page is for Australian parents whose child loves inventing games more than anything. It explains what video game design actually involves, works through one design idea in detail, shows how playtesting with real players changes a game, and links that work to what the Digital Technologies curriculum asks for at school.

In short

Video game design for kids is planning how a game plays before or alongside building it: its goal, its rules, how each level introduces a new idea, how hard it gets and how a player knows what to do without being told. Children learn it by sketching levels on paper, testing them on real players and changing what does not work, then building the design in Scratch, Roblox Studio or Python. What design means

Design is the part of a game you cannot see

Players notice art and music. What makes them keep playing is a set of decisions they never see written down. Those decisions are the designer's job.

The questions a game designer answers
QuestionAn example answerWhat goes wrong if nobody asks
What is the goal?Reach the flag before the timer reaches zeroPlayers wander, unsure whether they are winning
What can the player do?Run, jump and push cratesControls feel random, or some do nothing useful
How does the player learn?A safe first room where only jumping is neededA wall of instructions nobody reads
How hard is it, and when?Gentle at first, a spike at the end of each worldChildren quit on level two or get bored on level ten
Is it fair?Every danger can be seen before it hurts youPlayers feel cheated and stop trusting the game
How does it feel?A sound and a small bounce when a coin is collectedSuccess goes unnoticed, so nothing feels rewarding

Why design suits children so well

Design needs imagination, empathy and judgement more than technical knowledge, so a seven-year-old can do real design work long before they can write much code.

It also teaches a habit that matters far beyond games: imagining how someone else will experience what you made, and being willing to change it when they struggle.

Why design is not enough on its own

A design that is never built is never tested. Many brilliant paper ideas turn out to be dull, confusing or impossible once real players touch them.

That is why our lessons swing between the two: sketch, build a rough version, test, then redesign. Children who only design never learn what their ideas cost to make; children who only code often build games nobody enjoys.

A worked example

Level gating: one new idea at a time

Here is a single design idea worked through in full. It is one of the first things we teach, and it changes how children plan every game afterwards.

A child is designing a platform game with three mechanics: jumping, moving platforms and spikes. Their first plan puts all three in level one, "so it is exciting". Testers fall onto spikes before they have learned to jump, cannot tell why they died and give up. The fix is called level gating: each level introduces exactly one new idea in a safe place, lets the player practise it, and only later combines ideas the player already knows.

The same three mechanics, ungated and gated
LevelUngated planGated plan
1Jumping, moving platforms and spikes, all at onceJumping only, over gaps that cannot hurt you
2Harder versions of everythingMoving platforms only, with a soft floor underneath
3A bossSpikes only, placed where the player can see them early
4Another bossJumping plus spikes: two ideas the player already owns
5More spikesMoving platforms plus spikes
6The endAll three together, as the final test

The counting question

With three mechanics, how many different mixes could a level use? Three on their own, three pairs and one with all three: seven in total.

The general rule

For n mechanics the count of non-empty mixes is 2 to the power of n, minus 1. Four mechanics give 15, five give 31 (our arithmetic). The list doubles with each new idea.

The design lesson

No designer can build a level for every mix once a game has a handful of mechanics. They choose which mixes teach something and leave the rest out, deliberately.

Teaching without words

A well gated first level needs no instructions at all. If the only way forward is a small gap over a safe floor, the player will try jumping, and the game has taught them without a sentence of text.

Children love discovering this, because it means their game can be played by a younger sibling who cannot yet read the instructions.

What children do next

Older students extend the idea to difficulty: after introducing an idea, raise the challenge, then give a short easy stretch before the next new idea. They plan it as a simple chart before building anything.

They also learn to cut. A mechanic that does not combine well with the others is usually better removed than kept for its own sake.

Playtesting

Watching someone else play your game

The most important design tool costs nothing: another person playing your game while you stay silent and watch.

A simple playtest routine children can run at home
StepWhat the designer doesWhy it matters
1. Stay quietHands the game over with no explanationReal players will not have the designer beside them
2. Watch hands and faceNotes where the player hesitates, frowns or laughsConfusion shows long before a player says anything
3. CountWrites down how many tries each level takesTurns "it feels hard" into something you can compare
4. Ask one questionAsks "what were you trying to do there?"Reveals whether the player understood the goal
5. Change one thingFixes the biggest problem only, then tests againChanging everything at once hides what helped

Who to test with

Siblings, cousins, friends from school and classmates in a lesson all make good testers. The most useful are people who have not watched the game being made.

In our group lessons, students regularly play each other's games and report back, which gives every young designer honest testers of their own age.

Taking criticism well

The hardest part of playtesting for a child is hearing that their favourite level is confusing. We teach them to treat it as information about the game, not a judgement of themselves.

A useful rule is to believe what testers do and be more careful with what they suggest. If three players miss the same door, the door needs fixing; how to fix it is still the designer's call.

Does my child need to code to design games?

Not at first. Younger children can do real design work on paper and with simple tools.

Learning to build designs soon matters, though, because building and testing is how a designer finds out whether an idea works.

What if my child only wants to make huge games?

Almost every young designer does. We keep the dream and shrink the first version until it can be finished in a few weeks.

Finishing a small game teaches more than starting ten large ones, and the big dream becomes the long-term project.

Parents ask

What Australian parents ask about game design for kids

What is video game design for kids?

It is planning how a game plays: its goal, rules, levels, difficulty and feedback, so that someone else can enjoy it. Children practise it on paper and in simple tools, then build and test their designs.

What is level gating in game design?

It is introducing one new idea per level in a safe place, letting the player practise it, and only combining ideas the player already knows later on. It lets a game teach itself without written instructions.

Is game design different from game development?

Design decides how a game should play; development builds it. They overlap constantly, and children learn most by doing both: designing, building a rough version, testing and redesigning.

What age can my child start learning game design?

From about seven, using paper levels and Scratch. Design needs imagination and empathy more than technical skill, so it suits younger children well.

Does game design count for anything at school?

It practises the design strand of the Australian Curriculum v9 Digital Technologies, which asks students to design user interfaces and user experiences from Years 5 and 6 onwards.

How do I help my child playtest at home?

Play their game without asking for help while they watch quietly, count how many tries each level takes, and answer honestly when they ask what you were trying to do.

Will my child just play games in lessons?

No. Lessons are for sketching, building, testing and fixing. Playing each other's games is a short, structured part of testing.

Which tools will my child use?

Paper first, then Scratch for younger children, Roblox Studio or Python for older ones, and Godot or Unity for teens. The tool follows the design, not the other way round.

Can my child design without a powerful computer?

Yes. Paper design needs no computer, and Scratch runs in a normal browser. We check your computer before recommending a heavier engine.
