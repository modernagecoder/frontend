Connection

Can a family in Peru follow live online maths classes at home?

In Lima, almost certainly; elsewhere, very often, but it is worth checking the device before the connection. INEI's figures for the second quarter of 2026 show why.

The connection

Two homes in three across Peru now have an internet service at home, and in Lima Metropolitana more than four in five. A live maths class uses about as much connection as an ordinary video call, so a home that can hold one can hold the other.

In rural areas the picture is different: barely more than a quarter of homes had internet in 2026. For those families a class may have to happen somewhere with a stronger signal, and we would rather say so now than after a dropped first lesson.

The device

The computer column is the one to watch. Only 36.4% of Peruvian homes had at least one computer in 2026, and among those, INEI found 74.3% had just one. In a family with two schoolchildren and a parent working from home, that single machine is in demand.

Maths can be followed on a tablet, and a phone is fine for listening, but writing fractions or equations on a small touch screen is slow. If the only computer is shared, choose a class hour when it is free; we would rather move the hour than teach on a phone.

Live or recorded

What is the difference between live online maths and recorded lessons?

A recorded lesson explains; a live lesson listens. Videos are useful for revision, but maths is learnt by doing problems, and the most useful moment in a lesson is when a teacher sees the wrong step and asks why.

The teacher sees the working

On a shared whiteboard the learner writes each line and the teacher watches it appear. A sign error is caught in the second line, not discovered in the last one. With a video, nobody sees the working at all.

Questions get answered now

A learner who is lost can say "I do not understand why we divide here" and get an answer in the same minute, in plain English. A recorded lesson cannot hear the question, so the confusion waits until homework.

The pace belongs to the learner

A live teacher slows down when faces look puzzled and moves on when an idea has landed. In a one-to-one class that pace is set entirely around one learner; in a group, around learners of a similar level.

Our classes are always live. We do send short practice sets between lessons, and learners may revisit material at their own pace, but the teaching itself happens with a person, on video, at an hour your family chooses. All of it is in English: the teacher speaks clearly, writes the key words on the board and checks that each instruction has been understood.

A real lesson

What does an online maths lesson look like? Percentage points with real INEI data

Here is a lesson we teach to learners from about 13 and to adults, using the INEI table above. It deals with a mistake that turns up in newspapers, school projects and work reports alike: mixing up percentage points with per cent.

The question

INEI says internet at home rose by 4.3 percentage points in Lima Metropolitana, by 3.5 in other urban areas and by 3.4 in rural areas between the second quarters of 2025 and 2026. The 2025 figures were 80.4%, 62.7% and 23.7%.

We ask the learner a simple question: where did home internet grow fastest? Most people look at the three numbers and answer "Lima". Version 1 writes that answer as code, and prints a percentage sign after the difference.

The trap

Subtracting one percentage from another does not give a percentage; it gives percentage points. Lima moved from 80.4% to 84.7%, which is 4.3 points. Saying it "grew by 4.3%" is a different claim, and a wrong one.

To ask how fast something grew, you compare the change with where it started. That is Version 2: divide the points by the 2025 figure.

The ranking flips. Lima added the most points but grew the least relative to where it began; rural homes added the fewest points but their share of homes online rose by about a seventh in a single year. The biggest jump in points was the slowest growth. Both statements are true at once, and a careful writer says which one they mean.

There is a second lesson underneath. A small starting number makes relative growth look dramatic, so neither measure is "the honest one" on its own. We ask learners to write one English sentence that uses both, for example: "Rural home internet rose by 3.4 percentage points, a 14% increase on 2025, but it still reached only about a quarter of rural homes." That sentence would survive any fact-checker.

  1. Ages 9 to 11, no code. A class had 20 children and now has 25; another had 4 and now has 8. Which class grew more? Which grew faster? Explain the difference in one sentence.
  2. Ages 12 to 14, on paper. Using the computer column in the table, say how many points separate Lima from rural areas. Then say how many times more likely a Lima home is to have a computer.
  3. Ages 14 and up, Python. Run Version 1, then fix it without looking at Version 2. Add the national figures, 60.7% in 2025 and 64.5% in 2026, and see where Peru as a whole lands.
  4. University and adults. Find one news headline that says "rose by X%" about a percentage. Decide whether it means points or per cent, and rewrite it so nobody can misread it.

The school year

How do online maths classes fit around the Peruvian school year?

Easily, because they do not depend on it. MINEDU's norm for 2026 set 36 teaching weeks in four blocks, with short breaks between them, and classes with us can continue, pause or speed up across those breaks.

The 2026 calendar, in brief

Under RM 501-2025-MINEDU, the 2026 school year taught in four blocks of nine weeks: 16 March to 15 May, 25 May to 24 July, 10 August to 9 October and 19 October to 18 December. Between them came breaks of one or two weeks: 18 to 22 May, 27 July to 7 August, and 12 to 16 October.

The same norm lets regional education authorities set a different distribution of weeks where the climate or local life calls for it, so a school in the highlands or the Amazon may not match Lima exactly. Your child's school calendar is the one that counts.

How families use it

Many families keep the same weekly rhythm all year. Others use a break week for a short burst of extra sessions on a topic that went badly in the block just finished, which is easy online because nobody has to travel.

The longest pause is the summer, from the end of the school year in December until the next one begins (in 2026 that was mid-March). Learners who keep one or two maths classes a week through those months arrive at the new year with nothing forgotten; the class hour can move to a summer morning if that suits the family better.

Inside school, the national curriculum gives secundaria pupils four maths periods of 45 minutes a week on the regular school day, and six on the full school day. A live class with us adds a fixed, quiet hour where nobody else is asking the teacher a question, which is often what a pupil who sits in a large class most needs.

Schools themselves are going online in other ways. In September 2026 MINEDU announced a plan to connect 55,000 public schools to satellite internet. We are not part of that plan or connected with MINEDU; we mention it because more families will soon be choosing between online options, and it helps to know what to ask.

Set-up

What do you need at home for an online maths class?

Less than you might think, and nothing you need to buy from us. These are the steps we suggest before the first class.

  1. Choose the screen. A laptop or desktop is best; a tablet with a stylus or keyboard is workable. Keep the phone for WhatsApp messages with the teacher.
  2. Test the connection at the class hour. Run a short video call at the same time of day as the planned class, because evening connections in a busy home are often slower than morning ones.
  3. Find a quiet corner. A table, a chair, decent light on the learner's face and paper within reach. Maths is still worked on paper as well as on screen.
  4. Use a headset if you can. A cheap headset with a microphone removes echo and makes the teacher's English much easier to follow.
  5. Agree the hour on your own clock. You book in Peru time and the teacher's calendar converts it. Nothing changes between summer and winter, because neither country moves its clocks.
  6. Tell us the school topic. If a test is coming, say which chapter. Live lessons can follow the school's order when that helps.

A parent does not need to sit beside a learner of ten or older for the whole class. For a six or seven year old, a grown-up nearby for the first few lessons helps with the camera, the microphone and the occasional lost cursor.

In English

Is it hard to learn maths online in English from Peru?

For most learners, no more than learning it in a classroom, once the first few lessons have passed. Maths needs a modest vocabulary and a lot of symbols that are identical in Spanish and English.

What the index says

The EF English Proficiency Index 2025 puts Peru at 519 points, "Moderate", with Lima at 555, "High". The weakest skill across Peru is speaking, at 480. EF measures adults who sit its test online, not schoolchildren, but it matches what our teachers see: learners understand more than they say.

Online maths suits that profile. The learner mostly listens, reads and writes, and speaks in short phrases: "I think it is seven", "I do not understand the second line".

What the teacher does

Teachers speak slowly, use the same words for the same operations every time and put each new word on the board with a picture or an example. Nobody is expected to speak perfect English; everyone is expected to try.

Over a term, those short answers grow into full explanations. A learner who can say "I divided by 80.4 because I want growth compared with the start" has learnt the maths and the English in one sentence.

At the hour you choose. Peru keeps UTC minus 5 all year without daylight saving, and India keeps UTC plus 5:30, so India is 10 hours 30 minutes ahead of Peru on every day of the year. The table converts; it does not schedule.

Thinking, not copying

Will online classes teach my child to think, or just to copy answers?

To think. A live teacher who watches the working can tell the difference between a learner who understands and one who has found the answer somewhere, and the lessons are built to make understanding the only way through.

Working on the board

Every answer comes with its steps, written live. A copied answer has no steps behind it, and the teacher asks for them.

AI used carefully

Claude's consumer terms require users to be at least 18, and ChatGPT's terms ask under-18s for a parent's permission. Children do not use those accounts in our classes; older learners may use AI only to check reasoning they have already done.

One standard, any address

Lima or Puno, a city flat or a farmhouse with a good signal: the same teachers, the same lessons and the same expectations apply.

The lesson above is a fair sample. A chatbot will happily print "grew by 4.3%"; the learner who has done the lesson knows to ask "per cent of what?" That question is the skill we are teaching, and it carries well beyond maths.

For parents

Stage 5 is the lesson on this page. It is where maths starts to protect a learner from misleading numbers in the news.

Ask what your child got wrong this week and why. A clear answer is a better sign than a perfect worksheet.

For learners

When a number changes, ask "by how much?" and "compared with what?" before you say anything.

Write the unit after every answer: points, per cent, minutes, soles. Most mistakes hide in a missing unit.

Questions from Peru

Online maths classes in Peru: questions and answers

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. All our maths classes are live, online and in English only. Teachers speak clearly, match the learner's pace and accent, and write the important words on the board, so a learner whose English is still growing can keep up.

Are the classes live or recorded?

Live, every time. A teacher meets the learner on video, they share a whiteboard and work through problems together. We send short practice sets between classes, but the teaching is never a recording.

What do we need at home?

A steady internet connection, a laptop, desktop or tablet with a keyboard or stylus, and ideally a headset with a microphone. A phone is fine for messages but too small for writing maths comfortably.

Will the classes work outside Lima?

Usually, if the connection is steady at the class hour. INEI reports that 84.7% of homes in Lima Metropolitana and 66.2% in other urban areas had internet in the second quarter of 2026, against 27.1% in rural areas. Test a video call at the planned hour before the first class.

What time are classes in Peru?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India is always 10 hours 30 minutes ahead of Peru, so 09:00 in Peru is 19:30 in India.

Which ages and levels do you teach?

From age 6 to adults: primaria and secundaria maths, Cambridge IGCSE, IB Maths, SAT maths, university calculus and linear algebra, and statistics for work.

Can classes continue during the school holidays?

Yes. Because classes are online and the hour is yours, many families keep going through the summer break and the shorter breaks between school blocks, or add a few extra sessions on a weak topic.

Do you follow the Peruvian school curriculum?

We teach alongside school, not in its place. Our courses cover the maths a learner of that age meets, and in one-to-one classes the teacher can follow the order of your child's school topics. The school sets and marks its own tests.

Are you connected with MINEDU or any school?

No. We are an independent online school with teachers based in India. We are not connected with MINEDU, any UGEL, university or school in Peru, and we do not issue Peruvian qualifications. Learners who complete a course receive our own certificate of completion.

How do we pay from Peru?

By card, in US dollars; your bank converts each payment into soles. The first class is free and booking it does not require a card.
