AI and Python classes for teens in Guelph

Where can a Guelph teenager learn Python, data and AI with a live teacher?

Modern Age Coders teaches Python and AI to Guelph students between 13 and 18 in live online classes, in groups of fifteen to twenty or one to one. The usual start is Python for Teens; after that, students choose AI and Machine Learning for Teens, CCC preparation, or the Ontario Computer Science Companion beside ICS3U and ICS4U. Every class runs on Eastern time, and the first is free.

In Ontario, data arrives in a student’s coding before most families notice. The Grade 8 math curriculum already asks for code that analyses data, Grade 9 math pairs code with algebra, and the senior computer science courses add software design and the place of computers in society. For a Guelph teenager that is a good thread to pull on, because the most useful skill in modern AI is not writing a prompt but working out what a column of numbers can and cannot tell you. This page quotes what the ministry asks at each stage, lists what CEMC offers free, sets out which AI tools a 13 to 17 year old may use under each company’s terms, and walks through a forecasting project on the City of Guelph’s own green bin records.

Ontario, Grade 8 to 12

The data thread in Ontario coding, from Grade 8 math to ICS4U

Quoted text: the Ontario curriculum service and the 2008 computer studies document, both checked on 22 October 2026. Right-hand notes: ours.

Coding and data in Ontario courses a Guelph teen meets (Ontario Ministry of Education curriculum API and 2008 computer studies document, checked 22 October 2026)
Grade and courseMinistry wordingOur note
Grade 8 math, C3.1Students code “including code that involves the analysis of data in order to inform and communicate decisions”A 13 year old is already expected to use code to reason about data
Grade 8 math, C3.2Students “read and alter existing code involving the analysis of data in order to inform and communicate decisions”Reading someone else’s analysis and changing it, a real-world skill
Grade 9, MTH1W, C2.1Students “use coding to demonstrate an understanding of algebraic concepts including variables, parameters, equations, and inequalities”Variables in code and variables in algebra, taught as one idea
Grade 10, ICD2O (2023)Strands titled “Computational Thinking and Making Connections”, “Hardware, Software, and Innovations” and “Programming”Broad by design: thinking, technology and code in one course
Grades 11 and 12, ICS3U and ICS4U (2008)Each course opens with Programming Concepts and Skills and Software Development; “The fourth strand (strand D) deals with the role of computers in society”Building software and asking what it does to people, both on the syllabus

Notice how often the word data appears before Grade 11. The Grade 8 expectations do not ask students to analyse data by hand and then learn to code separately; they ask for code that analyses data to support a decision. That is exactly the shape of a small machine learning task, and it is why our Guelph students meet real public datasets early. There is also a small curriculum change families sometimes ask about: the 2008 document still describes a Grade 10 course, ICS2O, that is “organized in three strands”, but the current ministry list of computer studies courses does not include ICS2O and does include ICD2O. We state both facts and leave the history to the ministry; the practical point is that ICD2O is the Grade 10 course on today’s list.

Grade 8: real files early

We give Grade 8 students a small real table, ask a question it can answer, and have them write the code that answers it. The decision part matters: what would you do differently because of this number?

Grades 9 and 10: algebra meets code

MTH1W’s link between code variables and algebra is a gift. Students who see that a function in Python and a function in math are the same idea find both subjects easier.

Grades 11 and 12: the society strand

Strand D asks about computers in society. AI gives that question plenty to work with, and our older students write short, evidence-based notes on what a model they built could get wrong.

Credits are the school’s

Our lessons sit beside the school day, and we do not grant Ontario credits: ICD2O, ICS3U and ICS4U are earned only at school. Modern Age Coders has no connection with the Ontario Ministry of Education or any Guelph-area school board.

Free material is valuable, and we point students to it. What it cannot do is notice the specific mistake a particular student keeps making. City of Guelph data project

Can a simple model forecast Guelph’s green bin volume a year ahead?

The City of Guelph publishes the monthly volume of organic waste collected through its green bin program from 2012 to 2019. Our students clean the table, then forecast every month of 2019 from earlier years and check how far off each forecast is. All results here are our own calculations; the volumes are the City’s, in the units it published, which the dataset does not state.

Our forecasts of the 12 months of 2019 on the City of Guelph Total Organic Waste Volume by Month 2012_2019 file (96 monthly rows fetched 22 October 2026)
Forecast methodHow it guesses a 2019 monthAverage miss per month
Flat averageThe 2018 monthly average, for every month98.2
Same month last yearThe value for the same month in 201838.5
Same month, six-year meanThe average of that month from 2013 to 201851.4

The code below uses nothing beyond Python’s standard library, and most of it is cleaning: the year appears only on each January row, so the code carries it forward, and the month names arrive in several spellings with stray spaces, so the code trims and shortens them. Only then does it forecast. Run on 22 October 2026, it printed the three averages shown above:import json, urllib.request URL = ("https://services5.arcgis.com/k3dd78JyG9GFoZHG/arcgis/rest/services/" "CoG_Org_Waste_Month_Vol_2012_2019/FeatureServer/0/query" "?where=1%3D1&outFields=*&orderByFields=ObjectId&f=json") rows = [f["attributes"] for f in json.load(urllib.request.urlopen(URL))["features"]] # The year is filled in only on each January row, and month names are typed # inconsistently ("Feb ", "April", "Sept "), so clean both before anything else. volume, year = {}, None for r in rows: year = r["Year"] or year volume[(year, r["Month"].strip()[:3].title())] = r["Volume"] MONTHS = ["Jan", "Feb", "Mar", "Apr", "May", "Jun", "Jul", "Aug", "Sep", "Oct", "Nov", "Dec"] print(len(rows), "rows,", len(volume), "year-month cells,", min(y for y, m in volume), "to", max(y for y, m in volume)) def miss(guess): return round(sum(abs(guess(m) - volume[(2019, m)]) for m in MONTHS) / 12, 1) last_year = sum(volume[(2018, m)] for m in MONTHS) / 12 print("flat 2018 monthly average:", miss(lambda m: last_year)) print("same month in 2018:", miss(lambda m: volume[(2018, m)])) print("same month, mean of 2013-2018:", miss(lambda m: sum(volume[(y, m)] for y in range(2013, 2019)) / 6)) lowest = [min(MONTHS, key=lambda m: volume[(y, m)]) for y in range(2012, 2020)] print("lowest month each year, 2012-2019:", lowest)

1. Clean like a spreadsheet

The table looks like a copied spreadsheet: a year written once, then blanks, and month labels typed sixteen different ways, such as “Aug” and “Aug ” with a trailing space. Students who skip this step get twelve months that do not line up.

2. The seasonal naive baseline

Guessing that a month will look like the same month last year is called a seasonal naive forecast, and it is the baseline every serious forecaster tries first. Here it beats both alternatives: an average miss of 38.5 against 51.4 and 98.2.

3. Why the long average loses

A six-year mean should be steadier, yet it does worse. In 10 of the 12 months the 2018 value is higher than the 2013 value, so the older years drag the mean down. Recent data wins when a series is trending, an idea students test by trying three-year and two-year means.

February and the calendar

February has the lowest total in every year from 2013 to 2019. That looks like a pattern in behaviour until a student divides by the number of days: per day, February is the lowest month only in 2014, 2015 and 2019. Most of the dip is simply a shorter month. Turning totals into per-day rates is a first taste of feature engineering, choosing what to measure so that a model learns the right thing.

A few more honest details. The program began in 2012, and January 2012 shows 74.33 against 256.67 in February, so the first months describe a launch rather than normal service, which is why the six-year mean starts at 2013. The same-month model’s biggest misses on 2019 were April, 81.5 too low, and August, 73.3 too high. The City notes that the figures do not include organic waste collected in Waterloo Region and processed at the Guelph Waste Resource Innovation Centre, so this is Guelph’s own green bin collection only. We never add the monthly rows into yearly totals; the City publishes a separate yearly file for that. The data is used under the City of Guelph’s Open Data Licence Version 2.0, which grants a licence “to use the Information including for commercial purposes”, and attribution goes to the City of Guelph under its Open Government Licence. No endorsement by the City of Guelph is implied.

The green bin forecast shows why we hold AI tools back for teens who are still learning. An assistant asked to forecast the series would produce a confident model in seconds. It would be unlikely to point out that the year is missing from eleven rows in twelve, that “Sept ” and “Sept” are the same month, or that February’s dip is mostly the calendar. Those are the observations that turn a script into an analysis, and a Guelph teen who makes them is ready to use AI tools well later, because they will know what to check.

Baselines first

Why a Guelph teen should beat a simple baseline before reaching for AI

Because “same month last year” beat two more elaborate guesses, and only a student who tried it would know.

Forecasting is full of clever methods that lose to a plain baseline. A teen who has watched a six-year average lose to last year’s value understands something many adults do not: complexity is not accuracy, and every model has to earn its place against the simplest reasonable guess.

We teach in that spirit. State the question, clean the data, try the obvious answer, then try to beat it, and explain why the winner won. AI tools can join later, inside the age rules, as a reviewer whose suggestions are tested like any other model. Students who learn this way can tell a good result from a lucky one.

On CCC day there is no assistant at all, so the reasoning has to be the student’s from the first line. The longer argument is in an argument for learning to code in 2026.

FAQ

Guelph families ask

The questions Guelph parents and teens send us most.

Are classes held in Guelph?

They are online and live, so students join from home in Guelph. There is no local centre; teens choose a group of fifteen to twenty or a private teacher.

My child is in Grade 8. Is that too early?

No. Ontario's Grade 8 math already asks for code that analyses data, and Python for Teens starts at that level.

Do your classes give an Ontario credit?

No. We teach alongside school courses and do not grant Ontario credits; ICD2O, ICS3U and ICS4U credits come only from the school.

Is ChatGPT allowed for my 13 year old?

OpenAI's terms set 13 as the minimum and require a parent's or guardian's permission under 18. We involve it only where a parent agrees.

Do teens use Claude in your lessons?

No. Anthropic's terms say users must be at least 18, so Claude is never used with anyone younger.

Are CEMC's resources really free?

Yes. CEMC says no registration is required for its Courseware, and its Problem of the Week sends new problems every Thursday. It also notes Courseware gives no certificate.

Which CCC level should a Guelph student write first?

Junior, in most cases: CEMC describes it as suited to beginner to intermediate programmers. Students move to Senior once Junior feels routine.

Can my teen use autocomplete or AI in the CCC?

No. The CCC grader rules ban generative AI and say AI-based tools within an editor cannot be used, so contest practice uses a plain editor.

When do classes run for Guelph students?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Times are confirmed in Eastern time.

Is the green bin project real City data?

Yes. It uses the City of Guelph's published monthly organic waste volumes under its open data licence. Students clean and forecast it themselves; the City has not endorsed our courses.
