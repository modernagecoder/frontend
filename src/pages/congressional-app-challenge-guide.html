Congressional App Challenge guide

The Congressional App Challenge is the competition where a student’s app is judged by the office of their own Member of Congress. It is open to middle and high school students, it accepts any programming language and any topic, and a winning app is featured on the House.gov website. This guide is built from the official 2026 rulebook, read in full: who can enter, what a submission needs, what the judges look at, how the rules treat AI tools, and a ten-week plan our teachers use to take a student from a blank page to a working app and a clear demo video.

In short

The Congressional App Challenge is a coding competition for US middle and high school students, run district by district and judged by each Member of Congress’s office. Students enter alone or in teams of up to four, build an app in any language on any topic, and submit a 1 to 3 minute demo video plus written answers. The 2026 rulebook sets the deadline at 12:00 pm EDT, Monday, October 26, 2026.

Who can enter

The 2026 eligibility rules, line by line

These are the rules as the official 2026 rulebook states them. Each later cycle publishes its own rulebook, so check the current one on the official site before you register.

Congressional App Challenge eligibility, from the official 2026 rules (read 17 October 2026)
RuleWhat the 2026 rules say
Age"Students must be enrolled in middle school or high school on October 26th, 2026." Middle school "typically encompasses 6th-8th grades".
Residency"All competing students must be U.S. residents at the time of submission. There is no citizen requirement for students."
DistrictCompete in the district where you live or where you attend school; a district must be hosting a Challenge, and you may submit in only one district.
TeamsIndividuals, or "teams of up to four (4) students"; at least half of a team must live or attend school in the same Congressional District.
Prior work"Students may submit any app they've created after October 30th, 2025." A 2.0 version highlights only the new code.
Entries"Students may only submit ONE app per year." No entries across several teams.

What is open

Almost everything technical. The rules allow any topic, and any platform including web apps, desktop apps, mobile apps, browser extensions, robots, augmented and virtual reality, wearables and embedded systems.

Any language is allowed too, from C, C++, Java, JavaScript, Python and Swift to block-based coding. A sixth grader’s Scratch project and a senior’s React app compete under the same rules.

What trips families up

Registration asks for a personal email address that is not a school account, a parent or guardian’s name and email, and both home and school addresses with the full 9-digit zip code and Congressional District.

Finding the district matters, because a student can compete in only one. Look up both addresses early, and check that your district appears on the official list of participating districts.

Source: Congressional App Challenge rules page and the linked 2026 rulebook PDF, read 17 October 2026. We are not connected with the Congressional App Challenge or the Internet Education Foundation, which administers it.

The calendar

How a Challenge year runs: the 2026 cycle as published

The organisers publish dates for each cycle. Here is the 2026 calendar as published, which also shows the shape each year follows: registration in winter, building through spring and summer, submission in the fall.

What the calendar means for planning

From a May launch to a late October deadline is roughly five months, which is plenty for a student who starts in spring and very tight for one who starts in October.

A comfortable timeline is an idea in the summer, a working first version by September, and the last weeks for testing and the video.

If you are reading between cycles

Once a deadline passes, the next cycle’s rulebook and dates appear on the official site. Use the months in between to learn the tools and build a small practice app.

Remember the prior-work rule: for 2026, apps created after October 30, 2025 were eligible, so work started in the off season can count if the next rulebook keeps a similar rule.

What to submit

The demo video and six questions the judges read

The rulebook calls the demonstration video the most critical part of the application. It is also where many good apps lose marks, because the video does not explain what the app does.

What the 2026 demonstration video must include, from the official rulebook
ItemRule
Length"The video should be 1-3 minutes long." Videos outside the limit may be penalized.
NamesThe name of each participant and the name of the app
PurposeThe purpose of the app, explained in one clear sentence
AudienceWho the app is intended for
ToolsThe tools and coding languages used
FunctionA showcase of the app working
HostingUploaded to YouTube or Vimeo and set to "public"
  1. What is the title of your app?
  2. Explain the app’s purpose.
  3. What inspired you to create this app?
  4. What technical or coding difficulty did you face, and how did you address it?
  5. What did you learn while participating? What was your biggest takeaway?
  6. What would you change if you were to create a 2.0 version?

Writing the one-sentence purpose

The rules ask for the purpose in one clear sentence, and that sentence does more work than any other line in the application. A useful pattern is: who it helps, what problem it solves, and how.

Compare "An app for school" with "A book-swap app that lets students at our middle school list, find and borrow books from each other in under a minute." The second tells a judge exactly what to look for in the demo.

The technical difficulty answer

Question four is where a student shows they built the app themselves. Name one real bug, say what caused it, and say how it was fixed.

Judges can request the source code, and the rules say refusing that request means disqualification, so every claim in the answers should match the code.

The rulebook is clear that "This is not a video-creation competition." A screen recording with a clear voice-over is enough; fancy editing earns nothing.

The AI rule

Can students use AI tools in a Congressional App Challenge app?

Yes, under conditions the 2026 rules spell out. Families ask about this more than any other rule.

AI, originality and open source in the 2026 rules
TopicWhat the 2026 rules say
AI tools"The use of AI tools in app development for the Congressional App Challenge is permitted, provided that all AI usage is fully disclosed in the submission materials."
Limit on AIAI may support specific aspects of the project and "must not constitute the entirety of the technical development".
OriginalityThe app must be original and created by the student or team; all coding and technical development must be theirs.
LibrariesOpen-source libraries, frameworks and external tools are allowed if their use is clearly documented.

How we apply it in class

Students keep a simple AI log from day one: the date, the tool, what they asked, and what they kept. When the submission asks about AI, the answer is already written and accurate.

Our rule for students is the same as the judges’ spirit: if AI suggested a piece of code, the student must be able to explain it before it stays in the app.

Age-appropriate tools

Many general AI chatbots set their own minimum ages in their terms, and some are for adults only. Under-18 students should use only tools whose terms allow their age, with a parent aware of the account.

For most entries, AI is most useful for explaining an error message or suggesting a test case, not for writing the app.

Judging

How Congressional App Challenge apps are judged

Judging happens district by district, which is the feature that makes this competition unusual.

Judging and prizes in the 2026 rules
PointWhat the 2026 rules say
Who judgesThe Member of Congress and their office, who may use a panel of judges
CriteriaQuality of the idea (creativity and originality); implementation (user experience and design); "Demonstrated excellence of coding and programming skills"
Source codeJudges may request access to the app and source code; refusing means disqualification
WinnersAnnounced directly by the hosting Member of Congress
RecognitionEach district winner is featured on the House.gov and CongressionalAppChallenge.us websites, and can receive a copyright fee waiver under the ARTS Act
Ownership"Contestants will own the intellectual property rights to their Submission"

Reading the three criteria

The idea, the implementation and the code are weighed together. A brilliant idea that barely runs struggles, and so does polished code that solves nothing anyone needs.

Implementation includes user experience and design, so a judge who opens the app should understand it within seconds, without the student in the room.

Why the district matters

Each office judges the entries in its own district, so a student competes against classmates and neighbors, not the whole country.

Apps that solve a problem the student has seen in their own school or community are easy for a local office to understand, which is a natural advantage.

A worked example

Scoping an app: must, should, later

Most unfinished entries are too big, not too hard. Here is how one of our example projects, a book-swap app for a middle school, gets cut down to something a student can finish and demo.

Scoping a school book-swap app (our example; effort is our estimate in class hours)
FeatureBucketEffort (hours)Why
List a book with title and photoMust4Without it there is no app
Search books by titleMust3The core use in the demo
Request to borrowMust5Shows the app solving the problem
Sign in with a school-approved accountShould6Useful, but a demo can use test accounts
Ratings and reviewsLater6Nice, not needed to show the idea
Chat between studentsLater10Big, and raises safety questions

In our estimate the three must features take 12 hours (4 + 3 + 5), which fits weeks 4 and 5 of the plan at three hours a week with time to spare for bugs. Adding everything would take 34 hours, almost three times as long, and the extra features would barely change what a judge sees in a three-minute video.

Questions from families

Congressional App Challenge: common questions

What is the Congressional App Challenge?

It is a coding competition for US middle and high school students, run in participating Congressional Districts and judged by each Member of Congress and their office. It is administered by the Internet Education Foundation.

Who can enter the Congressional App Challenge?

For 2026, students enrolled in middle or high school on October 26, 2026 who were US residents at the time of submission. The rules say there is no citizen requirement.

Can students enter as a team?

Yes. The 2026 rules allow individuals or teams of up to four students, and at least half of a team must live or attend school in the same Congressional District.

What programming language can be used?

Any language, including C, C++, Java, JavaScript, Python, Swift and block-based coding. Any platform is allowed too, from web and mobile to robots and wearables.

Can AI tools be used?

Yes, for 2026, if all AI use is fully disclosed in the submission. AI may support parts of the project but must not be the entirety of the technical development.

How long should the demo video be?

The 2026 rulebook says one to three minutes. It must name the participants and the app, state the purpose in one sentence, describe the audience and tools, and show the app working.

What is the 2026 deadline?

The 2026 rulebook sets the deadline at 12:00 pm EDT on Monday, October 26, 2026. Later cycles publish their own dates on the official site.

How are winners chosen?

Each Member of Congress and their office judge their district, sometimes with a panel. The 2026 criteria were the quality of the idea, its implementation, and demonstrated excellence of coding and programming skills.

What do winners receive?

For 2026, each district winner is featured on the House.gov and CongressionalAppChallenge.us websites and can receive a copyright fee waiver through the ARTS Act. Districts may add certificates or small awards.

Can a middle schooler with only Scratch experience enter?

Yes. Block-based coding is allowed. A small, finished Scratch app with a clear purpose is a real entry; the plan on this page keeps the must list to three features for grades 6 to 8.

How can Modern Age Coders help?

A regular teacher works through the ten-week plan with your student: scoping, building, testing, the demo video and honest AI disclosure. Classes are weekdays or weekends, flexible, in a batch or one to one.
