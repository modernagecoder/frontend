## What does a primaria school in Juliaca teach about computers?

Digital skills, spread across subjects, but no programming lesson. Juliaca's schools follow the national curriculum.

### A competency without a timetable

Peru's national curriculum, the CNEB, includes competency 28, about working in digital environments, and marks it transversal: it is meant to appear in every area. No area is called computing, and no programming language is named anywhere in the standard.

In practice a Juliaca child may use a computer or tablet at school for research or presentations, but is unlikely ever to write a program there.

### What the week does contain

The primaria plan gives Matemática five hours a week in 1° and 2° grado and four from 3° to 6°, the same for Comunicación, and Inglés two hours in the first two grades and three after that.

Coding uses all three. Children count and compare in maths, read and follow instructions in Comunicación, and in our classes practise English while they do both.

## Which day of the week do people in Juliaca work most?

INEI asks working people in Juliaca how many hours they worked on each day of the past week. Our ten to twelve year olds add those answers up, and on the way learn two habits: never copy the same line seven times, and never turn an empty box into a number.

### The data

INEI's Encuesta Permanente de Empleo Nacional surveys homes in Peru's main cities every month. Its EPEN Ciudades 2025 dataset is published on Peru's open data platform under the Open Data Commons Open Database Licence (ODbL), a share-alike licence: if we published a database built from it, ours would have to use the same licence. We publish only counts and code.

The Juliaca file for 2025, juliaca-epen-2025.csv, has 8,402 rows, one for each person in the homes INEI visited across the year. Seven columns, C318_1 to C318_7, hold the hours worked on Sunday, Monday and so on to Saturday. We read those seven and nothing else.

### The question

Ask a class which day people work most and most children say Monday. Some say Saturday, because that is when they see their parents busiest. Nobody can be sure without counting.

The first program tries the obvious thing: add up every number in the Sunday column and the Monday column.

**Version 1: add up two columns** (Python)
```python
import csv

sunday = 0
monday = 0
with open("juliaca-epen-2025.csv", encoding="latin-1") as sheet:
    for line in csv.DictReader(sheet):
        sunday = sunday + int(line["C318_1"])
        monday = monday + int(line["C318_2"])
print("Sunday hours:", sunday)
print("Monday hours:", monday)
```
It stops before printing anything, with the error ValueError: invalid literal for int() with base 10: ''.

The error is in English, and the mentor reads it aloud with the children. "Invalid literal for int" means Python was asked to make a whole number out of something that is not one. The two quote marks at the end are the clue: the thing was empty. Babies, school children and people with no job were in those homes too, and nobody asked them about hours, so their boxes are blank. A blank is not the same as zero; it means "no answer".

Version 2 fixes that and a second problem. If a row has no Sunday answer it is skipped. And instead of seven copied lines, one for each day, the column names go into a list, the day names into another list in the same order, and a single loop walks through both.

**Version 2: skip blanks and loop over the days** (Python)
```python
import csv

columns = ["C318_1", "C318_2", "C318_3", "C318_4", "C318_5", "C318_6", "C318_7"]
days = ["Sunday", "Monday", "Tuesday", "Wednesday", "Thursday", "Friday", "Saturday"]
hours = [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0]
workers = [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0]
answered = 0

with open("juliaca-epen-2025.csv", encoding="latin-1") as sheet:
    for line in csv.DictReader(sheet):
        if line["C318_1"] == "":
            continue
        answered = answered + 1
        for i in range(7):
            h = int(line[columns[i]])
            hours[i] = hours[i] + h
            if h > 0:
                workers[i] = workers[i] + 1

print("People who answered:", answered)
for i in range(7):
    print(days[i].ljust(10), hours[i], "hours,", workers[i], "people worked")
```
It prints People who answered: 3416, then Sunday 9816 hours, 1297 people worked, Monday 20218 hours, 2636 people worked, Tuesday 19136 hours, 2535 people worked, Wednesday 19646 hours, 2607 people worked, Thursday 19900 hours, 2601 people worked, Friday 18728 hours, 2461 people worked and Saturday 15022 hours, 1984 people worked.

**Hours worked by day among the Juliaca people who answered (our calculation)**

| Day | Hours, all answers added | People who worked that day |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | 20,218 | 2,636 |
| Thursday | 19,900 | 2,601 |
| Wednesday | 19,646 | 2,607 |
| Tuesday | 19,136 | 2,535 |
| Friday | 18,728 | 2,461 |
| Saturday | 15,022 | 1,984 |
| Sunday | 9,816 | 1,297 |

Data: INEI, Encuesta Permanente de Empleo Nacional (EPEN) Ciudades 2025, Juliaca file, published on datosabiertos.gob.pe under the Open Data Commons Open Database Licence (ODbL, share-alike). The sums and the ordering are ours. Each person reported the week before their own interview, and interviews ran through all of 2025, so these are not hours for one particular week. They describe the people INEI interviewed, not every worker in Juliaca.

Monday wins, narrowly, so most children were right. The bigger surprise is Sunday: 1,297 of the 3,416 people who answered, about 38%, worked on Sunday as well. For many people in this survey, the working week did not stop at Saturday. Children then ask the next good question themselves: are Sunday workers working fewer hours, or are there just fewer of them? Dividing hours by people answers it, and that is next week's class.

1. **Age 6 to 8, unplugged.** Ask five people at home how many hours they worked yesterday. Write a dash, not a zero, for anyone who did not work. Why does it matter which you write?
2. **Age 8 to 10, in Scratch.** Make two lists, one of day names and one of numbers, and have a sprite say each day with its number. Use one loop, not seven blocks.
3. **Age 10 to 12, in Python.** Run Version 1 and read the error aloud. Fix it yourself before you look at Version 2.
4. **Going further.** Divide each day's hours by its number of workers. On which day do the people who work, work longest?

## What time would a child in Juliaca have class?

At any hour that suits the family. Juliaca is on Peru time all year, and the mentor adjusts to it.

**Some Juliaca hours and the same moment in India (conversions only)**

| Juliaca | India | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 07:30 | 18:00 | Before morning school |
| 10:30 | 21:00 | A holiday or afternoon-shift morning |
| 13:00 | 23:30 | Straight after a morning shift |
| 15:30 | 02:00, next day | Mid-afternoon |
| 17:30 | 04:00, next day | Before dinner |

Peru keeps UTC minus 5 hours throughout the year and has not changed its clocks since 1994 (tz database zone America/Lima; INACAL publishes the official hour). India keeps UTC plus 5 hours 30 minutes, so the gap is always 10 hours 30 minutes.

Since neither country moves its clocks, a slot booked now is the same slot next year. Book in Juliaca time and the system converts it for the mentor. Many families pick a morning class in the January to March holidays and keep it, or move it to the afternoon, once primaria restarts in March.

## Coding classes for kids in Juliaca: questions and answers

### Are the classes in Spanish?

No. Classes are live, online and taught only in English. Mentors use short sentences, show every step, adapt to your child's pace and accent, and when a child answers in Spanish they reply with the English words. We check at the free class that your child can follow.

### Are there coding classes for children in Juliaca?

Yes, live online. Children in Juliaca and nearby, including San Miguel, join from home on a computer. Scratch starts at 6 and typed Python at about 9.

### Is my child's English good enough?

If your child follows simple spoken instructions, usually yes. If not, start with Scratch or problem solving, choose one to one, or wait a few months. The free class shows you in minutes, and we will tell you honestly.

### We have no computer at home. Can my child join?

Not well on a phone alone. A borrowed laptop at a relative's home for two hours a week works. INEI counted a computer in about one Puno home in five in 2025, so you are not alone; tell us and we will help you plan.

### Does primaria in Juliaca teach coding?

No. The national curriculum treats digital skills as transversal and the primaria plan has no programming area. Children use computers at school sometimes but rarely write programs.

### What time are classes in Juliaca?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India is 10 hours 30 minutes ahead of Juliaca all year, so 07:30 in Juliaca is 18:00 for the mentor.

### What age is right to start?

Six, with Scratch blocks, where nothing is typed. From about nine most children are ready for Python. Older children can start straight with Python.

### What is the Juliaca project?

Children aged 10 to 12 add up INEI's Juliaca survey answers about hours worked on each day of the week. They learn to skip empty answers and to use one loop instead of copying code seven times. Monday comes out on top, but 38% of those who answered also worked on Sunday.

### How are fees paid?

Monthly, in US dollars, by card; your bank converts the amount into soles. A visitor from India sees rupees. The first class is free and needs no card.

### Is there a certificate?
