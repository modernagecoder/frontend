Who it suits

Who takes a Python course from Peru?

Mostly three kinds of learner, and each starts in a different place. The language is the same; the examples, the pace and the projects change.

Teenagers in secundaria

A pupil in 3rd, 4th or 5th of secundaria who wants to build things, not just use apps. Peruvian school has no computing subject, so most start from nothing, and that is fine: the teen batch assumes no prior code at all.

University students

Engineering, economics, science or business students who need Python for a course, a thesis or an internship and want it explained slowly and properly, in the language of the documentation they will read for the rest of their career.

Working adults

Accountants, analysts, teachers and managers who spend hours on spreadsheets and copied reports. Python automation is often the first program that pays for itself, and we build it with them line by line.

Which Python

Which version of Python should a course teach in late 2026?

A current one. Python 3.15.0 came out on 9 October 2026, and the CPython core team publishes a public table showing how long each version will be looked after. Learning on a release that has already reached end of life is the most common setup mistake we see.

Source: Python Software Foundation, CPython developer guide, "Status of Python versions" (devguide.python.org/versions), and the release list on python.org/downloads, which shows Python 3.15.0 on 9 October 2026 and 3.15.2 on 25 October 2026; both read on 9 November 2026. The guide marks future end-of-life dates as scheduled and open to adjustment.

What the statuses mean

The developer guide describes five phases. In bugfix, a released version still gets bug and security fixes, with new installers roughly every two months. After two years it moves to security, where only security fixes are accepted and no new installers are built. Five years after release it reaches end of life, and nothing changes again.

So on the day this page was written, two versions, 3.15 and 3.14, were in full bugfix support, and Python 3.10 had stopped receiving any fixes at all on 1 October 2026.

What we do in class

New learners install a current bugfix release from python.org in their first class, with the mentor sharing the screen. If a university lab or an employer needs an older version, we show what differs; for a beginner, almost nothing does.

The habit we teach is more useful than the number: check the official status table before you install anything, and read the release notes in English. Tutorials online age quickly; the table is updated by the people who make the language.

What you learn

What does a complete Python course cover, and in what order?

A complete course is a ladder, and skipping rungs is how people end up copying code they cannot fix. This is the order our Python courses follow, whatever the learner's age; the projects change, the sequence does not.

  1. Values and names. Numbers, text, true and false; storing them in variables; printing them. The first surprise, that 0.1 + 0.2 is not quite 0.3, arrives early, and the lesson further down this page is built on it.
  2. Decisions and repetition. if, elif, else, for and while, practised on small problems the learner can check by hand before running.
  3. Functions. Breaking a problem into named pieces, each with one job and a test the learner writes themselves.
  4. Collections. Lists, dictionaries, tuples and sets, and choosing the right one: counting things, grouping them and looking them up quickly.
  5. Files and errors. Reading and writing text and CSV files, and handling mistakes in the data instead of crashing on them.
  6. Objects. Classes, methods and why programs get organised this way once they grow past a few hundred lines.
  7. Libraries. Installing and using other people's code safely: data tools for analysts, web frameworks for builders, automation tools for office work.
  8. A real project. Something the learner designs, builds, tests and explains in English to their mentor, from a game to a report generator to a small AI model.

Python in Peru

Where does Python count in Peruvian school, olympiads and work?

In more places than the school timetable suggests. Python is not a school subject in Peru, but it is the language of MINEDU's own programming pilot and one of the two languages allowed in the first round of the national informatics olympiad.

At school

The national curriculum, the CNEB, has no computing or programming area in its plan of studies. Digital skills sit in competency 28, which every subject shares and none owns.

MINEDU has started to test Python directly. Its Fábrica de Programadores pilot, announced on 24 July 2025, set out to teach Python to 1,000 pupils in 4th and 5th of secundaria in Lima Norte, with the option of sitting the PCEP exam at the end, and MINEDU describes it as a pilot it hopes to replicate nationally. Most teenagers in Peru will not be in it, which is one reason families look for a course outside school.

In competitions

UTEC runs the Peruvian Informatics Olympiad, OPI, free for every secundaria pupil in the country. Its 2025 rules allow C++ or Python in the online first phase, but only C++ in the in-person final, and they ban generative AI for solving problems.

That makes Python a sound first language for a teenager curious about olympiads, with C++ as the second step for anyone who reaches the final. UTEC and the OPI have no link with us; what we teach is the reasoning, with problems we write ourselves.

At work and at university, Python is the default language of data analysis, scientific computing and most AI tooling, and nearly all of its documentation, error messages and forums are in English. Learning it in English from the first class removes a translation step that Spanish-taught learners meet later, usually at the worst moment, in front of an error they cannot search for.

One practical point. In INEI's household survey for April to June 2026, just 36.4% of homes across Peru owned so much as one computer. A Python course needs a laptop or desktop: a phone can show the video, but typing and running real programs on one is miserable. If the computer is shared, tell us at booking and we will plan class hours around it.

A real lesson

Why does Python say 0.1 added ten times is not 1?

Because computers store most decimals as binary fractions, which are very close to the number you typed but not equal to it. Here is a lesson we teach in the first months of every Python course, for learners of 13 and over.

The task

A learner is building a stopwatch that adds 0.1 of a second at every tick. After ten ticks it should show exactly one second, and the program checks ticks == 1.0 before ringing a bell.

The bell never rings. Version 1 shows why: the total is 0.9999999999999999, so the comparison is False. Then, to make things stranger, it asks Python to round four numbers that end in .5.

What is going on

The number 0.1 has no exact binary form, just as one third has no exact decimal form. Python stores the nearest binary fraction, and the tiny error adds up over ten additions.

The rounding line is a different lesson. Python's documentation says round() goes to the even neighbour when a number sits exactly halfway, so 0.5 rounds to 0, 1.5 to 2, 2.5 to 2 and 3.5 to 4. It is not a bug; it is a deliberate rule that stops rounding errors piling up in one direction.

The fix depends on the job. For a stopwatch, a physics result or anything measured, compare with math.isclose, which accepts a tiny difference. For money and anything else where every digit must be exact, build the numbers with Decimal from text, as in Decimal("0.1"). The last line of Version 2 is the one learners remember: build a Decimal from the float 0.1 instead of the text "0.1", and you get the long tail that the float was hiding all along.

None of this is special to Python. Java, C++ and spreadsheets store decimals the same way. A learner who understands this lesson stops trusting == on decimals in every language, and that one habit prevents a whole family of bugs at university and at work.

  1. Predict first. Before running Version 1, write down what you expect each line to print. Most people get the first two wrong.
  2. Change the step. Try 0.5 and 0.25 instead of 0.1. Both give exactly 1.0 after the right number of ticks. Explain why, using the word "binary".
  3. Find the halfway cases. Print round(x) for 4.5, 5.5 and 6.5, and predict each answer before you run it.
  4. Write it up. In three English sentences, tell a classmate when you would use isclose and when you would use Decimal.

Thinking, not copying

Can I just learn Python with an AI chatbot instead?

You can get code from a chatbot. You cannot get understanding from one without doing the thinking yourself, and the float lesson above shows why: the code ran, and it was still wrong.

Reason first

Learners predict what a program will print before they run it. When the prediction is wrong, that gap is where the learning happens, and we stay in it until the reason is clear.

Tools by age

Claude is for users of 18 and over, and ChatGPT requires a parent's or guardian's permission for anyone under 18. Teenagers in our classes work without adult AI accounts; adults use AI to question their code, not to write it for them.

Explain every line

A program is finished when the learner can explain each line in plain English. That rule applies to code they wrote and to any code a tool suggested.

Peru's own olympiad agrees: the OPI 2025 rules forbid generative AI for solving problems, and a contestant caught using it is removed. The same standard of teaching applies to a teenager in Trujillo, a student in Lima and an adult in Cusco: depth first, and a learner who can work without help when it matters.

Python course for Peru: questions and answers

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. Every class is taught live online in English, and only in English. Mentors speak slowly and clearly, adapt to each learner's accent and pace, and show every step on screen. Python itself is written in English words such as if, for and while, so the vocabulary of the course and of the language is the same.

I have never programmed. Can I start a Python course?

Yes. All three courses at the top of this page start from the first line of code. Teenagers of 13 to 18 join Python for Teens; adults join the zero-to-advanced course or the automation course. The free first class shows you the pace before you decide.

Which version of Python will I learn?

A current Python 3 release in bugfix support, installed from python.org in your first class. In November 2026 that means Python 3.15 or 3.14. Python 3.10 reached end of life on 1 October 2026, so we do not start new learners on it.

How long does it take to learn Python properly?

Our full courses take about nine to twelve months at two live classes a week, plus practice. A motivated adult can move faster one to one. We would rather a learner understands each step than races through a syllabus and forgets it.

When are the classes in Peru time?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India is 10 hours 30 minutes ahead of Peru all year, so 08:00 in Lima is 18:30 in India.

Do I get a certificate?

Yes, our own Modern Age Coders certificate of completion, awarded after the final exam of the course. It is not a Peruvian qualification and no ministry or university issues it. If you also want the Python Institute's PCEP or PCAP exam, our certification course prepares for it; the Institute runs the exam itself.

Does Python help with the OPI informatics olympiad?

It helps you start. The OPI 2025 rules allow Python or C++ in the online first phase, but only C++ in the in-person final. Many teenagers learn to reason in Python first and then move to C++. We are independent of UTEC and the OPI and never promise a result.

Is Python taught in Peruvian schools?

Not as a subject. The national curriculum has no computing area. MINEDU's Fábrica de Programadores pilot, announced in July 2025, teaches Python to 1,000 pupils in 4th and 5th of secundaria in Lima Norte, but most pupils in Peru are outside it.

Can I use ChatGPT or Claude while I learn?

Adults can, carefully, to question their own code. Claude is for users of 18 and over, and ChatGPT needs a parent's or guardian's permission under 18, so teenagers in our classes work without adult AI accounts. Every learner must be able to explain each line they submit.

What do I need at home?

A laptop or desktop computer, a steady internet connection and ideally a headset with a microphone. Python is free to install, and your mentor helps you set it up in the first class. A phone is enough to watch but not to write programs.

How are fees paid from Peru?

By card, in US dollars, once a month. Your bank converts the charge into soles, so the figure on your statement can move a little. The first class is free and does not need card details.
