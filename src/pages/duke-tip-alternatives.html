What happened

Did Duke TIP close, and what replaced it?

Yes. Duke's Office of the Provost states that the pandemic forced TIP's residential summer program to stop in 2020, and that TIP and the Academic Talent Search were permanently closed later that year. Duke's summer offering now continues as Duke Pre-College.

Duke's own account

A notice published by Duke's provost office tells the story. Duke TIP began after a 1980 request from Duke's provost, with a plan "to invite 7th graders to take the SAT" and offer high scorers further opportunities. The first three-week residential program welcomed 151 students in the summer of 1981.

The same notice says: "In 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic forced the suspension of the residential summer program, and TIP and the Academic Talent Search were permanently closed later that year." The old tip.duke.edu address now redirects to Duke's pre-college page.

Duke Pre-College today

Duke Pre-College runs summer programs for students currently in grades 6 to 8 and 9 to 12. Its page says "2027 dates and courses will be announced in late October 2026" and "Registration will open in December 2026", and that it "will not offer online courses for the summer of 2027."

It is a different kind of program from TIP. Duke's notice says that at Pre-College "standardized testing is no longer used as a primary means of assessment", and the program page says "Students must have a 3.0+ GPA" to participate.

Sources: Duke Office of the Provost notice on Duke TIP's founder and Duke Pre-College Programs, read 31 October 2026. Modern Age Coders is not connected with Duke University or any program on this page.

Northwestern CTD tiers

CTD sorts courses into eligibility tiers. Amber courses are open enrollment: "No test scores or portfolio materials needed to enroll." Emerald courses accept a report card plus teacher recommendation, or "90th percentile or above on a nationally normed, standardized grade-level achievement test" such as MAP or STAR.

Magenta asks for the 95th percentile, and the accelerated Indigo tier asks for qualifying ACT or SAT scores, or PSAT 8/9, SCAT or I-Excel taken in grade 5 or 6. CTD says it "accepts test scores or an admission portfolio for most courses".

Stanford and others

Stanford Pre-Collegiate Studies lists SUMaC, which "welcomes a select group of rising high school juniors and seniors" for advanced mathematics, the Stanford Math Circle for after-school math, and University-Level Online Math & Physics with Stanford Continuing Studies credit.

The Belin-Blank Center, which no longer runs above-level testing itself, points families to WKU's Center for Gifted Studies, Northwestern's CTD and the Johns Hopkins Center for Talented Youth.

Above-level testing

What is above-level testing, and why did TIP use it?

Above-level testing means giving a younger student a test written for older students. It was the heart of the old talent searches, including Duke's plan to have 7th graders take the SAT, and some centers still offer it.

In the Belin-Blank Center's words

The Belin-Blank Center explains it simply: "Give a younger student a test that was developed for older students." Bright students often answer almost everything right on a grade-level test, which psychologists call "hitting the ceiling".

A test built for older students has room above, so it shows how far beyond grade level a child really is. That helps a family decide between enrichment, moving up a grade in one subject, or something else.

A ceiling, in numbers (ours)

Imagine two fifth graders who both score 40 out of 40 on a grade 5 math test. The test cannot tell them apart. Now give both a test written for grade 8. One scores 31 of 40 and the other 14 of 40.

Both are strong, but they need different things: the first may be ready to accelerate, while the second may thrive on enrichment at grade level. The numbers are our illustration, not data from any program.

How to choose

How do I choose a gifted program for my child now?

Start from what your child needs, not from the program's name. A short checklist helps, and the steps below are a sensible order for a family starting from scratch. Both are ours.

A criteria checklist for comparing programs (ours)
Question to askWhy it matters
Is it enrichment or acceleration?Enrichment goes deeper at grade level; acceleration moves ahead, sometimes for credit
What does entry require?Open enrollment, a portfolio, grade-level percentiles or above-level scores
Online, weekend or residential?Residential summers suit some children; weekly online classes suit others
How long does it last?A two-week camp and a year of weekly classes give very different results
Who teaches?Look for subject experts and small, active classes
Does it fit the year?Summer programs leave the school year untouched; weekly classes need steady time
  1. Look at what you already have (ours). School report cards and any MAP, STAR or state test percentiles are often enough for open or portfolio-based entry.
  2. Decide on the goal. Is your child bored and needs depth, or racing ahead and needs pace? That choice rules out half the options.
  3. Consider above-level testing if you want to know how far ahead your child is. CTD lists it for grades 2 to 9.
  4. Pick one structured option for the year and one for the summer, rather than five at once.
  5. Review after a term. If your child is still coasting, move up a level; if stressed, step back.

Stretch problems

What kind of math stretches a gifted student?

Problems that reward thinking over calculation. Here is one worked example and a short set, all ours, of the kind a former TIP student would enjoy. Each answer was checked in Python.

The problem (ours)

How many zeros are at the end of 25! (that is 25 x 24 x 23 x ... x 1)? Multiplying it out gives a 26-digit number. There is a far better way.

A zero at the end comes from a factor of 10, which is 2 x 5. There are plenty of 2s among the factors, so count the 5s.

The reasoning

The multiples of 5 up to 25 are 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25: five numbers, each with one 5. But 25 = 5 x 5 has a second 5. So there are 5 + 1 = 6 fives, and 25! ends in 6 zeros.

The same idea for 100!: 100 / 5 = 20 multiples of 5, plus 100 / 25 = 4 multiples of 25 with an extra 5, giving 24 zeros. Counting factors replaced an impossible multiplication.

Duke TIP alternatives: questions answered

Is Duke TIP still running?

No. Duke's Office of the Provost says TIP's residential summer program was suspended in 2020 because of the pandemic, and that TIP and its Academic Talent Search were permanently closed later that year.

What replaced Duke TIP?

Duke now runs Duke Pre-College, summer programs for students currently in grades 6 to 12. It does not use standardized testing as a primary means of assessment, and it asks for a 3.0+ GPA.

What is the best alternative to Duke TIP?

There is no single replacement; it depends on whether your child needs testing, a summer program or year-round classes. Northwestern's CTD offers above-grade-level assessment and tiered courses, while Stanford Pre-Collegiate Studies, WKU and the University of Iowa run their own programs.

Does any program still offer a talent search?

Northwestern's Center for Talent Development lists above-grade-level assessment for grades 2 to 9. The Belin-Blank Center says it is not currently testing and recommends WKU, CTD and Johns Hopkins CTY.

Does my child need test scores for Northwestern CTD?

Not always. CTD's Amber tier is open enrollment with no scores, and its higher tiers accept either scores or an admission portfolio for most courses, according to its eligibility page.

When does Duke Pre-College registration open for 2027?

Duke's page says 2027 dates and courses would be announced in late October 2026 and registration opens in December 2026. It also says there will be no online courses in summer 2027.

What did Duke TIP's talent search do?

According to Duke's provost office, the original plan invited 7th graders to take the SAT and offered high scorers further opportunities, beginning with a residential program in 1981.

Do your classes require a test?

No. We place students after a free first class and a look at recent work. We teach advanced math, but we are not a talent search and award no scores.

When are your classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

Are you connected with Duke or any of these programs?

No. Modern Age Coders is an independent online school with no link to Duke, Northwestern, Stanford, the University of Iowa, WKU or Johns Hopkins. We describe each program only from its own site.
