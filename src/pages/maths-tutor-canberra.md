# Maths tutor in Canberra

Canberra is a city that was drawn before it was built, and its plan is full of geometry. State Circle, the road ringing Parliament House, is close to a perfect circle. Choose any three points on it and a little algebra gives the radius of the one circle passing through all three. Choose them well and you land within a few metres of 470.4 metres, the radius that fits every mapped point most closely. Choose three points bunched together and the answer drifts by more than a hundred metres. It is a small lesson in geometry, and a bigger one in how to measure. ## What is the radius of State Circle?

Through any three points that are not in a line there passes exactly one circle. Its radius, the circumradius, takes a few lines of algebra. We used it on State Circle, and compared it with a fit to every mapped point.

OpenStreetMap draws State Circle as 92 short pieces of road with 430 points between them. A least-squares fit, which finds the circle closest to all the points at once, gives a radius of 470.4 metres; individual points sit between 461.1 and 478.3 metres from its centre. Then we did what a student with a ruler would do: chose three points, found the circle through them, and repeated the exercise with different choices.

**Radius of State Circle by different methods (our calculation from OpenStreetMap)**

| Method | Radius (metres) | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Fit to all 430 points | 470.4 | The reference value |
| Three points about 120 degrees apart, first choice | 471.2 | Close to the fit |
| Three points about 120 degrees apart, second choice | 466.9 | Close to the fit |
| Three points about 120 degrees apart, third choice | 469.1 | Close to the fit |
| Three points about 120 degrees apart, fourth choice | 468.4 | Close to the fit |
| Three points within a 40 degree arc | 619.4 | Far too large |

### Spread wins

Three points spread around the circle gave radii from 466.9 to 471.2 metres, within a few metres of the full fit. For this road, the simple three-point method is as good as fitting hundreds of points.

### Bunching fails

Three points on a short arc nearly lie on a straight line, and a straight line is a circle of infinite radius. Small bumps in the mapped road then swing the answer to 619.4 metres.

### Why the length doubles

Adding every mapped piece gives 5930.3 metres, about twice 2&pi;r = 2955.5 metres, because the map draws each direction of traffic as its own line.

### What students practise

Perpendicular bisectors, the circumcircle, distance between points, circumference, and the idea that a measurement method can be sound or fragile depending on how it is used.

High school students construct the circle with a compass on a printed map; college students derive the circumradius algebraically.

### Honest limits

The map shows road centrelines, not a surveyed circle, so the radius describes the mapped road. We make no claim about how the road was designed.

Map data: OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL.

**Three ways to find the centre of a circle through three points**

| Approach | How it works | Year level |
|---|---|---|
| Compass and ruler | Draw the perpendicular bisector of two chords; they cross at the centre | Years 7 to 9 |
| Coordinates | Write each bisector as a line equation and solve the pair simultaneously | Years 9 and 10 |
| Formula | Use the circumradius formula: product of the three sides over four times the area | Years 11 and 12 |

All three give the same centre when the arithmetic is exact. The compass version is the one most students remember, because they can see why it works: every point on a chord's perpendicular bisector is the same distance from both ends of the chord, so the point on two such bisectors is the same distance from all three points. Once that idea is secure, the algebra stops being a recipe and becomes a description of a picture the student already understands.

Data: [OpenStreetMap](https://www.openstreetmap.org/copyright), State Circle ways, one Overpass query on 13 October 2026; fits and circumradii are our calculation.
