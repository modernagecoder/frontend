# BC Grade 10 Numeracy Assessment: format and practice

Every BC student meets the Grade 10 Numeracy Assessment, and it surprises many of them. It is not a math test on one course. It sets everyday situations, asks students to decide what matters, use math they learned years ago, and then explain and defend an answer in writing. This page explains the assessment from the Ministry&rsquo;s own page and documents, shows a full written response to a task we wrote, and gives fourteen practice questions with worked answers. Everything in our practice sections is our practice, not Ministry questions.

At a glance: Who: BC students, in their Grade 10 year; Status: A graduation requirement; Delivery: Online, three sections; Time: 2 hours, up to 1 more if needed; Written part: Two questions, on paper; Results: A four-level proficiency scale; Our help: A batch, or one to one; Clock: Pacific time, UTC-7 all year. ### Where it sits in graduation

The Ministry&rsquo;s 2019 specifications put it plainly: &ldquo;Along with acquiring 80 course credits for graduation, students are required to complete provincial assessments in numeracy at Grade 10 and in literacy at Grades 10 and 12.&rdquo; Schools register and run the session; families do not book it themselves.

Students in an online learning school still write at a physical location. The administration guide says online learning schools &ldquo;must assign a physical writing location&rdquo;, called an assessment centre, to students who need one.

### Retakes and results

The Ministry&rsquo;s parent brochure (December 2018) says students can re-take the assessment to improve, and &ldquo;Their best level achieved will be counted as their final result.&rdquo; The 2026-2027 guide adds that a disqualification &ldquo;will count as an attempt&rdquo;.

Results use a four-level proficiency scale: Emerging, Developing, Proficient and Extending, according to the 2019 specifications. On admissions, the brochure says &ldquo;Each post-secondary institution has the authority to determine admission requirements for students.&rdquo;

## Interpret, apply, solve, analyze, communicate

The assessment page says students solve problems &ldquo;by using five numeracy processes (different ways of thinking and working): interpret, apply, solve, analyze and communicate.&rdquo; The specifications define each one. Knowing them is the single most useful preparation for the written questions.

**The five numeracy processes, quoted from the Grade 10 Graduation Numeracy Assessment Specifications (July 2019)**

| Process | From the specifications | What to write on the paper (ours) |
|---|---|---|
| Interpret | &ldquo;Given insufficient or excess information, students will need to decide what information is relevant to solve the problem.&rdquo; | List the facts you will use, the ones you will ignore, and any assumption |
| Apply | Translating &ldquo;real-world problems into mathematical problems (mathematizing)&rdquo; | Name the math tool: a rate, a ratio, an area, a table, an equation |
| Solve | &ldquo;Students may also need to check mathematical solutions to determine if their solutions make mathematical sense.&rdquo; | Show every calculation with units, then a quick check |
| Analyze | Assessing &ldquo;the practicality and possible limitations of solutions&rdquo; | Say whether the answer is sensible and what could change it |
| Communicate | &ldquo;construct valid logical arguments to defend their decisions and assumptions&rdquo; | Finish with a clear recommendation in a full sentence |

### The definition behind it

The specifications define the skill being measured: &ldquo;Numeracy is the ability to interpret information within a given situation, apply mathematical understanding to solve an identified problem, and to analyze and communicate a solution.&rdquo; That is the five processes in one sentence.

They also describe the cycle the processes form: a situation becomes a real-world problem, then a math problem, then a math solution, which is analyzed in context and finally communicated. If the solution does not make sense, the student goes round again.

### Why students lose marks

Most students are comfortable with solve, the middle step. The marks they lose are usually at the ends: they skip interpret (and use the wrong numbers), or skip analyze and communicate (and write a number with no recommendation). The rubric below rewards all five.

Our habit for every written question: five short labelled headings, one per process. It takes a minute and makes a marker&rsquo;s job easy.

## The four task types and the math they draw on

The 2019 specifications describe four kinds of task, one of each in an assessment, set in everyday contexts. They also say which math concepts the tasks use, and most of it comes from before Grade 9.

**Task categories from the Grade 10 Graduation Numeracy Assessment Specifications (July 2019)**

| Task type | What it asks, in the specifications&rsquo; words |
|---|---|
| Reasoned Estimates | &ldquo;make or use estimates across multiple variables in order to build a logical argument for a possible solution&rdquo; |
| Plan and Design | &ldquo;analyze time, space, cost, and people in order to make a recommendation&rdquo; |
| Fair Share | Decide how to &ldquo;share something fairly&rdquo; |
| Model | &ldquo;come up with a model or strategy, given a data set; and then to apply this model or strategy to a new data set&rdquo; |

### The math involved

The specifications say: &ldquo;Most of the numeracy tasks will include concepts up to and including Grade 8&rdquo;, across number sense, patterns, geometry and measurement, data and probability, and financial literacy. From Grades 9 and 10 the assessment may also use operations with rational numbers, linear relations, spatial proportional reasoning, statistics in society, experimental probability and simple financial literacy.

So a student who struggles with Grade 10 algebra can still do well, and a strong Grade 10 math student can still be caught out by a rusty Grade 7 skill such as unit conversion or percentages.

### Contexts and difficulty

Tasks are set in four contexts: &ldquo;personal, career, societal, and scientific&rdquo;. Questions are written to the first three levels of Webb&rsquo;s Depth of Knowledge, from recall to strategic thinking; the specifications note that &ldquo;Level 4 cognitive rigour is not assessed.&rdquo;

The Ministry&rsquo;s official Reference Pages give formulas under three headings, geometry, rates of change and measurements, including the Pythagorean theorem, y = mx + b and conversions such as &ldquo;1 inch = 2.54 cm&rdquo;. Practise with them open, since that is how the assessment works.

## The marking rubric for the student-choice questions

The two paper questions are marked with a published rubric (revised September 2023). Reading it is the fastest way to understand what a strong answer looks like.

**Marking rubric snapshot for the student-choice question, quoted from the Ministry&rsquo;s Marking Rubric (Rev. 09/2023)**

| Score | Snapshot, in the rubric&rsquo;s words |
|---|---|
| 4 | &ldquo;Solution is effective and comprehensive&rdquo;; &ldquo;Reasoning is clearly communicated.&rdquo; |
| 3 | &ldquo;Solution is reasonable; calculations may contain errors. Reasoning can be followed.&rdquo; |
| 2 | &ldquo;Solution is incomplete but on the right track&rdquo;; &ldquo;Reasoning may be unclear or inconsistent.&rdquo; |
| 1 | &ldquo;Solution contains an ineffective approach and/or fundamental mathematical errors.&rdquo; |
| 0 | Includes &ldquo;Information simply recopied from the problem.&rdquo; and &ldquo;Any answer without supporting work.&rdquo; |

### Two lines worth remembering

A bare answer scores 0: &ldquo;Any answer without supporting work.&rdquo; Even a correct number earns nothing without the work behind it. That single line is why the written questions reward students who show reasoning.

The rubric also says &ldquo;Errors in transcription do not take away from the level of proficiency.&rdquo; Copying a number wrongly from the screen is treated more kindly than a missing explanation.

### What separates a 3 from a 4

At level 3 calculations &ldquo;may contain errors&rdquo; and reasoning &ldquo;can be followed&rdquo;. At level 4 the solution is comprehensive and reasoning is clearly communicated. The difference is mostly analyze and communicate: limitations named, assumptions stated, a recommendation made.

The Ministry publishes a Marking Guide and Student Exemplars (2024) on the same page. Comparing a level 3 and a level 4 exemplar side by side teaches more than any summary.

## A Plan and Design task, answered with all five processes

This task is ours, written in the style the specifications describe. It is our practice, not a Ministry question. The response below is laid out the way we teach students to answer the paper questions.

The task. A Grade 10 class of 46 students and 4 adults is going to a science centre that opens at 9:30 am. The school can book vans that seat 8 passengers plus a driver, and every van needs one adult as driver. The drive takes 50 minutes. Loading takes 15 minutes, and the teacher wants a 10-minute buffer for traffic. The adults are the only drivers. How should the trip be organized, and what time should everyone gather?

1. Interpret. Relevant facts: 46 students, 4 adults, 8 passengers per van plus a driver, adults must drive, 50 minutes of driving, 15 minutes loading, 10 minutes buffer, arrival by 9:30 am. Assumption: all vans leave together, and a driver cannot also count as one of the 8 passengers.
2. Apply. Two calculations: how many vans the students need (students divided by seats, rounded up), and a backward timeline from 9:30 am.
3. Solve. 46 &divide; 8 = 5.75, so 6 vans are needed for the students. But there are only 4 adults, so only 4 vans can be driven, carrying 4 &times; 8 = 32 students. That leaves 14 students without a seat. Timeline: 9:30 minus a 10-minute buffer is 9:20; minus 50 minutes driving is 8:30 departure; minus 15 minutes loading is 8:15 gathering.
4. Analyze. The plan as given does not work: the class is two vans short, because there are two drivers too few. Options: recruit 2 more adult drivers (6 vans), run two trips (but a second round adds about 100 minutes of driving, so the second group arrives near 11:10 am), or use a larger vehicle such as a school bus. A second trip wastes most of the visit for 14 students, so it is the weakest option.
5. Communicate. Recommendation: book 6 vans and find 2 more adult volunteers who can drive, so that all 46 students travel together. Everyone gathers at 8:15 am, vans leave at 8:30 am and arrive by 9:20 am, with 10 minutes to spare. If no extra drivers can be found, book a school bus instead of vans.

**The backward timeline from the worked task (ours)**

| Step | Minutes | Clock time |
|---|---|---|
| Arrive (latest) |  | 9:30 am |
| Traffic buffer | 10 | 9:20 am |
| Drive | 50 | 8:30 am departure |
| Loading | 15 | 8:15 am gathering |

### Why this answer would score well

It uses only the relevant facts, states its assumption, shows every calculation, and, most importantly, spots that the plan as described cannot work. Then it compares options with reasons and ends with a clear recommendation. That is analyze and communicate doing their job.

A student who stopped at &ldquo;6 vans, gather at 8:15&rdquo; would have a reasonable calculation but would miss the driver shortage, which is the real point of the task.

### The math is Grade 7 math

Division with rounding up, multiplication and subtracting time: nothing here goes beyond the middle grades. That is typical, and it matches the specifications&rsquo; note that most tasks use concepts up to Grade 8.

What makes it hard is the reading: noticing that drivers are a constraint. Interpret is where the marks start.

## Fourteen numeracy practice questions

These fourteen questions are our practice, not Ministry questions; we wrote them for this page in the four task styles the specifications describe. For the official samples, use Sample A and Sample B on the Ministry&rsquo;s page. Keep a calculator and the Reference Pages beside you, as in the real assessment.

1. Reasoned estimate. About how many basketballs (diameter 24 cm) would fill a classroom 9 m long, 7 m wide and 3 m high? State your assumptions.
2. Measurement. A trail is 12 km long. Hiking at 4 km/h, how long does it take? Give the distance in metres too.
3. Rate. A cyclist rides 18 km in 45 minutes. What is the average speed in km/h?
4. Pythagorean theorem. A 5 m ladder leans on a wall with its foot 1.4 m from the wall. How high up the wall does it reach?
5. Plan and design: area. A 6 m by 4 m garden has a 1 m wide path all the way round it. What is the area of the path?
6. Plan and design: volume. A cylindrical rain barrel has radius 0.3 m and height 0.9 m. How many litres does it hold? (1 m3 = 1000 L.)
7. Fair share. Three volunteers worked 4, 6 and 10 hours on a planting day. The club wants to share 60 seedlings in proportion to hours worked. How many does each get?
8. Fair share, reasoning. Someone says the 60 seedlings should be split equally, 20 each. Give one argument for and one against.
9. Linear relation. A 30 cm candle burns down 2 cm every hour. Write a rule for its height h after t hours. When is it 12 cm tall?
10. Model. A seedling measured each week was 5, 8, 11, 14 and 17 cm tall in weeks 0 to 4. Find a rule and predict week 10. What is one limitation of your prediction?
11. Statistics. A bus was late by 2, 0, 5, 1, 12, 3 and 2 minutes over seven days. Find the mean and the median. Which better describes a typical day, and why?
12. Experimental probability. A spinner with three equal sections landed on red 18 times in 50 spins. What is the experimental probability of red? How does it compare with the theoretical probability?
13. Scale. On a map with scale 1:50 000, two lakes are 7.2 cm apart. How far apart are they in kilometres?
14. Communicate. Using your answer to question 10, write a two-sentence recommendation for when to move the seedling to a bigger pot, if the pot holds plants up to 30 cm tall.

## Worked answers to the fourteen questions

Each answer shows the numeracy process behind it, not just the number. The arithmetic was checked in Python 3.13.

1. About 14,000. Room volume 9 &times; 7 &times; 3 = 189 m3. If each ball takes up a 0.24 m cube when stacked in rows, that is 0.243 &asymp; 0.0138 m3, so 189 &divide; 0.0138 &asymp; 13,700. Assumptions: an empty room and neat stacking. A different packing gives a different number, so a range such as 13,000 to 17,000 is honest. Estimates are judged on their reasoning.
2. 3 hours; 12,000 m. Time = distance &divide; speed = 12 &divide; 4. And 1 km = 1000 m.
3. 24 km/h. 45 minutes is 0.75 h, and 18 &divide; 0.75 = 24. Converting minutes to hours first avoids the common error of dividing by 45.
4. 4.8 m. 52 = 1.42 + h2, so h2 = 25 &minus; 1.96 = 23.04 and h = 4.8.
5. 24 m2. With the path the outer rectangle is 8 m by 6 m = 48 m2. Subtract the garden, 24 m2. A common error is adding 1 m, not 2 m, to each side length.
6. About 254 L. V = &pi;r2h = &pi; &times; 0.09 &times; 0.9 &asymp; 0.2545 m3, and 0.2545 &times; 1000 &asymp; 254 L.
7. 12, 18 and 30. Total hours 20, so each hour earns 60 &divide; 20 = 3 seedlings: 4 &times; 3, 6 &times; 3, 10 &times; 3. Check: 12 + 18 + 30 = 60.
8. For: everyone volunteered, and a garden may need the same number of plants in each plot. Against: the person who worked 10 hours did more than twice the work of the person who worked 4. Fair Share tasks reward a stated definition of fair, not one right answer.
9. h = 30 &minus; 2t; after 9 hours. Set 30 &minus; 2t = 12, so 2t = 18. The rate of change is &minus;2 cm per hour, the slope in y = mx + b form.
10. h = 5 + 3w; 35 cm at week 10. The height rises by 3 cm every week from 5 cm. Limitation: plants do not grow at a steady rate forever, and pot size or light may slow growth, so predicting far beyond the data is risky.
11. Mean about 3.6 minutes; median 2 minutes. The total is 25 minutes over 7 days. The 12-minute day pulls the mean up; the median is closer to a typical day, so it describes the bus better.
12. 0.36, against a theoretical 1/3 &asymp; 0.33. 18 &divide; 50 = 0.36. The values are close; with only 50 spins some difference is expected, and more spins would usually bring the experimental value nearer to one third.
13. 3.6 km. 7.2 cm &times; 50 000 = 360 000 cm = 3600 m = 3.6 km.
14. One good answer: &ldquo;Using our rule of 3 cm of growth per week, the seedling reaches 30 cm around week 8 or 9, so it should move to a bigger pot by week 8. Because growth may slow, measure it each week and move it sooner if it gets close to 30 cm.&rdquo; A recommendation, a reason from the model, and a limitation.

Questions 9 and 10 use linear relations, one of the Grade 9 and 10 concepts the specifications list. Students who also take coding find the Model task familiar: a rule fitted to data and tested on new values is what a short program does. Our [BC Computer Programming 11 and 12 guide](/computer-programming-11-and-12-bc) shows where that leads.

## A four-week plan for the Numeracy Assessment

The assessment rewards habits more than memorized content. This plan of ours builds them in four weeks; confirm the session date with the school first.

1. Confirm the session with the school. The school registers students and runs the assessment, so ask when your teen is scheduled.
2. Do Sample A online, untimed. Learn the navigation, the question types and where the two paper questions fall.
3. Read the five process definitions. Then label each step of your Sample A written answers with the process it shows.
4. Refresh the middle-grade toolkit. Unit conversions, percentages, ratios and rates, area and volume, mean and median. Short daily sets are enough.
5. Practise with the Reference Pages open. Know where each formula is, so you do not waste time searching.
6. Write one paper response a day. Five labelled headings, every calculation with units, a recommendation at the end. Use our worked task as a model.
7. Mark your own answer with the rubric. Decide which level it reaches and what would lift it one level.
8. Study the official exemplars. Compare a level 3 and a level 4 response from the Ministry&rsquo;s 2024 guide.
9. Do Sample B timed. Two hours, calculator allowed, dark pencil for the paper questions.
10. The day before, rest. Review your rubric notes, pack two dark pencils and your own calculator if you prefer it to the online one.

| Week | Focus | Done when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sample A untimed; the five processes | Every Sample A step labelled with a process |
| 2 | Middle-grade toolkit; Reference Pages | Ten mixed skill questions correct with the pages open |
| 3 | One written response a day, self-marked | Five responses written and scored with the rubric |
| 4 | Exemplars, then Sample B timed | Sample B done in 2 hours and reviewed |

## The Grade 10 Numeracy Assessment: common questions

### What is the BC Grade 10 Numeracy Assessment?

It is a provincial assessment of numeracy and a graduation requirement in BC, taken in the Grade 10 year. It focuses on applying math learned across subjects from Kindergarten to Grade 10, using five processes: interpret, apply, solve, analyze and communicate.

### What is the format?

It is delivered online in three sections. In the common section students answer 24 computer-scored questions on four situations. In the student-choice section they choose two of four questions and write two responses on paper, marked by BC educators. A final self-reflection section is not scored.

### How long is the Numeracy Assessment?

The 2026-2027 administration guide gives 2 hours, and students may take up to 1 additional hour if needed.

### Can I use a calculator?

Yes. The administration guide says the online assessment includes an online calculator and students may also bring their own. Phones, tablets and similar handheld devices may not be used as calculators. Check the Ministry's calculator policy with your school.

### What math is on it?

The 2019 specifications say most tasks use concepts up to Grade 8, such as number sense, patterns, geometry and measurement, data and probability, and financial literacy. Some Grade 9 or 10 concepts may appear, including linear relations, proportional reasoning, statistics and experimental probability.

### How are the written questions marked?

With a published rubric that scores responses from 0 to 4. An answer without supporting work scores 0. Higher scores need a complete, reasonable solution with clearly communicated reasoning. The Ministry also publishes a marking guide with student exemplars.

### Can my teen rewrite the assessment?

The Ministry's 2018 parent brochure says students can re-take it to improve their proficiency, and that the highest level reached counts as the final result. Ask the school about upcoming sessions.

### Are your practice questions from the real assessment?

No. Every question on this page is our practice, not Ministry questions. For official practice, use Sample A and Sample B, their answer keys and the Reference Pages on the Ministry's Grade 10 Numeracy Assessment page.

### How can my teen prepare?

Try Sample A online, learn the five processes, refresh middle-grade skills such as rates, ratios and conversions, write one structured response a day, mark it with the rubric, study the official exemplars and do Sample B timed.

### When are classes for BC families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Because BC stopped changing its clocks in 2026, the lesson you book in October is at the same BC time in December.

### What does numeracy help cost?

Your first class is free. Ongoing help is billed once a month for eight live sessions, without a term contract, and ends whenever you choose at a month end. The fees section lists today's figures.
