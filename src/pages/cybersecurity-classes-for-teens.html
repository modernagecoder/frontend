Cybersecurity classes for teens

Teenagers who get curious about cybersecurity often start from one of two places: a scam message that nearly fooled someone in the family, or a friend who mentions a school cyber team. Both are good starting points. Security is one of the few areas of computing where a 14-year-old can do real, useful work early, as long as the learning is defensive and stays on systems built for practice. This page explains the American programs teens hear about, what each one actually involves according to its own website, how to begin safely at home, and how our live classes fit around them.

Which one first?

A teen who likes teamwork and deadlines will enjoy CyberPatriot, but it depends on a school or organization fielding a team. A teen who likes working alone at their own pace can start on CyLab Security Academy tonight.

Between registration windows

Registration for the 2026 to 2027 season ran June 2 to October 1, 2026; uscyberpatriot.org publishes each new window. The months before a season are the useful ones for learning Windows and Linux settings, networking basics and careful note-taking.

Ask the school early. Because teams need an adult coach from a participating organization, a teen often has to find a teacher willing to start one.

What CyberPatriot rewards

Teams are scored on how secure they make a system, so the work is defensive by design. In general terms, hardening a computer means reviewing accounts and settings and keeping software current; that is our summary, not CyberPatriot’s scoring list.

Methodical work, careful notes and teamwork matter in any team competition, and our course builds them in its practice rounds. We teach the concepts; competition practice belongs to the team and its coach.

What a CTF is

CTF stands for Capture the Flag. Each challenge hides a short piece of text, the flag, that you find by reading, reasoning and testing. Hints come one at a time on CyLab’s platform.

In our experience, General Skills and Cryptography are the gentlest places for a beginner. CyLab describes the first as the terminal, scripting and quick file work, and the second as codes, ciphers and locked data; old ciphers can be solved with a pencil.

The rule that never bends

A CTF platform is a playground built to be broken. A school website, a friend’s account or a game server is not. The same curiosity used on a system without permission stops being practice.

Our rule in class is short: only test what you own, or what was built for practice and says so. We teach that rule before anything technical.

CyLab Security Academy also publishes research. Its own announcement describes a white paper on competition data and AI use, which is worth reading with a teen who is tempted to let a chatbot solve the puzzles. Whatever the platform’s age rules are for accounts, check them with your teen when signing up; they were not on the pages we could read.

Start at home

A safe first practice set, and a little code

October is Cybersecurity Awareness Month, and CISA, the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, says everyone should take four core steps. They make a good first week.

  1. Avoid and report phishing scams. CISA’s first step. Our practice table below trains the eye.
  2. Use strong passwords. Long and random beats short and clever; the arithmetic further down shows why.
  3. Use multifactor authentication and a password manager. A second factor protects an account even after a password leaks.
  4. Update software. Updates close known holes, and keeping systems current is part of everyday defensive work.

The messageThe red flagThe safe move

A game friend offers free in-game currency if you share your passwordAnyone asking for a passwordNever share it; report the account
An email from your "bank" with a misspelled sender addressA look-alike addressDo not reply; a parent checks through the official app
A text saying a package could not be delivered, with a short linkAn unexpected delivery and a linkCheck the order on the shop’s own site
A pop-up saying your laptop is infected, with a phone number to callA scare plus a phone numberClose the tab; tell a parent or school IT

SecretCalculationPossibilities

What the numbers teach

The script on its own is weak. Twelve words give only 20,736 four-word passphrases, fewer than a teen might guess. The strength comes from the size of the list and the randomness of each pick, not from the word count.

With a 7,776-word list, four random words give more possibilities than eight random letters and digits, and are far easier to remember. That trade-off is the whole lesson.

What the numbers do not say

These counts only hold when every choice is truly random. A passphrase a person picks, such as a favorite team and a birth year, is far weaker than its length suggests.

We do not print crack times. They depend on how a site stores passwords, which is why hashing is the next lesson in our course, and why a password manager plus a second factor matter more than any single clever password.

From user to defender

Four stages of a teen in cybersecurity

Our description of the stages a teen moves through in this subject.

Stages of a young defender (ours)
StageWhat it looks like
1. Careful userSpots phishing, uses a password manager, turns on two-factor sign-in
2. ExplainerCan explain hashing, encryption and why updates matter to a parent
3. PractitionerSolves beginner CTF challenges and writes up how
4. Team defenderHardens a system methodically, documents it, works in a team

For parents

Stage 1 protects the whole family. Ask your teen to set up two-factor sign-in on your accounts; it is a real job and a confidence boost.

If your teen talks about hacking, ask what they practiced on. A clear answer naming a practice platform is the right one.

For teens

Write up every challenge you solve, even easy ones. Writeups are how security people show what they know.

Never test a system you do not own or have permission to test. Practice platforms exist so you never need to.

Cybersecurity for teens: questions

What should a cybersecurity class for teens teach?

Defense: how the internet works, passwords and hashing, two-factor sign-in, phishing, malware concepts, updates, encryption and practice puzzles on systems built to be tested. It should never teach attacks on real systems.

Does my teen need to know coding first?

No. Our course starts with no coding. Python helps later, especially for capture-the-flag puzzles, so many teens add our Python course after the first few months.

What is CyberPatriot?

The National Youth Cyber Education Program created by the Air and Space Forces Association. Its competition has teams of two to six middle or high school students secure virtual Windows and Linux systems, with an adult coach.

When can my teen join CyberPatriot?

Teams register through a school or approved organization. For the 2026 to 2027 season, CyberPatriot listed team registration as June 2 to October 1, 2026; check uscyberpatriot.org for the next registration window.

What happened to picoCTF?

The picoCTF website says picoCTF has joined CyLab Security Academy, Carnegie Mellon University's free cybersecurity learning platform. Logins created before May 8, 2026 still work there.

Is there an AP Cybersecurity course?

Yes. College Board lists AP Cybersecurity as launching in fall 2026, with the first exams in May 2027. Whether a student can take it depends on whether their high school offers it.

Is learning hacking legal for teens?

Practicing on platforms built for it is legal; CyLab says its challenges are built to be hacked so the practice is legal. Testing a real system without permission is not. We teach defense and only use practice environments.

What can my teen do at home this week?

Start with CISA's four core steps: avoid and report phishing, use strong passwords, use multifactor authentication with a password manager, and update software. The practice set on this page is a good first exercise.

What times are classes for US families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible. Choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

How much do cybersecurity classes cost?

The first lesson is free. After that, classes are paid one month at a time, and the fees section on this page shows the monthly figure in US dollars.
