## Where coding sits for a Vancouver child: the ADST modules in Grades 6 and 7

British Columbia teaches coding mainly through Applied Design, Skills, and Technologies (ADST). We read the Grade 6 and 7 core page on the province&rsquo;s curriculum site on 22 October 2026. Quoted words are the province&rsquo;s; the commentary is ours.

**BC ADST 6-7: the module rule and the Computational Thinking module, quoted from curriculum.gov.bc.ca (read 22 October 2026)**

| What BC says | What it means for your child |
|---|---|
| Students will experience &ldquo;a minimum of three modules&rdquo; of ADST 6-7 in each of Grades 6 and 7 | Three modules a year, but which three is up to the school |
| Schools may choose from the listed modules or &ldquo;develop new modules&rdquo; | One Vancouver classroom may do Computational Thinking while another does Drafting or Digital Literacy |
| Computational Thinking content: simple algorithms &ldquo;for sorting, searching, sequence, selection, and repetition&rdquo; | The five building blocks of most beginner programs, named outright |
| The same module lists &ldquo;cryptography and code breaking&rdquo;, with cyphers as the example | Secret codes are on the official list, which children love |
| Visual programming tools named as examples: Kodu and Scratch | Scratch experience before Grade 6 is a real head start |

### Not guaranteed

Because the school picks the modules, a Vancouver child can finish Grade 7 without ever meeting the Computational Thinking one. A class outside school is often the only steady place a child codes every week.

### Before Grade 6

BC&rsquo;s module rule begins in Grade 6, so younger children rely on what their teacher chooses to do. Ages 6 to 10 are the years when a weekly class adds the most.

Children like code-breaking, so here is a puzzle we use, written by us. A shift cipher moves each letter a few places along the alphabet. This short Python program moves every letter of a secret word back by three places and prints the answer, hello. It uses only lower-case letters, which keeps the arithmetic simple for a Grade 6 child:

```
message = "khoor"
key = 3
plain = ""
for ch in message:
    plain = plain + chr((ord(ch) - 97 - key) % 26 + 97)
print(plain)
```

The province names cyphers; turning them into a first Python project is our choice. The next challenge is to write the encoder, then swap programs with a friend.

## Six checks that only make sense for a Vancouver family

Our checks, for parents of a child aged 6 to 12 in Vancouver. General advice on class size and fees applies everywhere; these six add what is specific to British Columbia and to a large city.

### 1. Does the teacher know ADST?

Ask what your child will meet in the Grade 6 and 7 modules. A teacher who can name the Computational Thinking module, and knows the school may not offer it, can plan around the gap.

### 2. Is Scratch a step, not the end?

BC names Scratch and Kodu as examples. Scratch is a fine start, but by about age 10 a child who is ready should be moving into typed code. Ask when and how that move happens.

### 3. Has the provider noticed the clock?

BC moved to permanent Pacific time in 2026 and will not turn its clocks back on 1 November 2026. An online provider in another province or country should state Vancouver times in Pacific time, with no autumn shift.

### 4. What does the commute cost?

For a class in a room across the city, add travel both ways and the wait in between. For an online class, the cost is a quiet corner and a laptop. Either can be right; compare them honestly.

### 5. Year-round or a camp?

Summer and spring-break camps are a good taste. Steady progress needs a weekly class with the same teacher, so ask what happens after the camp ends.

### 6. Is the child asked why?

In any trial, count how often the teacher asks your child to explain. Sorting, searching and selection only stick when the child can say what the code is doing and why.

### What we would not use to choose

Certificates, badges and a long list of languages tell you little about a six-to-twelve-year-old&rsquo;s lesson. One finished project your child can explain tells you a great deal.

## Which Vancouver neighbourhoods have the most parks with a playground?

The City of Vancouver publishes its parks and the facilities in each park as two open data files. Our older children, about 10 to 12, join them in Python. The first surprise comes before any counting. The figures are our counts, not the City&rsquo;s.

The trap. Both files separate columns with semicolons, not commas. Open one with Python&rsquo;s csv module in the usual way and it finds a single column whose name is the entire header line, parkid;name;official;..., so every later step fails. This is the four-line check that reveals it:

```
import csv
with open("parks.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as f:
    reader = csv.DictReader(f)
    print(reader.fieldnames)
```

Adding delimiter=";" fixes it. Children who meet this once never forget to look at a file before trusting it.

**Parks with at least one Playgrounds row, by neighbourhood: our count from the City of Vancouver Parks and Parks facilities files, downloaded 22 October 2026 (top seven of 21)**

| Neighbourhood (as named in the file) | Parks with a playground |
|---|---|
| Renfrew-Collingwood | 12 |
| Kensington-Cedar Cottage | 11 |
| Hastings-Sunrise | 9 |
| Grandview-Woodland | 8 |
| Mount Pleasant | 8 |
| Downtown | 7 |
| Marpole | 7 |

Our script joins the two files on parkid. It first collects the IDs of parks with a Playgrounds row, then walks the parks file and counts each matching park once by neighbourhood:

```
import csv

playground_parks = set()
with open("parks-facilities.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f, delimiter=";"):
        if row["facilitytype"] == "Playgrounds":
            playground_parks.add(row["parkid"])

by_area = {}
with_washroom = 0
with open("parks.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f, delimiter=";"):
        if row["parkid"] in playground_parks:
            area = row["neighbourhoodname"]
            by_area[area] = by_area.get(area, 0) + 1
            if row["washrooms"] == "Y":
                with_washroom += 1

print(len(playground_parks), "parks list a playground")
print(with_washroom, "of them list a washroom")
for area in sorted(by_area, key=by_area.get, reverse=True)[:5]:
    print(area, by_area[area])
```

It reports 114 parks with a playground, 52 of which also list a washroom, and the five neighbourhoods at the top of the table above.

### Parks, not swings

The facilities file has a facilitycount column, and a few parks list two, three or four playgrounds. We count parks, not playgrounds, so a park is counted once however many it lists, and we never add the counts into a city total.

### One neighbourhood with none

The parks file uses 22 neighbourhood names. 21 have at least one park with a playground row; Shaughnessy has none in this file. That is a fact about the file, not a claim about where children play.

### A useful second question

Parents of young children often care about washrooms. Only 52 of the 114 playground parks list one, so a filter on two columns at once answers a real family question.

### Two numbers that disagree

The City&rsquo;s own description says the Park Board maintains over 220 parks, while the parks file we downloaded has 218 rows. Both can be true: a description is written once, and the facilities extract is updated weekly. Students learn to report the number they computed and the date they downloaded it.

## Should a Vancouver child learn to code when AI can write the parks script?

An AI tool can write it. Knowing that it is wrong takes a person who has written one.

Ask an AI assistant to count Vancouver playgrounds and it may forget the semicolon delimiter, or add up the facilitycount column and call the total a number of parks. Both mistakes produce a confident answer. A twelve-year-old who has hit the delimiter trap personally notices immediately, because they know what the file looks like inside.

Our classes for children are built on thinking first: look at the data, predict, write, test, explain. Children learn how AI tools work and where they fail, and they practise checking answers, but they do their own reasoning. Outside class, OpenAI&rsquo;s rules say a parent must give permission before anyone younger than 18 uses ChatGPT.

BC lists sorting, searching, selection and repetition for its Grade 6 and 7 module because those are ways of thinking; a tool that writes the code does not do the thinking for the child. The longer argument is in [is coding still worth learning in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
