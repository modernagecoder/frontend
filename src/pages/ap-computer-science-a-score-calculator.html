AP Computer Science A score calculator

Students who search for an AP CSA score calculator usually have a practice test in front of them and one question: is this a 3, a 4 or a 5? College Board publishes enough to answer part of that honestly. It tells you how many questions each section has, what each section is worth and how many points each free-response question carries. It does not publish the raw score you need for each AP score. So this page does two things, and keeps them apart: it builds a composite out of 100 from the published weights, which is exact arithmetic, and then it explains what that composite can and cannot tell you about May.

In short

An AP Computer Science A estimate starts from the published weights: 42 multiple-choice questions are 55% of the score and four free-response questions, worth 7, 7, 5 and 6 points, are 45%. Our composite is MC right / 42 × 55 plus FRQ points / 25 × 45. College Board does not publish the raw cutoffs for a 3, 4 or 5, so any score from a calculator is an estimate.

Reading the table

The free-response questions carry 7, 7, 5 and 6 points, which is 25 points in all by our own addition. So the 45% of Section II is spread over 25 rubric points, while the 55% of Section I is spread over 42 questions.

The whole exam runs in the Bluebook app. AP Central says plainly: "This is a fully digital exam." Responses are submitted automatically at the end, and the Java Quick Reference is built into Bluebook.

What the multiple choice covers

The Course and Exam Description gives approximate multiple-choice weights by unit: Unit 1, Using Objects and Methods, 15 to 25 percent; Unit 2, Selection and Iteration, 25 to 35 percent; Unit 3, Class Creation, 10 to 18 percent; Unit 4, Data Collections, 30 to 40 percent.

Those are ranges for Section I only. They are useful for choosing what to revise, and they are a warning against treating Unit 3 as the heart of the exam just because it feels the hardest in class.

Our estimate method

Turning a practice test into one number out of 100

This is our method, and every step is labelled. The first three steps are exact arithmetic on published weights. Only the last step is a judgement, and it is ours, not College Board’s.

  1. Use a full-length, current-format practice. Forty-two multiple-choice questions in 90 minutes and four free-response questions in 90 minutes, without notes beyond the Java Quick Reference. A half test gives you half an answer.
  2. Count Section I. Write down how many of the 42 you got right. There is nothing to subtract for wrong answers in this method; we treat a blank and a wrong answer the same.
  3. Mark Section II against a rubric. Give each free-response answer a score out of its own maximum: 7, 7, 5 and 6. Mark it the way the scoring guidelines describe, or have a teacher do it. Self-marking is where most estimates go wrong.
  4. Weight both parts. Multiply the Section I count by 55 / 42 and the Section II points by 45 / 25, then add. The result is a composite out of 100 that respects the published 55 and 45 split.
  5. Read the composite as a range, not a score. Compare it with the planning targets further down this page, which we set for our own students, and treat the result as a direction of travel rather than a forecast.

One rubric point beats one question

A single free-response point moves the composite by 1.8, while a single multiple-choice question moves it by about 1.31. So one extra rubric point is worth roughly 1.4 extra correct answers in Section I.

That is why students who leave a free-response part blank lose more than they think. A partly correct method that earns 2 of 4 points on Part A of Question 1 is worth 3.6 composite points, almost three multiple-choice questions.

But the clock favors Section I

Measured per minute, the picture flips. A minute in Section I is worth about 0.61 composite points a minute, against 0.50 in Section II, because 55% of the score fits into the same 90 minutes as 45%.

In practice this means two habits: never leave a multiple-choice answer blank, and on the free response write something for every part, because the first points on a question are usually the cheapest to earn.

What the three results show

Student A answers six more multiple-choice questions than Student B and still finishes below B. Student C gets eleven fewer questions right than A and finishes highest, because 21 of 25 rubric points is a lot of weight.

The lesson for revision is not that one section matters more. It is that the two sections reward different skills: Section I rewards tracing code someone else wrote, Section II rewards writing your own, and a student who only practises one of them leaves points behind.

Why 42.0 and not 42

The division mcRight / 42.0 uses a double literal on purpose. If both sides were int, Java would do integer division, 30 / 42 would become 0, and every student would score zero on Section I.

That is a real AP CSA idea, from the casting and range topic in Unit 1, and it shows up in multiple-choice questions that ask what an expression evaluates to. Change 42.0 to 42 and run it again to see the bug for yourself.

Ways to extend it

Add a check that each free-response score is no higher than its maximum of 7, 7, 5 or 6, and print a warning if it is. That is a guard condition, the same idea the scoring guidelines reward in method-writing questions.

Or store several practice results in an ArrayList and print the composite for each, which turns this into a small Unit 4 exercise in traversing a list.

Marking Section II fairly

How free-response points are actually earned in 2026

A composite is only as honest as the free-response marks that go into it. The 2026 Scoring Guidelines on AP Central set out the rules, and three of them change how a student should mark practice work.

Rules from the 2026 AP Computer Science A Scoring Guidelines (read 17 October 2026)
RuleWhat the guidelines sayWhat it means for self-marking
Algorithm pointsEach question has one or two criteria labelled algorithm, "each of which is worth one point", and they require all necessary steps connected in an appropriate orderDo not award the algorithm point because most of the steps are there; the order and the connections matter
When an algorithm point is lostThe list includes required pieces omitted, steps assembled incorrectly, and "A value is returned from a void method or constructor."Check method headers and return statements before you count the point
Non-algorithm pointsAssessed with a narrower focus, earned even when other errors are presentA wrong loop does not cancel a correct method call elsewhere in the same answer
Errors to IgnoreMinor slips that do not cost the point, such as "Spelling/case discrepancies where there is no ambiguity" and missing parentheses on a call with no parametersDo not dock yourself for small typing slips the guidelines forgive

The common self-marking mistake

Students marking their own code tend to be generous on algorithm points and harsh on small syntax slips, which is the reverse of what the guidelines do. The guidelines forgive a missing semicolon where the structure clearly conveys intent, and refuse an algorithm point when a step is in the wrong place.

A practical fix: mark each criterion separately, one line per point, and write down why each point was earned or not. If you cannot say why, a reader probably could not either.

Old questions, new format

AP Central keeps free-response questions from earlier years online, but warns that after the 2025 to 2026 course and exam revisions those older questions "do not completely align with the current AP CSA Exam".

The difference matters for a calculator. In 2025 each of the four questions had 9 possible points, and College Board’s scoring statistics give mean scores of 4.53, 5.24, 4.11 and 3.95. Those means describe the old format; scoring statistics for the 2026 exam were not on AP Central when we checked on 17 October 2026.

Sources: 2026 AP Computer Science A Scoring Guidelines, the past exam questions page and the 2025 scoring statistics, read 17 October 2026.

What a calculator cannot do

Why no AP CSA calculator can give an official score

Every calculator online, including this one, hits the same wall: the step from raw points to a 1 to 5 score is set by College Board and not published for the current exam.

How College Board describes scoring

The Course and Exam Description explains that free-response scores are weighted and combined with the computer-scored multiple choice, and that this raw score is converted into a composite AP score on a 1 to 5 scale.

It also says AP Exams are criterion-referenced rather than graded on a curve: every student who meets the criteria for a given score receives it, however many students that is. So the cutoffs are not a fixed share of test takers, and a score distribution cannot be turned into cut points.

What the five scores mean

College Board’s AP Score Scale Table attaches a recommendation to each score: a 5 is "Extremely well qualified", a 4 "Very well qualified", a 3 "Qualified", a 2 "Possibly qualified" and a 1 "No recommendation".

Those descriptions are about readiness for college credit and placement. Whether a given college gives credit for a given score is the college’s own decision, which is a separate question from the estimate on this page.

Using the number well

A composite is a tool for revision, not a verdict

The most useful thing about a composite is not the total. It is what the two halves say when you put them side by side.

Track it over time

One practice composite is noise. Three, taken two weeks apart under the same conditions, show a trend you can trust.

Split it by question

Write the four free-response scores separately. A student scoring 6 of 7 on class design and 1 of 6 on 2D arrays has a clear next topic.

Mark the misses

For every wrong multiple-choice answer, note the unit. A pile of Unit 4 misses at 30 to 40 percent of Section I matters more than a few in Unit 3.

For parents

Ask to see the two halves of the composite, not only the total. The split tells you whether your teen needs more code writing or more code reading.

A falling composite in February is common when Unit 4 arrives. It usually recovers once arrays and lists settle.

For students

Time yourself honestly. A composite earned with extra minutes describes a different exam.

Keep every marked free-response answer. Rereading your own lost points is the fastest revision there is.

AP Computer Science A scores: common questions

Is there an official AP Computer Science A score calculator?

No. College Board publishes the section weights and the free-response point values, but not the raw score needed for each AP score on the current exam. Any calculator, including ours, gives an estimate.

How is the AP Computer Science A exam weighted?

Section I has 42 multiple-choice questions worth 55% of the score, and Section II has 4 free-response questions worth 45%. Each section lasts 90 minutes.

How many points is each AP CSA free-response question worth?

In the current format, Question 1 is worth 7 points (Part A 4, Part B 3), Question 2 is worth 7, Question 3 is worth 5 and Question 4 is worth 6, according to the Course and Exam Description.

How do I estimate my AP CSA score from a practice test?

Our method multiplies the number of multiple-choice questions right by 55 / 42, multiplies the free-response points by 45 / 25, and adds the two for a composite out of 100. It is an estimate, not an official conversion.

Is AP Computer Science A graded on a curve?

College Board says AP Exams are criterion-referenced, not graded on a curve: every student who meets the criteria for a score receives it, however many students that is.

Can I use old AP CSA free-response questions in a calculator?

With care. AP Central says the older questions do not completely align with the current exam, and before 2026 each question had 9 points rather than 7, 7, 5 and 6.

Do small typing mistakes cost points on the AP CSA free response?

Often not. The 2026 Scoring Guidelines list errors to ignore, such as spelling or case slips with no ambiguity, as long as no specific guideline says otherwise.

What does an AP score of 3 mean?

On the College Board AP Score Scale Table a 3 means Qualified, a 4 Very well qualified and a 5 Extremely well qualified. Colleges decide their own credit rules.

When is the next AP Computer Science A exam?

AP Central lists Wednesday, May 12, 2027, Session 2, for the AP Computer Science A Exam.

What times are AP CSA classes for US students?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible. Choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

How much do AP Computer Science A classes cost?
