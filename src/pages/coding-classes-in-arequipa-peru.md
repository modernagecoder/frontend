## Do schools in Arequipa teach coding?

Not as a subject of its own. Arequipa follows the national curriculum, the CNEB, and its secundaria timetable has no computing area. Digital skills are expected across every subject instead.

### Digital skills without a lesson

The CNEB lists competency 28, "Se desenvuelve en entornos virtuales generados por las TIC", as one of two competencies that are transversal: they belong to every area rather than to a subject. Its capacities include managing information and creating virtual objects, but the standard names no programming language.

In practice this means that a child can use digital tools in maths, science or communication, but nothing in the plan requires a teacher to show how a program is written.

### The weekly timetable in secundaria

Under the regular school day, secundaria has a 35-hour week. Matemática and Comunicación get 4 hours each in every grade, Inglés 3 hours, and Educación para el Trabajo 2 hours. There is no area called computing, informatics or programming.

MINEDU has started to test Python in schools: its Fábrica de Programadores pilot, launched in July 2025, trains 1,000 students of 4° and 5° de secundaria in Lima Norte in Python, and those who finish both stages can sit the PCEP exam. It is a Lima pilot, so an Arequipa student who wants the same path learns it outside school.

## Should an Arequipa child start with robotics or with coding?

With coding, in our view, because coding is the part of robotics that transfers. We teach programming only; we do not sell or ship robot kits.

### What MINEDU itself says about robotics

MINEDU's own document on educational robotics puts it plainly: "la robótica en educación no es el objetivo, es el medio". Robotics is a means to learn, used inside STEAM projects, not a goal on its own.

Peru has used robotics in schools for a long time. The same document says MINEDU has worked with educational robotics since 1996, through the Infoescuela project, and that in 2009 and 2010 it supplied LEGO WeDo kits to almost all public primary schools.

### Where coding fits

Every robot runs a program: do this, repeat that, if the sensor sees a wall then turn. Those three ideas, sequence, loop and condition, are exactly what a child learns in the first months of Scratch, and they work the same when the program drives a sprite, a game character or a motor.

A child who has learned to code can pick up any robot kit later and understand why it moves. A child who has only assembled kits often cannot yet write the instructions that make one move differently.

1. **Ask what the child will write.** In any robotics workshop, ask whether the child writes the program or follows a printed one. Writing it is where the learning happens.
2. **Ask what happens after the kit.** Kits stay at the academy or need to be bought. A coding skill goes home with the child and works on any computer.
3. **Try a robot without a robot.** In our classes, younger children program a sprite that must follow a line, avoid walls and count its moves: the same logic as a line-following robot, with no hardware to break.
4. **Add hardware later if the interest lasts.** If your child loves building, a kit at 11 or 12, after a year of Scratch or Python, is far more rewarding than one at 7.

## Is the Colca Canyon really three metres above the sea?

The Gobierno Regional de Arequipa publishes an inventory of the region's tourist attractions as open data. Our older students use it to learn a habit every programmer needs: text that looks like a number is not a number until you have cleaned it and checked it.

### The data

The dataset is the Inventario de Atractivos Turísticos de Arequipa, published by the Gobierno Regional de Arequipa on Peru's Plataforma Nacional de Datos Abiertos under the Open Data Commons Attribution Licence (ODC-By), and last modified on 30 June 2026. The file we downloaded on 9 November 2026, inventario-atractivos-turisticos-arequipa.csv, has 338 rows: churches, valleys, beaches, festivals and dishes, each with a province, a district and an altitude.

The altitude column is where it gets interesting. People typed it by hand over several years, and once you replace every digit with the same symbol, the column still has 35 different spellings.

### The question

Many Arequipa families have driven up to the Colca valley, in the province of Caylloma. So we ask: which attraction in Caylloma sits lowest? A twelve year old guesses the bottom of the canyon, which is a fair guess.

The first program pulls out the digits from each altitude, turns them into a number, sorts the 64 Caylloma places and prints the lowest, the next one and the highest.

**How the file writes heights: five real entries, exactly as typed**

| Attraction | Province | Altitude as written |
|---|---|---|
| Centro Historico De Arequipa | Arequipa | 2 335 m s. n. m |
| Monasterio De Santa Catalina | Arequipa | 2335 m.s.n.m |
| Valle Del Colca | Caylloma | 3,651 |
| Pueblo De Chivay | Caylloma | 3.635 msnm |
| Cañon Del Colca | Caylloma | 3 m.s.n.m |

**Version 1: pull out the digits and sort** (Python)
```python
import csv

def metres(text):
    digits = ""
    for ch in text:
        if ch.isdigit() or ch == ".":
            digits += ch
        elif ch.isalpha():
            break
    if digits == "":
        return None
    return float(digits)

colca = []
with open("inventario-atractivos-turisticos-arequipa.csv", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        if row["Recurso - Provincia"] == "Caylloma":
            colca.append((metres(row["Recurso - Altitud"]), row["Recurso - Nombre"]))

colca.sort()
print("Places in Caylloma:", len(colca))
print("Lowest:", colca[0])
print("Next:", colca[1])
print("Highest:", colca[-1])
```
It prints: Places in Caylloma: 64, Lowest: (3.0, 'Cañon Del Colca'), Next: (3.635, 'Pueblo De Chivay') and Highest: (4910.0, 'Mirador De Patapampa').

The answer says the Colca Canyon is three metres above sea level and the town of Chivay three and a half. Children laugh, and then the real work begins. Chivay is the easier case: the same file lists the Plaza Principal de Chivay at "3630 m.s.n.m", so "3.635" must be 3,635 written with a dot between the thousands, a common way of writing large numbers in Peru. The program read the dot as a decimal point.

The canyon is harder. "3 m.s.n.m" cannot be fixed by any rule, because nothing in the text says what the full number should be; a digit or more seems to have been lost when it was typed. The honest thing for a program to do is not to guess, but to flag it for a person to check.

**Version 2: read the dot the Peruvian way, and flag what looks wrong** (Python)
```python
import csv
import statistics

def metres(text):
    digits = ""
    for ch in text:
        if ch.isdigit() or ch == ".":
            digits += ch
        elif ch.isalpha():
            break
    if digits == "":
        return None
    whole, dot, rest = digits.partition(".")
    if dot and len(rest) == 3:      # Peru often writes 3.635 for 3,635
        return float(whole + rest)
    return float(digits)

places = []
with open("inventario-atractivos-turisticos-arequipa.csv", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        m = metres(row["Recurso - Altitud"])
        if m is not None:
            places.append((row["Recurso - Provincia"], m, row["Recurso - Nombre"]))

by_province = {}
for prov, m, name in places:
    by_province.setdefault(prov, []).append(m)

for prov, m, name in places:
    middle = statistics.median(by_province[prov])
    if middle > 2000 and m < 100:
        print("Check by hand:", name, m, "in", prov, "middle", middle)

trusted = sorted((m, name) for prov, m, name in places
                 if prov == "Caylloma" and m >= 100)
print("Lowest we trust:", trusted[0])
print("Chivay now reads:", [m for m, name in trusted if name == "Pueblo De Chivay"])
```
It prints: Check by hand: Cañon Del Colca 3.0 in Caylloma middle 3605.0, then Lowest we trust: (1900.0, 'Valle De Sangalle') and Chivay now reads: [3635.0].

Version 2 makes two changes. A dot followed by exactly three digits is treated as a thousands separator, which fixes Chivay and the Luicho hot springs in La Unión, written "2.722". And any place under 100 metres in a province whose middle altitude is over 2,000 metres is printed for a human to check. Only the canyon is caught. The lowest Caylloma attraction we can trust is the Sangalle valley at 1,900 metres, in the Cabanaconde district, the same district the file gives for the canyon. The twelve year old's guess was not far off.

| Province | Places with a height | Middle altitude, metres |
|---|---|---|
| Arequipa | 80 | 2,353.5 |
| Caylloma | 64 | 3,605 |
| Castilla | 52 | 2,731 |
| Islay | 47 | 15 |

Data: Gobierno Regional de Arequipa, Inventario de Atractivos Turísticos de Arequipa, Plataforma Nacional de Datos Abiertos, Open Data Commons Attribution Licence (ODC-By). Counts and medians are our own, from Version 2's cleaning. In the province of Arequipa the lowest place is the Laguna y Salinera de San Camilo in La Joya at 1,117 metres and the highest is the Chachani volcano at 5,760 metres; across the file, 96 places sit above 3,000 metres and 16 above 4,000. Two of the 338 rows belong to other departments, Ica and Cusco, and two have no altitude at all.

1. **Under 8, pencil and paper.** Write the number two thousand three hundred and thirty-five in four different ways on paper. Which ones would a friend read correctly?
2. **Scratch, around 8 to 10.** Put six heights from the table into a list and find the smallest. Then add "3" as a seventh height and watch it win.
3. **First Python, around 10 to 12.** Start with Version 1: explain the Chivay result in your own words, then write the thousands-separator fix before looking at ours.
4. **Ages 13 and over.** The file gives the Misti volcano 4,100 metres and the Chachani 5,760. Find out what each number measures, the summit or the place where visitors stand, and write a check that would warn you.
5. **Going further.** Count the 35 spellings yourself, then write one function that turns every one of them into a number or into a clear "cannot read".

## Which maths and computing contests can a student in Arequipa enter?

Two national ones matter most for secundaria students: the ONEM for maths and the OPI for programming. We are not connected with either organiser; we describe them from their own pages and teach with our own problems.

### ONEM, the national school maths olympiad

The Olimpiada Nacional Escolar de Matemática is run by MINEDU, and El Peruano reports that it is organised "gracias a una alianza estratégica entre el Ministerio de Educación y la empresa privada Aceros Arequipa", a company that carries the city's name. The 2026 edition, the XXII ONEM, drew more than 1.7 million secundaria students.

Students enter through their school. The 2026 school stage was held across the country on Wednesday 8 July 2026, then winners moved on through the UGEL and regional stages to a national stage on Sunday 11 October 2026 at Huampaní, with 237 finalists. The school-stage paper has ten questions in which students write their own answer rather than choosing one, with two hours to solve them.

### OPI, the Peruvian computing olympiad

The Olimpiadas Peruanas de Computación are organised by UTEC and are free for every secundaria student in the country. For 2026, registration closed on 18 March 2026 and the final was held on 11 April 2026.

Under the 2025 rules, the first round accepted C++ or Python, the in-person final allowed only C++, and using generative AI to solve problems was banned. That last rule fits how we teach: students learn to reason their way to a program, not to paste one.

### At what age can my child start?

From 6. Six and seven year olds start with Scratch blocks, where nothing has to be typed. From about 9, children move to typed Python. Teenagers can start from zero at 13, and adults at any age; our learners range from 6 to 67.

### Do schools in Arequipa teach programming?

Not as a subject. Arequipa follows the national curriculum, where digital skills are a transversal competency across all areas and the 35-hour secundaria week has no computing area. MINEDU's Python pilot, Fábrica de Programadores, started in Lima Norte in 2025.

### What time are classes in Arequipa?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. Arequipa keeps Peru time all year; India is 10 hours 30 minutes ahead, so 09:00 in Arequipa is 19:30 for the mentor.

### Can adults in Arequipa join?

Yes. University students and working adults take the same live classes in their own groups, usually Python first and then data analysis or AI agents. Classes fit around work, at whatever hour you choose.

### Can you help my child prepare for the ONEM?

We teach the reasoning the ONEM rewards, with original problems we write ourselves, never the organiser's papers. Students enter the ONEM through their school, starting at the school stage, which in 2026 was held on 8 July. We are not connected with MINEDU or the ONEM.

### How much does it cost, and in which currency?

The fee is monthly and shown on this page in US dollars, charged to your card in dollars; your bank converts it to soles. A visitor in India sees rupees instead. The first class is free and needs no card.

### Do you give a certificate?

Courses that include one give our own certificate of completion. It is not a Peruvian school qualification and is not issued by MINEDU, a university or any school; what it shows is the work the learner has actually done.

### What will my child actually build?

Younger children make games and animated stories in Scratch. From about 9 they write Python programs and simple games, and older children and teenagers work with real data, such as the Arequipa tourist inventory project on this page.
