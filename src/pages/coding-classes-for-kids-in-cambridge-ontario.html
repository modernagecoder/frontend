Grade 7 coding in Cambridge

Sub-programs and a defined count: the Ontario Grade 7 coding a Cambridge child meets

Cambridge public schools teach the Ontario curriculum. We copied the Grade 7 text from the ministry’s online curriculum on 22 October 2026, and set our classroom approach beside each line.

Grade 7, C3.1Code “that involves events influenced by a defined count and/or subprogram and other control structures”.
Two new ideas at once. We teach them one at a time: first a loop that runs a set number of times, then a named block of code the main program calls.
Grade 7, notesA defined count repeats an instruction a set number of times, or, in the ministry’s words, “until a condition has been met”.
Children write both kinds and compare: repeat 10 times, and keep going until the total passes 100. Knowing which one a problem needs is half the work.
Grade 7, notes“Sub-programs are used to assemble a complex program by writing portions of the code that can be modularized.”
We call them functions and give them good names. A program made of well-named functions reads almost like a list of instructions in English.
Grade 7, notesThe notes add that a sub-program can be reused across programs, or called more than once by the same main program.
Our bikeway project calls the same function four times with four different inputs. Children see straight away why writing it once beats writing it four times.

The ministry’s own example of a sub-program is one that finds the area of a rectangle and is then reused to work out surface area and volume. That is the shape of every good function: a small job, done well, used often. The notes also suggest that “One way to introduce the idea of a sub-program is to use a defined count”, which is the order we follow. Grade 7 is also where Ontario’s cellphone memo, PPM 128, moves from the whole-day rule for younger children to a class-time rule: for Grades 7 to 12 it says devices are “stored out of view and powered off or set to silent mode during instructional time”. Our classes run at home on a computer, after school. The Ontario coding guide for Grades 1 to 8 has the full ladder.

Cambridge data project

How many kilometres of bike lane does Cambridge have? Measuring the City’s bikeway layer with a function

The City of Cambridge publishes its Bikeway Network as open data: every piece of bike lane, signed route and trail, with its type and its length in metres. Our older kids write one function that adds up the length of any type, then call it for each type. The kilometres in this section are our arithmetic on the copy of the layer we downloaded on 22 October 2026.

Bikeway Network, City of Cambridge: our sum of SHAPE.LEN by TYPE, in kilometres (rounded to two decimals; the seven largest types shown)

The layer holds 1,541 pieces, every one marked IN SERVICE. Its map projection measures in metres, so SHAPE.LEN is a length in metres and dividing by 1,000 gives kilometres. Added together, all the pieces come to about 247.0 km by our sum; that is our figure from the raw lengths, not a City total, and the rounded rows above will not add up to it exactly. Here is the program a 12-year-old writes, with the Grade 7 sub-program in the middle:import json with open("bikeway-network.json", encoding="utf-8") as f: pieces = [feature["attributes"] for feature in json.load(f)["features"]] def km_of(kind): metres = 0 for piece in pieces: if piece["TYPE"] == kind: metres += piece["SHAPE.LEN"] return round(metres / 1000, 1) for kind in ["BIKE LANE", "SIGNED ROUTE", "MULTI-USE SECONDARY", "MULTI-USE PRIMARY"]: print(kind, km_of(kind), "km")

Write it once

km_of takes a type name, walks through the pieces, adds the lengths that match and returns kilometres. The four calls at the bottom reuse it, exactly as the Grade 7 notes describe.

A trade-off to discuss

Each call walks the whole list again, four walks for four types. For 1,541 pieces that is instant. Children who did the Grade 6 efficiency work can say how to make it one walk, and when that would matter.

Rounding at the end

The function rounds only after adding. Rounding each piece first would throw away a little every time, and 1,541 small losses add up. Bike lane comes out as 62.9 km either way here, but the habit matters.

A row is a piece, not a path

Why 266 pieces of bike lane? A lane is split wherever something changes, at a junction, a surface or a road segment. Our check found the shortest piece is 0.03 metres long, and 71 pieces are under 10 metres; the longest is about 2,051 metres. Counting rows tells you how the City stores the map, not how many routes a child could ride. Measuring length is the honest way to compare types.

Two more questions a class can ask of the same file. Grouped by asset type, on-road bikeways come to about 157.9 km and trails to about 89.1 km in our sums. By surface, 1,253 pieces are asphalt, 171 granular and 28 wood plank, the boardwalk sort, while 47 have no surface recorded. We leave out street and trail names in class. Coding and AI

Is coding still worth it for a Cambridge child in the age of AI?

Yes, because breaking a big job into small named parts is what people do and machines still need.

The bikeway project is a small example. Deciding that the job needs one function called four times, that rounding belongs at the end, and that rows are not routes: none of that is typing. An AI tool can produce code, but a child who has designed functions knows whether the code it produced is organised sensibly, and can fix it when it is not.

Children under 13 in our classes do not work with adult chatbots. A teacher shows them, with games and sorting activities, how a model picks up patterns from examples and where that goes wrong. Most of the time they work things out themselves: plan, write a small part, test it, then the next part.

Grade 7 math in Ontario treats splitting a solution into named parts as something every child should learn, and a child who can do that can read, judge and repair what an AI hands back. The longer argument is in why kids should keep learning to code.

Are there coding classes for kids in Cambridge, Ontario?

Yes, live online. We have no Cambridge premises; children learn from home with a teacher, one to one or in a group of fifteen to twenty.

Is this Cambridge in Ontario or in England?

This page is for Cambridge, Ontario, in the Kitchener, Cambridge and Waterloo area. Everything here follows the Ontario curriculum and Eastern time.

What is a sub-program in Grade 7 math?

It is a named piece of code that the main program can call, often more than once. Programmers call it a function. Ontario adds it to the Grade 7 coding expectations.

Can a younger child learn functions?

In a simple form, yes. Scratch custom blocks let children of 8 or 9 make their own named blocks, which is the same idea in a friendlier shape.

What age can my child start?

Six is a good starting age, around Grade 1. Our kids classes go up to 12, and teenagers move on to separate courses.
