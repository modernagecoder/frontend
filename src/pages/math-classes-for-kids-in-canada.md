# Math classes for kids in Canada

Elementary math in Canada is set by each province, and a child in Grade 3 in Ottawa meets a different checkpoint from a child in Grade 3 in Red Deer. What does not change from province to province is what makes a child good at math: knowing why a method works, being able to picture a problem, and checking an answer instead of hoping. This page sets out where Grade 1 to 8 math is formally checked in 2026 to 2027, which free challenges a younger child can try, what we teach in each grade band, and two worked examples from our own classes, so you can see the teaching before you book anything.

### How to read a checkpoint

An EQAO or PAT result describes one sitting in one year. It is useful as a map of what a child has and has not yet secured, and much less useful as a verdict on the child.

We read a report alongside the child's own work. If a Grade 6 result shows a weakness with fractions, the question we ask is which earlier idea is missing, usually the meaning of a fraction as a share or a point on a number line, not how to drill the test format.

### What we do not do

We do not teach to a test paper, promise a level or a mark, or run practice in the exact format of a provincial test. Our classes build the math those tests are trying to see.

We are not connected with EQAO, Alberta Education or any school board. Dates come from their pages, and families should check those pages before each school year, because they can change.

## Why coding now turns up in elementary math

Some Canadian curricula now place coding inside the math or design program. A parent who sees a loop on a math worksheet is not imagining it.

**Coding in elementary math and design, quoted from the curriculum pages (read 22 October 2026)**

| Where | What the curriculum says |
|---|---|
| Ontario math, every grade 1 to 8 | Students "solve problems and create computational representations of mathematical situations using coding concepts and skills". |
| Ontario math, Grade 1 | Students write and run code, "including code that involves sequential events". |
| Ontario math, Grade 6 | Students work "by writing and executing efficient code". |
| Ontario math, Grade 8 | Code includes "the analysis of data in order to inform and communicate decisions". |
| BC design (ADST 6) | The Computational Thinking module names "simple algorithms" and "visual programming". |

Our view, labelled as ours: coding in math is useful when the program says something about the math. A loop that adds 3 each time is a pattern rule in motion; a program that counts how many times a die lands on six is a probability experiment. That is how we use it in class, and it is why the Snow Fort Wall example below ends with eight lines of Python. Children who enjoy that side can go further in our [Math Through Coding](/courses/maths-through-coding) course.

Sources: the Ontario curriculum content service behind [Ontario elementary mathematics (2020)](https://www.dcp.edu.gov.on.ca/en/curriculum/elementary-mathematics), and [BC ADST 6](https://curriculum.gov.bc.ca/curriculum/adst/6/core), read 22 October 2026.

## Two worked examples from our classes, labelled ours

We wrote both problems for our students. The first is for Grades 4 and 5, the second for Grades 7 and 8. Each shows the same habit at a different age: see the situation, then describe it in more than one way.

Worked example 1 (Grades 4 and 5): Canoe Water Share. Four paddlers share 3 litres of drinking water equally on a canoe day. How much does each paddler get?

1. Picture it. Draw 3 bottles, one litre each. Four people cannot share one whole bottle each, so cut every bottle into 4 equal parts. Each part is one quarter of a litre.
2. Share it out. There are 3 bottles times 4 parts, which is 12 quarters. Twelve quarters shared among 4 paddlers is 3 quarters each.
3. Name the answer. Each paddler gets three quarters of a litre. The child writes it as 3/4 L and then, because 1 litre is 1000 mL, as 750 mL.
4. See the pattern. 3 litres among 4 people gave 3/4. The teacher asks what 5 litres among 4 people would give. The child predicts 5/4, which is one and a quarter litres, and checks it with the bottle picture.
5. Say the rule. Sharing a things equally among b people gives a/b each. The fraction bar means "shared among", which is the idea Grade 6 division of fractions will lean on later.

Worked example 2 (Grades 7 and 8): Snow Fort Wall. A fort wall is built in stages. Wall 1 uses 5 blocks, wall 2 uses 8 blocks, wall 3 uses 11 blocks, and every wall adds the same number of blocks as the one before. How many blocks does wall 20 use, and which wall uses exactly 92 blocks?

**The Snow Fort Wall as a table (our example)**

| Wall number (n) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 10 | 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blocks | 5 | 8 | 11 | 14 | 32 | 62 |

1. Find what changes. The blocks go up by 3 each time, so 3 belongs next to n in the rule.
2. Find what stays. 3 times 1 is 3, but wall 1 has 5 blocks, so 2 more blocks are always there. The rule is blocks = 3n + 2.
3. Test the rule. Wall 4: 3 times 4 plus 2 is 14, which matches the table. Wall 20: 3 times 20 plus 2 is 62.
4. Work backwards. For 92 blocks, take away the 2 that are always there (90) and share by 3: wall 30. Check: 3 times 30 plus 2 is 92.
5. Let a program check it. The short program below grows the wall step by step and then searches for 92 blocks. It agrees with the rule.

**Snow Fort Wall · our Python check** (python)
```python
# Our Snow Fort Wall pattern (a Grade 7 or 8 coding-in-math task)
blocks = 5            # wall 1 uses 5 blocks
for wall in range(1, 11):
    print("Wall", wall, "uses", blocks, "blocks")
    blocks = blocks + 3   # every new wall adds 3 blocks

# Working backwards: which wall uses exactly 92 blocks?
wall = 1
while 3 * wall + 2 < 92:
    wall = wall + 1
print("92 blocks makes wall", wall)
```
We ran this under Python 3 on 22 October 2026. The loop is the "add 3 each time" picture; the rule 3n + 2 is the shortcut. A child who can explain why both give the same numbers understands linear patterns, which is the base for Grade 9 algebra.

**What the program prints** (text)
```text
Wall 1 uses 5 blocks
Wall 2 uses 8 blocks
Wall 3 uses 11 blocks
...
Wall 10 uses 32 blocks
92 blocks makes wall 30
```

### Why one situation, three ways

A table shows what happens, a picture shows why, and a rule lets a child jump straight to wall 20 or wall 30. Children who only ever see the rule forget it. Children who build it from the table can rebuild it when they forget.

This is the lesson family behind our elementary teaching: one situation, three faces.

### The mistakes these examples catch

In the canoe problem, many children answer 4/3 because they divide the larger number by the smaller. The bottle picture shows at once that each person gets less than one litre.

In the fort problem, the common slip is the rule 5n, because wall 1 has 5 blocks. Testing it on wall 2 gives 10 instead of 8, which is why we always test a rule on a second case.

## Eight practice problems for Grades 1 to 8, written by us

Two problems for each grade band, with answers. They are our own problems, not copied from any provincial test or contest. Ask your child to explain each answer out loud; the explanation tells you more than the number.

**Our practice set with answers (ours)**

| Band | Problem | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Grades 1 and 2 | A bus has 9 children on it. At the next stop 6 more get on. How many children are on the bus? Show it by making a ten. | 15 (9 and 1 make 10, then 5 more) |
| Grades 1 and 2 | Sam has 42 hockey cards in tens and ones. How many tens and how many ones? | 4 tens and 2 ones |
| Grades 3 and 4 | Chairs are set out in 6 rows of 7. How many chairs? Draw the array, then find a second way to count. | 42 (for example 5 rows of 7 is 35, plus one more row of 7) |
| Grades 3 and 4 | A ribbon is folded into 4 equal parts. Three parts are blue. What fraction is not blue? | One quarter (1/4) |
| Grades 5 and 6 | Write 3/5 as a decimal and as a percent. Which is larger, 3/5 or 0.58? | 0.6 and 60 percent; 3/5 is larger |
| Grades 5 and 6 | A garden bed is 3.5 m long and 2 m wide. What is its area? Estimate first. | Estimate about 7; exact 7 square metres |
| Grades 7 and 8 | A pattern goes 4, 9, 14, 19. Write a rule for term n and find term 50. | 5n minus 1; term 50 is 249 |
| Grades 7 and 8 | The temperature is minus 8 degrees at 7 a.m. and rises 3 degrees each hour. When does it reach 4 degrees? | After 4 hours, at 11 a.m. |

If a child gets an answer right but cannot say why, we treat the problem as not yet learned. If a child gets it wrong but explains a sensible method with one slip, we treat it as nearly there. That difference shapes what the next class covers.

## What a month of math classes looks like

Our plan for a typical month, with two live classes a week. The order of topics follows the child's school year; the rhythm stays the same.

1. Week 1, first class: a short placement chat and a few problems at the child's grade, to find the earliest idea that is not yet secure. The teacher then tells parents what the chat showed.
2. Week 1, second class: rebuild that idea with pictures and objects on screen, even if it belongs to an earlier grade. Five minutes of mental math to start.
3. Week 2: bring the idea up to the child's current grade topic. The child explains a method out loud before writing it, and checks one answer a second way.
4. Week 3: mixed practice, including one problem written in words that hides the operation, so the child has to decide what to do rather than follow a heading.
5. Week 4: a short review problem set, a puzzle to finish, and a short talk with parents about what is secure, what is nearly there and what comes next month.

### Homework, lightly

Ten to fifteen minutes between classes for younger children. More is rarely better at this age.

### School first

If a school unit is coming up, the teacher can bring it forward, so class supports what happens in the classroom.

### No screens to stare at

Children draw on paper and hold it up to the camera. Thinking happens with a pencil, not a slideshow.

## Four stages of a young mathematician

Our stages, used to place a child honestly in the first class, whatever their grade.

**From counting to reasoning (ours)**

| Stage | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1. Counting | Gets answers by counting one at a time, often on fingers, and is unsure what a written sum means |
| 2. Calculating | Follows taught methods accurately but cannot say why they work, and stalls on a word problem |
| 3. Explaining | Chooses a method, explains it, and checks an answer with a picture or estimate |
| 4. Reasoning | Spots structure, writes a rule, works backwards, and enjoys a problem with no obvious first step |

### For parents

Many children sit at stage 2 for years because school marks reward accurate methods. Stage 3 is where confidence starts, and where most of our teaching time goes.
A child can be at stage 4 in one topic and stage 2 in another. That is normal, and the placement chat finds it.

### For children

If you can explain it to someone younger, you know it.
A wrong answer you can explain is more useful than a right answer you guessed.

## Math classes for kids: common questions

### What grades do your math classes for kids cover?

Grades 1 to 8, roughly ages 6 to 14, with an early-years course for younger children. Elementary Math covers Grades 1 to 5 in three levels and Middle School Math covers the years before high school. Each child is placed after a short chat in the first class.

### Do you follow my province's math curriculum?

We teach the math that every provincial curriculum is built on, and the teacher matches the order of topics to your child's school after the first class. Provinces set their own curricula, so tell us your province and grade and we will line the classes up with them.

### Will this help with EQAO in Ontario?

EQAO says its tests assess literacy and numeracy knowledge and skills, and it lists May 4 to June 8, 2027 for the Grade 3 and Grade 6 assessments. We teach the math underneath rather than the test format, and we make no promise about a result.

### What about the Alberta Provincial Achievement Tests?

Alberta says PATs are administered every year to students in Grades 6 and 9, written digitally, with the Grade 6 tests in June. Our classes build the Grade 6 math that sits under them; we do not run practice in the test's own format.

### Is my child too young for online math classes?

Six-year-olds do well in short, lively online classes with a parent nearby for the first few weeks. Very young children work with real objects and drawings at home while the teacher guides on screen.

### Is coding part of your math classes?

In the middle grades it can be, because some curricula, such as Ontario's, now include coding inside math. Our Math Through Coding course goes further for children aged 10 to 15 who enjoy building programs.

### Can my child write CEMC math contests through you?

No. CEMC says all students must participate through their school, so a teacher has to order the contest. We help children prepare, for example for Gauss in Grades 7 and 8, and they write at school.

### How big are the batches?

A batch has fifteen to twenty children, grouped by grade and level, with one regular teacher. If you prefer, a one-to-one plan gives your child a teacher alone.

### When are classes held for families across Canada?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. We confirm times in your own province's clock, and again when the clocks change.

### How do I know the classes are working?

Ask the teacher at any point what is secure, what is nearly there and what comes next. The clearest sign is a child who explains a method in their own words and checks an answer without being asked.
