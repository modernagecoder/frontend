The Ignite program

A gifted program at three South Australian schools

As described by two Ignite schools on 7 October 2026. The SA Department for Education site blocked our automated read, so we relied on its schools' pages.

Glenunga International High School describes itself as "a Department for Education Special Interest School" offering programs for students with high intellectual potential through Ignite. Its page says the program is funded by the Department of Education, supports identified gifted and talented students, and has been running since 1997.

The same page names the three schools offering it: "Glenunga International High School, The Heights High School, and Aberfoyle Park High School."

Aberfoyle Park High School describes IGNITE as "a year 7 to 10 specialist learning program catered to the needs of gifted students", and states that "All students can apply for the IGNITE program regardless of zone."

Glenunga's page notes that its Ignite students follow enrichment or accelerated pathways. How each school organises its classes is a question for the school itself.

Puzzles we wrote

Colouring arguments: proving something is impossible

Colour a board like a chessboard, in alternating dark and light squares. Many moves, tiles and paths must always switch colour or always cover one of each. Counting colours can then prove that a task can never be done, without trying every possibility.

Colour first

Shade the grid in a checkerboard pattern. Every square now has exactly one colour, and neighbours always differ.

Find what never changes

A domino always covers one dark and one light square. A step to a neighbouring square always changes colour.

Count and compare

If the task needs equal numbers of each colour and the board has unequal numbers, the task is impossible.

Original puzzles solved with colouring
PuzzleThe colouring ideaAnswer
An 8 by 8 board has two opposite corners removed. Can 31 dominoes cover the remaining 62 squares?Opposite corners share a colour, leaving 30 of one colour and 32 of the other; each domino covers one of eachNo
Can dominoes exactly cover a 5 by 5 board?25 squares is odd, and every domino covers 2No
A 4 by 4 board loses the two corners at the ends of its top row. Does colouring rule out a domino covering?Those corners are different colours, leaving 7 of each, so colouring cannot rule it outNo; and a covering does exist
A knight jumps around a chessboard. Can it return to its start in an odd number of moves?Every knight move changes colour, so it is back on its starting colour only after an even number of movesNo
On a 3 by 3 grid, a path visits every square once, stepping to neighbours. Can it start on an edge square that is not a corner?Shade corners and centre dark: 5 dark, 4 light. A 9-square path alternates colours, so it must start and end on darkNo; it must start on a corner or the centre

The first puzzle is a classic for a reason. Trying to place 31 dominoes by hand takes ages and never settles the question. Colouring settles it in one line: the board has 30 squares of one colour and 32 of the other, but 31 dominoes would cover exactly 31 of each. No amount of clever placing can fix that.

The grid path in the last row rewards slow reading. Shade the four corners and the centre dark; the four edge squares in between are light. Any path that steps only to neighbours must alternate dark, light, dark, light. A path through all nine squares therefore has five squares in one colour and four in the other, so it must begin and end on the colour that appears five times. Dark appears five times, so a full path has to start on a corner or the centre. Starting on a light edge square would need five light squares, and there are only four. One count rules out every attempt at once, which is exactly what makes colouring arguments so satisfying.

Parity, odd and even, is the simplest relative of these ideas. The 5 by 5 board fails before any colouring at all, because 25 squares cannot be split into pairs. Encourage students to try the cheapest argument first: count, then colour, then search for a construction only if neither settles the question.

The third puzzle teaches the limit of the method, and it is just as important. When the colours balance, colouring proves nothing either way. A student then has to find an actual covering, here a domino across the middle two top squares and three rows of dominoes below, to show the task is possible. Knowing when an argument works, and when it does not, is the heart of mathematical reasoning.

Why proof matters

From "I could not do it" to "it cannot be done"

The jump from failing to find something to proving it cannot exist is one of the most powerful ideas a young mathematician meets.

What changes in a child's thinking

Before colouring arguments, a stuck child concludes they are not clever enough. After them, they ask whether the task might be impossible, and look for something that never changes.

That question, what stays the same, reaches far beyond chessboards: into parity of numbers, balanced equations and later into the invariants used in computer science.

How to explore at home

Draw grids on paper and cut out dominoes from card. Let your child try to cover a board, then show the colouring.

Ask them to invent a board that colouring cannot rule out but that still cannot be covered. It is a genuinely hard and rewarding challenge.

Questions about Ignite

South Australian families ask

What is the Ignite program?

A South Australian special interest program for gifted students, funded by the Department of Education and offered at Glenunga International High School, The Heights High School and Aberfoyle Park High School, according to Glenunga's page.

When is the Ignite assessment?

Glenunga lists the ACER Ignite Assessment on 20 February 2027 for Year 6 students entering Year 7 the following year.

What does the assessment include?

Four parts over about three hours with a break: Reading Comprehension, Mathematical Reasoning, Abstract Reasoning and Written Expression.

Who marks it?

Glenunga's page says the test materials are sent to ACER in Melbourne for marking.

Can Year 5 students sit it?

Glenunga's page says Year 5 students may apply but need a signed letter of recommendation from their primary school principal.

What is a colouring argument?

A proof that colours a board, usually in a checkerboard pattern, and counts colours to show a task such as covering it with dominoes is impossible.

Are colouring puzzles too hard for Year 5?

Usually not. The 5 by 5 board needs only the idea of odd and even, and most curious Year 5 students can follow the corners puzzle once the board is coloured in front of them.

How long is the Mathematical Reasoning part?

The school pages we read do not say, so we do not state a length. ACER and the school provide format details at registration.

Can you help with registration or school choice?

No. We give no admissions or school-choice advice.

Will classes help my child get in?

We promise no result. We teach reasoning and mathematics that are valuable in themselves.

What time would lessons be in Adelaide?

We agree a weekly time after the free trial. During daylight saving Adelaide is five hours ahead of India time.
