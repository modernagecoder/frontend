## Why is the wait longer than half the gap?

If trains ran exactly every 20 minutes, a passenger arriving at a random moment would wait 10 minutes on average. When gaps vary, the long gaps take up more of the day, so more random arrivals fall into them. The average wait becomes the sum of the squared gaps divided by twice their total.

**Trains from Bangor towards Belfast in the NI Railways timetable, for dates in autumn 2026**

| Day | Trains | Average gap | Half the average gap | Real average wait |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wednesday 30 September | 51 | 19.82 minutes | 9.91 minutes | 13.64 minutes |
| Saturday 3 October | 31 | 32.00 minutes | 16.00 minutes | 16.88 minutes |
| Sunday 4 October | 14 | 60.00 minutes | 30.00 minutes | 30.00 minutes |

### 1. Rebuild the timetable

Each journey in the file has a start time and a pattern of timed links between stations. Adding the links up gives the time every train leaves Bangor, 51 of them on the Wednesday.

### 2. Measure the gaps

On the Wednesday the gaps run from 3 minutes to 60: six gaps of 3 minutes and six of 6 in the busy hours, eighteen of 30 minutes, and one of an hour late in the evening.

### 3. Test by simulation

Drop 200,000 imaginary passengers at random times between the first and last trains and measure each wait. The simulation gives 13.64 minutes, exactly what the formula predicts.

### Where the extra wait comes from

Half of the Wednesday's gaps are 20 minutes or longer, but those long gaps cover 76.0% of the day. A random passenger is therefore about three times as likely to arrive in a long gap as a short one. That is the inspection paradox: sample by time, and the long gaps are over-represented. On Sunday, when every gap is exactly an hour, the paradox disappears and the real wait is exactly half the gap.

Of course, most commuters check the timetable and do not arrive at random, so the 13.64 minutes describes someone who turns up without looking. It also shows what the timetable could offer: 51 trains spread evenly over the same hours would give an average wait of 9.91 minutes. Pairing trains in the rush hour suits passengers who plan their journey; someone who turns up at a random moment gains less from it.

## Five checks before quoting an average wait

Learned on the Bangor line, then used for bus stops, lift queues, website response times, class sizes and any average where people meet the long cases more often than the short ones.

**Decisions behind the Bangor timetable study**

| Question | For the Bangor trains | What goes wrong if you skip it |
|---|---|---|
| Average of what? | Waits felt by passengers, not gaps between trains | A service that seems twice as frequent as it feels |
| Which day is it? | A Wednesday, a Saturday and a Sunday | A weekday figure quoted for the weekend |
| Does the formula hold? | Checked with 200,000 simulated arrivals | A formula applied without being tested |
| Who arrives at random? | Only passengers who do not check the timetable | A result stretched to cover everyone |
| Which version of the timetable? | The file updated on 4 September 2026 | Old times compared with new ones |

The first row is the lesson that transfers. The same trick explains why a school's average class size can look small while most pupils sit in big classes, and why the average gap between buses is shorter than the gap most passengers experience. Whenever something is counted by how often people meet it, the long cases weigh more.

## AI can average any table. Why should a Bangor teenager ask what the average means?

Because the average gap between trains and the average wait for a train are different numbers, and a quick summary easily confuses them.

Ask an AI tool how often trains leave Bangor and it may correctly say every 20 minutes on average, and then suggest a 10 minute wait. The mistake is subtle and plausible. A learner who has simulated 200,000 passengers knows the right question: averaged over what, and from whose point of view?

The project also practises working with a real, messy data format. Rebuilding journeys from TransXChange patterns and timing links is exactly the kind of careful data engineering that AI systems depend on and rarely explain.

So a Bangor teenager should keep learning to code in 2026, at the end of a line whose Grade A station at Helen's Bay dates from the 1860s or 1870s: AI can compute an average instantly, but people still decide which average matters.
