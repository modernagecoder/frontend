# Four summers of camp is not four years of coding. Here is the arithmetic.

> A week-long holiday camp gives a child about twenty concentrated contact hours and then nothing to do with them. Twelve months of weekly ninety-minute sessions gives about seventy-two hours plus fifty-one intervals in which the student is expected to build something, and it is in those intervals that most progress actually happens. This page compares the two honestly, including the four situations where a camp is the better purchase, and explains why we insist on the same teacher for twelve months rather than treating continuity as a comfort feature.

## The arithmetic first

| | A one-week camp | A twelve-month track |
|---|---|---|
| Contact hours | About 20, in five days | About 72, across a year |
| Teacher continuity | One week | The same person throughout |
| Written progress record | Rarely | Monthly, per student |
| Finished, published projects | Usually one, often unfinished | Four, one a quarter |
| Commit history at the end | Five days of it | Twelve months of dated work |
| Gaps in which to build alone | None | Fifty-one |

The final row decides the outcome and is the hardest to see on a brochure. A camp gives a child twenty concentrated hours and then nothing to do with them. A weekly programme gives seventy-two hours and, crucially, fifty-one intervals in which they are expected to build something.

## Why a skill met in July and untouched until July is re-learned

Anybody who has learned an instrument knows the shape of this. Twenty hours in one week produces a real ability that then decays, fastest in the first month, because nothing is reinforcing it.

Eleven months later the student is not back at zero, which is a genuine benefit, but they are a long way below where they finished. So a child attending a camp every summer for four years does not accumulate four years of ability. They accumulate something closer to a year and a half, repeatedly re-approached. Parents can usually see this happening and often blame the child or the camp, when it is neither.

Weekly teaching works differently for a structural reason: nothing has time to decay before it is used again. A concept met in week three is needed in week five and assumed by week nine.

## Four situations where a camp is the better purchase

**You do not yet know whether your child likes this.** The strongest case, and a real one. A week is a cheap, fast, low-commitment experiment and a better first purchase than a year of anything.

**The social experience is the point.** Being in a room with other children doing something difficult is valuable in a way a weekly call does not fully replicate, particularly for a younger child.

**The schedule genuinely will not hold.** A family who knows weekly commitment will not survive is better off with concentrated weeks than a year of missed sessions.

**There is a specific short-format thing on offer.** A robotics week, a hardware project needing equipment nobody has at home. Those are better done in person and in a block, and we cannot offer them.

## The sensible combination

Use a camp to find out, and a year to get somewhere. If your child comes back talking about it, that is the moment for continuity, because enthusiasm has a short half-life and the next summer is a long way away.

Our free first class is a smaller version of the same test: one problem, ninety minutes, no card, and an honest account of what your child did with it.

Volunteer and school-based programmes in The Bahamas have done real work getting equipment and enthusiasm into classrooms, and children who have been through them usually arrive with better instincts. None of this is an argument against them. It is an argument about what continuous paid teaching has to offer to be worth paying for.
