> Good vibe coding projects for students share three properties: they are small enough to finish, they solve an irritation the student personally feels, and they contain one honest difficulty that forces learning, like persistent state, a live data source, or two users wanting the same thing. This page grades twelve such ideas across four rungs from first afternoon to portfolio piece, names the trap inside each, and lays out the 21-day idea-to-shipped plan used in Modern Age Coders classes. Reviewers of finished projects reward four things: it runs live, a note explains one decision, someone real has used it, and the student can tour the code cold. Classes run worldwide at USD 100 a month for groups, USD 150 one to one, first class free.

## The twelve ideas, with every trap named

Rung one, a first afternoon: the pocket-money ledger (trap: decimals and overdrafts teach validation), the sibling chore rotator (trap: fairness over weeks requires state that survives closing the tab), the homework countdown board (trap: dates, midnight, and time zones).

Rung two, a weekend or two: the bus-stop weather mirror from a public API (trap: the source will be down one morning and the page must say so), the quiz-night scoreboard (trap: ties and disputed points are rules-as-code), the stubborn flashcards where wrong cards return sooner (trap: scheduling logic is real logic and the model gets it subtly wrong first try).

Rung three, the 21-day term project: the league table from fixtures (trap: tiebreak ordering by points then goal difference then what), the club signup sheet with twenty places (trap: two people taking the last place at once, a first race condition), the reading log with covers from a book API (trap: other people's messy data).

Rung four, the portfolio piece: the revision planner that builds a timetable from exam dates (trap: clashing constraints), the recycling-day reminder on real local rules (trap: civic data is inconsistent), and the commissioned tool built for one real user who is not you (trap and crown: they will want something you never guessed, and you have to ask).

## The scoping conversation that decides everything

As pitched: a fitness app with logging, graphs, streaks, badges, friends, a leaderboard, an AI coach, and phone apps. Seven features, four systems, honestly four months. As shipped: my pushup tracker, one user, ten seconds to log a day, one 30-day chart, one streak number, works in the phone browser, nothing else, v2 only if v1 survives two weeks of real use. The three scoping questions: who is the one user, what is the one daily action, what can be deleted and added back later.

## The 21-day plan

- Days 1 to 2: cut the scope, write the ten-line spec.
- Days 3 to 7: the ugly walking skeleton; meet the trap early, on schedule.
- Days 8 to 12: defeat the trap properly; read more code than you prompt.
- Days 13 to 16: one real user, watched silently; their hesitations are the to-do list.
- Days 17 to 19: fix the top three findings, then stop; the rest become known limits.
- Days 20 to 21: ship at a live link, write a two-paragraph note, tell somebody.

## What reviewers actually reward

It runs live at a link that works on a phone. A short note explains one decision and one thing that went wrong. A real person other than the builder has used it. The builder can tour the code cold. None of these is visual polish, and all four are reachable from every rung.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where do good ideas come from?** Irritation. An idea the student did not choose survives two weeks; an irritation they feel survives the debugging.

**Do rebuilt YouTube projects count?** As practice. They become projects the moment they diverge: a feature the video never built, a real user, the messy cases handled.

**Do competitions allow AI-assisted projects?** Policies vary; the safe rule is disclose plainly and be able to explain every part without the AI present. Judges forgive assistance and punish the student who cannot answer what their own line does.

**Two friends, one project?** Yes: split by parts, not turns. One owns data and logic, the other the interface, integrating weekly.

**Five abandoned projects, normal?** Common, and fixable by one finishing experience: a deliberately small project taken all the way to shipped changes how a student chooses forever.
