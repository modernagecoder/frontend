## What does a child in Huánuco need at home for online coding classes?

A computer with a keyboard, a connection that holds a video call, and a quiet corner for about an hour. We say this first because, in Huánuco, the computer is the part many families do not yet have.

### Fewer than one home in five has a computer

In 2025, 17.5 per cent of households in the department of Huánuco had at least one computer, about half the national figure of 33.9 per cent. Internet reaches more homes than computers do: a third of households pay for a service, and almost seven in ten people aged six and over went online, mostly, we assume, on a phone.

So we will not pretend that every Huánuco family can start next week. If your child has only a phone, coding classes are not yet the right buy, and we would rather tell you so than take a fee for a class your child cannot follow.

### What works, and what does not

A laptop or desktop with a keyboard and a mouse or trackpad is what works. Scratch runs in a web browser, so even an older machine copes with the first months; typed Python needs a real keyboard, because nobody learns to type code on a touch screen.

The connection needs to hold a video call for an hour. A shared family computer is fine. Many children in our classes use a parent's work laptop in the evening, and the first free class is the place to check that the set-up holds.

1. **Check the machine.** Open a browser and visit the Scratch website. If a project plays smoothly, the computer is ready for the first months.
2. **Check the connection.** Make a video call to a relative for ten minutes at the hour you have in mind for the class. If the picture freezes often, try another hour or move nearer the router.
3. **Choose the corner.** A table, a chair and headphones with a microphone. Young children concentrate far better with headphones, and the mentor hears them clearly.
4. **Book the free class.** The mentor sees your child, your screen and your sound, and tells you honestly whether the set-up will hold a full course.

## Do primary schools in Huánuco teach coding?

Not as a subject. Huánuco follows Peru's national curriculum, the CNEB, which treats digital skills as something every area should use rather than as a lesson with its own hour.

### Six years of primaria, no computing hour

Under the Ley General de Educación, primaria lasts six years. MINEDU's normative ages are measured on 31 March, so a child starts 1° de primaria at six and usually finishes 6° de primaria at eleven.

In the CNEB, the competency about working in "entornos virtuales" (number 28) is transversal, meaning it is spread through every area instead of owning a lesson. A teacher may bring a tablet into a maths hour, yet the plan never asks anyone to show a child how a program gets written.

### How Huánuco children did in 2025

MINEDU's census assessment of 4° de primaria, published in INEI's 2026 compendium, found 23.2 per cent of Huánuco pupils at the satisfactory level in maths in 2025, against 30.0 per cent nationally, and 24.2 per cent in reading against 32.7.

Coding does not replace school maths, and we make no promise about marks. What it does is give a child a reason to use numbers: to move a character ten steps, count a score, or check that a total adds up, as the Kotosh project below asks.

## At what age can a child in Huánuco start coding?

At six, with blocks. The age decides the tool, not the other way round, and children are always grouped with others of a similar age.

### Six to eight

Classes are short and playful. Children drag coloured blocks to make a character walk, talk and change costume, and by the end of a term they have built an animated story of their own. Reading is helpful but not essential: the mentor reads instructions aloud and shows each step.

### Eight to ten

Scratch grows into real games with scores, levels and timers. Children learn the three ideas every program uses, doing things in order, repeating, and deciding, and many begin to type their first lines of Python. A Minecraft or Roblox course suits children who need a game to stay curious.

### Ten to twelve

Typed Python becomes the main tool. Children write programs that draw, ask questions, keep lists and read small files, and they begin to see how a computer can learn from examples. At this age a child can follow the Kotosh project on this page from start to finish.

**A first year for a Huánuco child, by age (our own plan)**

| Age | First term | Second term | By the end of the year |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 to 7 | A talking character and a short story | A simple maze game | Their own animated story, shown to the family |
| 8 to 9 | A game with a score | Levels and a timer | A quiz or platform game with three levels |
| 10 to 12 | First Python: drawings and questions | Lists, loops and small files | A small data project, such as counting visitors |

The plan is ours and is adjusted to each child. Some children move faster; nobody is pushed to type before they are ready.

## What does a live online coding class look like for a young child?

A mentor on video, the child's own screen shared, and the child doing the building. Here is a typical hour for a group of eight and nine year olds.

1. **Hello and a warm-up.** The mentor greets each child by name and asks one quick question, often a puzzle, to settle the group.
2. **Today's goal.** One small, visible goal: make the bat fly across the screen, or keep score when the cat catches a fish.
3. **Build it together.** The mentor shows one step, then each child does it on their own computer. Children share their screen when stuck, and the mentor guides rather than takes over.
4. **Make it yours.** Every child changes something: a colour, a sound, a rule. This is where real thinking happens, because the change usually breaks something first.
5. **Show and tell.** Two or three children show what they made and explain one thing they fixed, in their own English, at their own pace.
6. **A note for parents.** After class, families hear what was built and what comes next.

We teach children to think through a problem, not to copy an answer. A mentor who sees a child stuck asks "what did you expect to happen, and what happened instead?" before giving any hint. That habit, more than any language or tool, is what a child takes away from a year of classes. If you want a gentler first look at how this works for the very youngest, our guide to [online coding classes for kids in Peru](/online-coding-classes-for-kids-peru) walks through it age by age.

## Can a Spanish-speaking child in Huánuco follow classes in English?

Many can, with a patient mentor, and the free first class is where we find out. Every class is in English only; we never teach in Spanish.

### An honest fit note

EF's English Proficiency Index 2025 publishes no score for Huánuco, city or region. For Peru as a whole it gives 519, 52nd of 123 countries, in the moderate band, with Lima city at 555. We cannot tell you how strong English is in Huánuco classrooms, and we will not guess.

What we can say is which children usually manage. A child at a bilingual school, or one who already watches cartoons in English, follows from the first week. A child who has had only the school English hours may need a few weeks of patience, and a six year old with no English at all is usually happier starting a little later.

### How mentors make English easy

Mentors speak slowly, use short sentences and the same words for the same actions every week: "drag", "repeat", "when the flag is clicked". They show each step on screen before saying it, so a child can follow with their eyes.

When a child replies half in Spanish, the mentor understands and offers the English phrase back, kindly. Scratch blocks and Python keywords look identical in Lima, Huánuco or London, and most of an hour goes on making things work rather than on talk. For more on this, see [coding classes in English for Peruvian kids](/coding-classes-in-english-for-peruvian-kids).

Families often find the English is a second gift: an hour of building and explaining every class is an hour of listening and speaking with someone who waits for the answer. That is also why we never rush a young child, and why, after the free class, the mentor tells you both how keen your child was and how relaxed they seemed in English.

## How many children visited Kotosh in 2023?

Kotosh is the archaeological site with an exhibition hall that the Ministerio de Cultura runs in the district of Huánuco. The ministry publishes how many people visit each month, and our ten to twelve year olds use that file to practise one careful habit: before adding up a group, hunt down every place it might be hiding.

### The data

The Ministerio de Cultura releases a dataset called Museos through datosabiertos.gob.pe, the national open-data portal, with an ODC-By licence (Open Data Commons Attribution), so anyone may reuse it if they credit the ministry. Its visitor file, which we saved on 10 November 2026 as visitantes-museos.csv, has 21,014 rows: one per museum, per month and per kind of visitor, Peruvian or foreign, from 2007 to April 2024.

The 416 rows for the department of Huánuco all belong to one place, the Sala de Exhibición de la Zona Arqueológica Monumental de Kotosh. Each row splits visitors into adults, students, children, military visitors and older adults, and again into paying and free tickets.

### The question

We ask a group of eleven year olds a simple question: how many children visited Kotosh in 2023, and which month was busiest? Most guess the July holidays.

The first program reads every Huánuco row for 2023, adds up the column called NIN_PAGANTES, which means paying children, by month, and prints the total, the busiest month and the figure for March.

**Version 1: add up the children column** (Python)
```python
import csv

children = {}
with open("visitantes-museos.csv", encoding="latin-1") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f, delimiter=";"):
        if row["NOM_DPTO"] == "Huanuco" and row["ANIO"] == "2023":
            month = row["NOM_MES"]
            children[month] = children.get(month, 0) + int(row["NIN_PAGANTES"])

print("Children in 2023:", sum(children.values()))
busiest = max(children, key=children.get)
print("Busiest month:", busiest, children[busiest])
print("March:", children["Marzo"])
```
It prints: Children in 2023: 12666, then Busiest month: Enero 3660 and March: 245.

January wins, not July: January is the middle of Peru's summer school break, and families travel. That part of the answer holds. The total does not. When the children look at the column names again, they find NIN_ four times: paying children, children on a special paying ticket, children on a special free ticket, and children who entered free. Version 1 counted only one of the four.

**Version 2: find every children column by its name, then check the totals** (Python)
```python
import csv

children = {}
checked = 0
with open("visitantes-museos.csv", encoding="latin-1") as f:
    reader = csv.DictReader(f, delimiter=";")
    kid_columns = [name for name in reader.fieldnames if name.startswith("NIN_")]
    print("Columns with children:", kid_columns)
    for row in reader:
        if row["NOM_DPTO"] == "Huanuco" and row["ANIO"] == "2023":
            month = row["NOM_MES"]
            kids = sum(int(row[name]) for name in kid_columns)
            children[month] = children.get(month, 0) + kids
            groups = [name for name in reader.fieldnames
                      if name.split("_")[0] in ("ADU", "EST", "NIN", "MIL", "ADM")]
            if sum(int(row[name]) for name in groups) == int(row["TOTAL"]):
                checked += 1

print("Children in 2023:", sum(children.values()))
print("March:", children["Marzo"])
print("Rows that add up to TOTAL:", checked, "of 24")
```
It prints: Columns with children: ['NIN_BOLESPPAGANTES', 'NIN_PAGANTES', 'NIN_BOLESPNOPAGANTES', 'NIN_NOPAGANTES'], then Children in 2023: 13517, March: 355 and Rows that add up to TOTAL: 24 of 24.

Two ideas make Version 2 better. Instead of typing one column name, it asks the file for every column whose name starts with NIN_, so a column nobody remembered cannot be missed. Then it checks its own work: for every row it adds up all the visitor groups and compares the result with the file's TOTAL column. All 24 rows for 2023 agree, which tells us the groups really cover everyone. The answer rises from 12,666 to 13,517 children, and March from 245 to 355, because March is when school groups often enter free.

1. **Ages 6 to 7, with paper.** Draw four boxes and put a number of paper children in each. Count only one box, then count all four. Which answer is the real one?
2. **Ages 8 to 9, in Scratch.** Make four variables for the four kinds of children ticket and a fifth called total. Make the cat say the total only after all four are added.

4. **A step further.** Change Version 2 to count students, the EST_ columns, instead of children. Which month do students visit most, and why might that be?

## What time would a Huánuco child have class?

At the hour you choose. Huánuco keeps Peru time all year, and the mentor in India works around your child's day.

**How a Peru hour reads in India (example conversions, not a timetable)**

| Peru time, Huánuco | India time | Day in India |
|---|---|---|
| 08:00 | 18:30 | Same day |
| 10:00 | 20:30 | Same day |
| 12:30 | 23:00 | Same day |
| 16:00 | 02:30 | The next day |
| 17:30 | 04:00 | The next day |
| 19:00 | 05:30 | The next day |

Clock facts: Peru sits five hours behind UTC in every month and has no summer time (tz database, zone America/Lima; the official hour is kept by INACAL), while India sits five and a half hours ahead of UTC. So the gap stays at 10 hours 30 minutes all year.

Young children take in most when they are fresh, so many Huánuco families choose a morning slot in the school holidays and an afternoon slot once school is back. Because neither Peru nor India moves its clocks, an hour agreed in January is still the same hour in October. When you book the free class, you pick the hour in your own clock and the system converts it for the mentor.
