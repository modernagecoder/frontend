## Should a Lima university student learn Python or Java first?

Python first for almost everyone; Java first only if your degree already teaches it in the first year or you are sure you want backend or Android work.

### The case for Python

Python lets a first-year student write something useful in the first week: a script that renames files, a grade calculator, a chart from a CSV. The syntax gets out of the way, so attention goes to the logic.

It is also the language of data work in economics, engineering, the sciences and AI, which makes it useful to students well outside computing.

### The case for Java

Java makes you state types and structure classes from the start. Many computing degrees use it for object-oriented programming courses, and students who meet it early find those courses easier.

If your curriculum switches to Java in the second year, learn Python now and take our Java course the semester before. The ideas carry over; only the syntax is new.

Whichever you choose, the habits matter more than the language: small programs every week, tests before trust, and code you can explain line by line in English. Students already deep in a computing degree will find the specific path on [our Java course page for Peru](/java-course-peru) and [our Python course page for Peru](/python-course-peru).

## Why learn programming in English as a Lima student?

Because the field already works in English, and because your English is likely to grow fastest in exactly these years. We teach only in English, and always live.

### What the EF figures suggest

EF's English Proficiency Index 2025 gives Peruvians aged 18 to 20 a score of 469, in its low band, and those aged 26 to 30 a score of 559, in its high band. The same edition scores Lima city 555 and people working in IT 522.

These are different people, not the same people measured twice, so we read it cautiously: the years around university look like a time when English can grow fast. Practising it with code, where the vocabulary is narrow and repeated, is an efficient way to use that window.

Python's documentation, Stack Overflow answers, library READMEs and most video courses are in English. So are compiler errors, whatever the language of your operating system.

Mentors are based in India and teach in clear, steady English. They expect questions, repeat when asked, and help you say "this function returns a list of tuples" as naturally as you say it in Spanish.

## Do applicants really score higher on every retake at UNI?

UNI publishes anonymised data on its admission applicants as open data. We use it, in our own lesson, to teach a mistake that appears in research, business dashboards and student projects alike: comparing averages of groups whose members keep changing.

### The data

The Universidad Nacional de Ingeniería shares "Postulantes a los procesos de admisión" through Peru's national open-data portal, licensed ODC-By (Open Data Commons Attribution). The file Admision_UNI_2018-2_al_2020-1.csv covers four admission cycles, 2018-2 to 2020-1, in 30,358 rows.

Its data dictionary describes the id column as an anonymous identifier that "debería aparecer nuevamente con el mismo identificador" when a person applies more than once. That makes repeat applicants traceable without knowing who they are. We use only the ordinary exam route, rows with a score, and print only averages.

### The question

For each applicant we sort their attempts in time, then ask: how does the average score change from first attempt to second, third and fourth?

Version 1 does what most first analyses do. It puts every first attempt in one column, every second attempt in another, and compares the column averages.

**Version 1: average score by attempt number** (Python)
```python
import csv
from collections import defaultdict
from statistics import mean

tries = defaultdict(list)
with open("Admision_UNI_2018-2_al_2020-1.csv", encoding="utf-8") as data:
    for row in csv.DictReader(data):
        if row["modalidad"] == "ORDINARIO" and row["calificacion_final_postulante"]:
            when = (int(row["año_postulacion"]), int(row["ciclo_postulacion"]))
            tries[row["id"]].append((when, float(row["calificacion_final_postulante"])))

by_attempt = defaultdict(list)
for scores in tries.values():
    for n, (when, score) in enumerate(sorted(scores), start=1):
        by_attempt[n].append(score)

print("Applicants:", len(tries))
for n in sorted(by_attempt):
    print("Attempt", n, "rows", len(by_attempt[n]), "mean", round(mean(by_attempt[n]), 3))
```
It prints: Applicants: 11054, Attempt 1 rows 11054 mean 7.45, Attempt 2 rows 3888 mean 9.301, Attempt 3 rows 1312 mean 10.124 and Attempt 4 rows 305 mean 10.24.

Read naively, retakes help a lot at first and then barely at all: from attempt 3 to attempt 4 the average rises by only 0.116 points. But the columns contain different people. Only 305 applicants reach a fourth attempt, and they are not a random fifth of the 1,312 who made a third. Version 2 compares each person with themselves.

**Version 2: the same person, before and after** (Python)
```python
import csv
from collections import defaultdict
from statistics import mean

tries = defaultdict(list)
with open("Admision_UNI_2018-2_al_2020-1.csv", encoding="utf-8") as data:
    for row in csv.DictReader(data):
        if row["modalidad"] == "ORDINARIO" and row["calificacion_final_postulante"]:
            when = (int(row["año_postulacion"]), int(row["ciclo_postulacion"]))
            tries[row["id"]].append((when, float(row["calificacion_final_postulante"])))

for scores in tries.values():
    scores.sort()

for k in (1, 2, 3):
    pairs = [(s[k - 1][1], s[k][1]) for s in tries.values() if len(s) > k]
    stayed = [s[k - 1][1] for s in tries.values() if len(s) == k]
    gain = mean(b - a for a, b in pairs)
    print(f"Attempt {k} to {k + 1}: {len(pairs)} people, same-person gain {gain:.3f}")
    print(f"   at attempt {k}: returners {mean(a for a, b in pairs):.3f}, leavers {mean(stayed):.3f}")
```
It prints: Attempt 1 to 2: 3888 people, same-person gain 1.698, at attempt 1: returners 7.603, leavers 7.367, Attempt 2 to 3: 1312 people, same-person gain 1.043, at attempt 2: returners 9.081, leavers 9.413, Attempt 3 to 4: 305 people, same-person gain 0.856 and at attempt 3: returners 9.385, leavers 10.348.

**Two ways to measure the change between attempts (our calculation, ordinary route)**

| Step | Change in column averages | Average change for the same people | People in the pair |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attempt 1 to 2 | 1.851 | 1.698 | 3,888 |
| Attempt 2 to 3 | 0.823 | 1.043 | 1,312 |
| Attempt 3 to 4 | 0.116 | 0.856 | 305 |

Data: Universidad Nacional de Ingeniería, Postulantes a los procesos de admisión, 2018-2 to 2020-1, from the national open-data portal under an ODC-By licence. Every figure is our own, in points on the file's own score scale. The file also contains addresses and school names, which we neither use nor print. A "first attempt" here is the first within these four cycles; some applicants may have applied before 2018-2.

The last line explains everything. At attempt 3, the people who came back for a fourth try averaged 9.385; those who did not come back averaged 10.348. The column for attempt 4 is filled by weaker attempt-3 scorers, so its average looks flat even though those same people improved by 0.856 on average. Comparing crowds hid most of the change. This is the general rule: when membership changes between groups, compare matched pairs. It says nothing about whether anyone should retake an exam; that is a personal decision, and admission is decided by the university alone.

1. **Check the pairing.** Confirm no id appears twice in the same cycle in the ordinary route before trusting the sort.
2. **Add uncertainty.** Compute a 95% interval for the 0.856 gain with 305 pairs. Is it clearly above zero?
3. **Find the selection.** Group attempt-1 scores into bands and show, band by band, who returns.
4. **Write it in English.** Summarise the finding in three sentences a non-programmer could follow, without claiming cause.
5. **Generalise.** Name one dashboard at a company or university where the same trap could appear, and how you would pair the data.

## Coding for university students in Lima: questions and answers

### Are the classes in Spanish?

No. All classes are live, online and taught only in English. Mentors keep a steady pace, adapt to your accent, and help you put into English what you already understand in Spanish.

### I study at a Lima university. Do your classes count towards my degree?

No. We give no credits or grades and we are not connected with UNI, UNMSM, PUCP, the Universidad de Lima or any other university. Our classes are practice beside your degree.

### I have never programmed. Where do I start?

With the Python masterclass and the Git course. Both start from zero. After about three months most students move on to data structures.

### Will this get me a job?

We make no job or salary promises. We teach skills that software teams use and help you build projects you can show and explain; how you use them is up to you.

### Can I use AI tools in class?

Sometimes, and openly. You attempt problems yourself first, then compare with an AI suggestion and explain any difference. The aim is that you understand every line you submit.

### What time are classes?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. For the mentor in India it is always 10 hours 30 minutes later: 09:30 in Lima is 20:00 there.

### Can I pause during exams?

Yes. Plans are monthly. Pause during finals and restart when the semester allows.

### How much does it cost?

You pay monthly by card in the US dollars shown here, and your bank handles the change into soles. Browsing from India shows rupees instead. Your first class is free of charge.

### Do I get a certificate?

Some courses close with a completion certificate from us. It records your work; it is not a university credit or degree, and neither SUNEDU nor any university has a part in it.
