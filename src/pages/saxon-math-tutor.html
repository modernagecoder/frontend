Saxon Math tutor online, alongside the books you already use

Saxon Math has a reputation among homeschool and school families for being steady and relentless: a short new idea each day, then a long mixed set that keeps reaching back to old lessons. Many children thrive on it. Some reach a point, often in the middle courses or Algebra 1, where the mixed sets stop feeling like review and start feeling like a wall. This page explains how the program is designed, from the publisher’s own description, and how a live tutor can work with the Saxon book on the table rather than replacing it.

In short

Saxon Math is a K to 12 math program published by Houghton Mifflin Harcourt (HMH) that teaches in small daily increments and keeps every earlier lesson in cumulative practice and tests. A Saxon Math tutor helps a student who has stalled by finding which earlier lesson each missed problem depends on and reteaching it. How Saxon works

How does Saxon Math teach, according to its publisher?

HMH describes Saxon as incremental and cumulative: each lesson adds one small step, and practice keeps returning to everything taught before. Understanding that design is the first step to helping a student who is struggling with it.

The Saxon Math design in HMH’s own words (hmhco.com/programs/saxon-math, checked 31 October 2026)
FeatureWhat HMH says
Starting point"To master mathematics, students need to build on prior learning"
Increments"An incremental approach gives students the time to understand and practice a small concept before adding the next step."
Distribution"Distributing skills across the year ensures that students have time to practice and master a concept."
Review"Cumulative practice and assessments cover ideas from earlier lessons"
StrandsConcepts from every strand are "woven together and connected throughout the year"
Daily routine"Easy-to-follow daily lessons offer a consistent structure"
Summative tests"Power Up, Cumulative, Benchmark, and End-of-Year tests"
Ongoing checksFormative assessment "through daily instruction, lesson practice, and written practice"

What a lesson contains

HMH lists three parts to the classroom experience: Classroom Discourse (daily discussion and practice), Hands-On Learning with manipulatives, and Writing, where "written practice" gives students the chance to review and apply earlier concepts.

Online, the program adds Drawing Tools, "iTools digital math manipulatives", Calculator Activities and an eGlossary, and teachers get a Guide to Differentiated Instruction for planning.

Where the method comes from

HMH says the program’s authors work "Guided by the incremental approach developed by John Saxon." The name on the cover is the name of the method.

HMH’s homeschool page lists Saxon Math among its home math programs, beside Math in Focus and Head for Home Math Skills workbooks, which is why so many homeschool families meet it.

Where students stall

Why do some students get stuck in Saxon Math?

In our teaching experience, Saxon students rarely get stuck on the new lesson of the day. They get stuck on problem 18 of the mixed set, which quietly depends on something taught thirty lessons ago. The patterns below are general guidance from our classes, not statistics.

Common stall points with Saxon Math and what we do about them (our teaching notes)
What the parent seesWhat is usually underneathWhat helps
New lessons are fine, mixed sets are slowOne or two old lessons were never secureSort missed problems by the lesson that taught them
Test scores drop while daily work looks fineDaily sets were done with the lesson example openDo a few mixed problems closed-book each week
Tears at Algebra 1Negative numbers and fraction arithmetic are shakyRebuild integers and fractions before more algebra
Every problem gets the newest methodThe student guesses which lesson a problem belongs toPractise naming the idea before solving
Hours per lessonToo many problems attempted without help on the first errorStop after two misses on one idea and ask

Why the design causes this

Cumulative practice is exactly what HMH says it is: ideas from earlier lessons keep coming back. That is a gift to a student who understood them, and a slow leak for one who memorised a step and moved on.

Because the review is mixed, a gap does not show up as one bad lesson. It shows up as a scatter of wrong answers across many days, which is hard for a parent to diagnose from the gradebook.

What not to do

Do not skip the mixed practice to get through more lessons. The review is the program; without it Saxon becomes a thin textbook.

Do not restart the whole book either. A student who is stuck at lesson 64 usually needs three or four earlier lessons retaught, not sixty.

Worked examples

How does a tutor find the lesson behind a wrong answer?

Our method, shown on a made-up week of work. Every missed problem is traced to the lesson that first taught it, and the lessons that appear most often are the ones to reteach.

Four worked problems at Algebra 1 level, in the mixed style Saxon students meet (our problems and solutions)
ProblemIdea it depends onWorked solution
Simplify 3(2x - 5) - 4(x - 2)Distributing a negative6x - 15 - 4x + 8, so 2x - 7. The usual slip is -4 times -2 written as -8.
Solve 0.4x + 1.2 = 0.1x + 3Clearing decimalsMultiply every term by 10: 4x + 12 = x + 30, so 3x = 18 and x = 6.
Three consecutive odd integers add to 81. Find them.Writing an equation from wordsn + (n + 2) + (n + 4) = 81, so 3n + 6 = 81, n = 25: the numbers are 25, 27 and 29.
Solve (2/3)x - 5 = 7Undoing a fraction coefficientAdd 5: (2/3)x = 12. Multiply by 3/2: x = 18. Check: 12 - 5 = 7.

Naming the idea first

Before solving any mixed problem, we ask the student to say in a few words what kind of problem it is: "distributing a negative", "clear the decimals". It takes ten seconds and stops the habit of applying today’s method to everything.

When a student cannot name the idea, that is the gap, found before a single wrong answer is written.

Checking without the key

Each worked example above ends with a check a student can do alone: substitute the answer back, or estimate first. For x = 6, 0.4 times 6 plus 1.2 is 3.6, and 0.1 times 6 plus 3 is also 3.6.

A student who checks their own work needs the answer key less, and learns more from the times the check fails.

With a tutor

How does a live tutor work alongside Saxon Math?

The book stays in charge of the sequence. The tutor’s job is to keep the cumulative review working by repairing old lessons before the gaps spread.

  1. Bring the book and the last two weeks of work. The first class starts from your child’s current lesson and their recent mixed sets and tests.
  2. Trace the misses. The teacher sorts wrong answers by the lesson that taught them, the method shown above.
  3. Reteach the two or three lessons that matter. Usually with a different explanation from the book’s, so the idea lands a second way.
  4. Return to the current lesson. The student carries on in Saxon; we do not skip ahead or reorder the book.
  5. Watch the next Cumulative test. If the same lessons come back wrong, we revisit them; if not, we look for the next pattern.

A batch may suit

A student in the middle courses or Algebra 1 who needs steady weekly teaching of the same topics the book covers, with classmates working through similar material.

1-on-1 may suit

A homeschool student whose Saxon lesson number does not match a class, or one who needs several old lessons repaired quickly.

Saxon alone may suit

A student who checks their own work, reads the lesson carefully and keeps test scores steady. Many children never need a tutor for Saxon.

Homework stays the student’s own. We teach the idea and set similar problems written by us; we do not complete Saxon sets or tests for anyone, and we never share test answers.

Stage 2 is the most common reason families call us. It is also the easiest to fix, because the gaps are few and findable.

Saxon Math tutoring: common questions

Can you tutor my child in Saxon Math online?

Yes. Our teachers work live by video with the student's own Saxon book, starting from the current lesson and recent mixed sets. Classes run as a batch of 15 to 20 or one to one.

Who publishes Saxon Math?

Saxon Math is published by Houghton Mifflin Harcourt (HMH). Its program page describes an incremental approach with cumulative practice and assessment, from kindergarten to Algebra 2. We are not connected with HMH.

Why is Saxon Math so hard for some children?

Its mixed practice keeps returning to earlier lessons, so one insecure idea shows up as scattered wrong answers for weeks. In our experience the fix is to find and reteach those few lessons, not to slow the whole book down.

What tests does Saxon Math use?

HMH lists Power Up, Cumulative, Benchmark and End-of-Year tests as its summative assessments, with formative checks through daily lessons and written practice.

Which Saxon levels do you help with?

All of them, but most families come to us from the middle courses, Algebra 1, Geometry or Algebra 2. Younger children are taught through our elementary course with the Saxon lessons beside it.

Should my child skip the mixed practice to catch up?

We would not. The cumulative review is how Saxon is designed to work. It is better to repair the old lessons behind the misses so the review starts helping again.

Will you do my child's Saxon homework or tests?

No. We teach the idea behind a problem and set similar problems of our own. Homework, tests and grades remain the student's work.

My child's book has a different title from the levels you list. Is that a problem?

No. Editions and titles vary. Send a photo of the contents page and a recent test and the teacher will place your child by what the lessons cover.

When are classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

Is the first class free?

Yes. The first class is free and needs no card details. Bring the Saxon book and the last week or two of work.
