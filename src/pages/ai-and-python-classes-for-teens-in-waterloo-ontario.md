# AI and Python classes for teens in Waterloo

**Where can a Waterloo teenager learn Python and AI properly?** Modern Age Coders teaches Python and AI live online to Waterloo teens aged 13 to 18, either in a group of fifteen to twenty students or with a private teacher. Most start with Python for Teens, then move to AI and Machine Learning for Teens, CCC preparation or the Ontario Computer Science Companion. Lessons are booked in Eastern time, and the first is free.

Few cities put a teenager closer to Canadian computing than Waterloo. The University of Waterloo&rsquo;s Centre for Education in Mathematics and Computing, known as CEMC, writes the Canadian Computing Competition, hosts the Canadian Computing Olympiad and publishes free Python lessons, and a student here can use every one of them from a laptop at home. Being near all that does not, on its own, teach anyone to program. What turns it into skill is steady practice with someone who reads the code back and asks why. This page sets out what CEMC offers a Waterloo teen in 2026 to 2027, quoting CEMC, what Ontario computer studies courses expect, which AI tools a teenager may legally use, and a first machine learning project we built on the City of Waterloo&rsquo;s own trail counter records.

## Can a model guess tomorrow&rsquo;s traffic on the Laurel Trail? A first machine learning project

The City of Waterloo publishes hourly counts from counters on its trails. Our students download one counter&rsquo;s history, train three very simple models on 2018 and test them on 2019. Every number in this section is our own calculation, not the City&rsquo;s.

**Our results on City of Waterloo Trail Counter Data, counter LOC_ID 4, Laurel Trail at University Ave. W. (44,114 hourly rows fetched 22 October 2026; trained on 356 complete days in 2018, tested on 360 complete days in 2019)**

| Model (trained on 2018) | How it guesses a 2019 day | Average miss on 2019 (people per day) |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | The same number every day: the 2018 daily average, about 171 | 90 |
| Weekday model | The 2018 average for that day of the week | 90 |
| Month model | The 2018 average for that month, from about 59 a day in January to 324 in July | 65 |

Here is the whole project in standard-library Python. It pages through the City&rsquo;s public query service 2,000 rows at a time, adds the hourly counts up by date, keeps only dates with all 24 hours, and scores each model by its mean absolute error. It printed the three numbers in the table when we ran it on 22 October 2026:

```
import json, urllib.request, datetime

URL = ("https://services.arcgis.com/ZpeBVw5o1kjit7LT/arcgis/rest/services/"
       "City_of_Waterloo_Trail_Counter_Data/FeatureServer/0/query"
       "?where=LOC_ID%3D4&outFields=DATE,TOTAL_COUNT&orderByFields=OBJECTID"
       "&resultRecordCount=2000&f=json&resultOffset=")

daily, hours, offset = {}, {}, 0
while True:
    page = json.load(urllib.request.urlopen(URL + str(offset)))
    rows = page["features"]
    if not rows:
        break
    for r in rows:
        a = r["attributes"]
        day = datetime.datetime.fromtimestamp(a["DATE"] / 1000, datetime.timezone.utc).date()
        daily[day] = daily.get(day, 0) + (a["TOTAL_COUNT"] or 0)
        hours[day] = hours.get(day, 0) + 1
    offset += len(rows)

full = [d for d in daily if hours[d] == 24]
train = [(d, daily[d]) for d in full if d.year == 2018]
test = [(d, daily[d]) for d in full if d.year == 2019]

def fit(key):
    groups = {}
    for d, n in train:
        groups.setdefault(key(d), []).append(n)
    return {k: sum(v) / len(v) for k, v in groups.items()}

def error(key):
    model = fit(key)
    return round(sum(abs(model[key(d)] - n) for d, n in test) / len(test))

print(offset, "hourly rows,", len(train), "train days,", len(test), "test days")
print("same guess every day:", error(lambda d: 0))
print("guess by weekday:", error(lambda d: d.weekday()))
print("guess by month:", error(lambda d: d.month))
```

### 1. Beat the baseline

A model is only interesting if it beats a dumb guess. Guessing the yearly average every day misses by 90 people a day on 2019. Knowing the weekday does not help at all here, which surprises most students; knowing the month cuts the miss to 65.

### 2. Split by time

Training on 2018 and testing on 2019 is honest in a way a random split is not: the model never sees the future it is graded on. Students then try training on 2017 to see whether a longer history helps.

### 3. Audit the labels

The same counter can carry two spellings. In the City&rsquo;s file, LOC_ID 2 appears as both &ldquo;Iron Horse Tail at John St. W.&rdquo; and &ldquo;Iron Horse Trail at John St. W.&rdquo;, and LOC_ID 10 as both &ldquo;Spur Line Trail at Roger Street&rdquo; and &ldquo;Spurline Trail South of Roger St.&rdquo;. Group by ID, never by name.

### What the model cannot know

The 2019 days averaged about 133 people against 171 in 2018, so every 2018 model guesses high. Is that a real change in walking and cycling, a counter moved or partly blocked, or something else? The data alone cannot say. The City&rsquo;s own note warns that construction meant traffic &ldquo;has been diverted around many EcoCounter installations resulting in a perceived drop in traffic&rdquo;, without naming this counter. And 15 April 2018, 14 September 2019 and 15 September 2019 record zero people across all 24 hours, which looks more like a counter gap than an empty trail. A student who flags those days before trusting the error figure is doing real data science.

Two more honest details. The item description says &ldquo;This data is updated daily&rdquo;, yet the newest rows we could fetch are dated 30 November 2021, so this is a historical exercise, not a live feed. The whole table held 534,489 rows across all counters on the day we queried it; we used one counter so a laptop finishes in seconds. The data is used under the Waterloo Open Data User Licence, which lets anyone &ldquo;use the Information commercially&rdquo;. Contains information provided by the City of Waterloo under licence ([licence](https://data.waterloo.ca/pages/open-data-licence), [dataset](https://data.waterloo.ca/datasets/5d41afff252e45b5b5fe7fc3fd5df3ab_0)). The City has not endorsed this page or our teaching.

The CCC rule is the one that changes how a teen should practise. Students who learned with an assistant always on tend to freeze at that point. So our contest lessons run with AI tools off from the first week, and our machine learning lessons treat an AI tool as something to test, not something to trust.

### Parents decide on ChatGPT

OpenAI puts the permission decision with the parent until 18. We tell families exactly where in a course a tool would be used, and a teen whose parent says no loses nothing: every lesson works without it.

### Adults only, for some tools

Claude is an 18-and-over product under Anthropic&rsquo;s terms, so it never appears in a lesson for teenagers. The agentic coding course in the row of four above is an adult course for the same reason, and we do not place teens in it.

### Build the model yourself

The trail counter project uses no AI service at all. Fitting averages by hand is the start of machine learning, and a teen who can explain why the month model wins understands more than one who pasted a prompt.

## Why a Waterloo teen should write Python before asking AI to

Because the hardest part of the trail project is not the code; it is noticing what is wrong with the result.

An AI assistant will happily write the trail counter script. It will not stop to ask why 2019 runs low, whether a zero day is a broken counter, or why the same ID carries two names. Those questions come from a student who has written the loop, seen the output and felt something was off. That instinct is what we are trying to build.

So we teach thinking first: break the problem into steps, write each step, predict the output, then check it. AI tools come in later, within the age rules above, as something a student questions the way a reviewer questions a draft. A teen who learns that way can use any tool that appears in the next five years; one who learned by pasting cannot.

The CCC rules ban AI helpers for exactly this reason: on contest day the thinking has to be the student&rsquo;s own. The longer argument is in [is coding still worth learning in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).

Yes, online and live. There is no Waterloo centre: teenagers from 13 to 18 log in from home, either to a group of fifteen to twenty or to private lessons with their own teacher.

For Junior and most of Senior, yes. CEMC itself warns that a perfect score may not always be possible in Python or Java, so students aiming at the top Senior problems or the CCO add C++.
