## What do Koblenz schools teach about computing, and when?

Rhineland-Palatinate is in a period of change. For now, computing is optional for most pupils; from the 2028/29 school year it becomes a subject at every school.

### Today: optional for most pupils

In its Informatik-Monitor 2026/27 the Gesellschaft für Informatik places Rhineland-Palatinate in a group of six Länder where no Informatik lesson is binding on every pupil. A Koblenz child spends grades 1 to 4 at Grundschule, as in most of Germany, and there is no fixed computing lesson in those years.

Since 2021/22, a group of 21 Informatik-Profil-Schulen across the Land has taught compulsory Informatik in grade 5. Whether a child attends one of them depends on the school, not on the city.

### From 2028/29: a subject for all

The same monitor reports the plan: from the 2028/29 school year Informatik becomes a compulsory subject at all schools in Rhineland-Palatinate, with four weekly hours in total across the years it is taught.

A child who is 6 now will meet it in secondary school. A child who is 11 now may finish lower secondary just as it arrives. Either way, the years from 6 to 12 are open, and they are the years when puzzles and games make thinking in steps feel like play.

## How does a live class in English work for a young child?

Short, friendly and hands-on. The child does the clicking and typing; the mentor watches, encourages and asks questions.

### Only English, explained simply

We teach in English and nothing else, and we say so before any trial. Mentors use short sentences, show rather than tell, and repeat in easier words. A Koblenz child who knows only a few English words still follows, because most of the class happens on the screen.

### The same face every week

Each child has one mentor, based in India, who learns how that child thinks: who races ahead, who goes quiet before an answer, who would rather tinker with a finished game than start from an empty screen. Younger children relax quickly when the person on the screen stays the same.

### Blocks first, then words

Children of 6 to 8 snap Scratch blocks together and make sprites move and talk. From about 9, children type Python. The code words, repeat, if, print, are English, so lessons and code reinforce each other.

A class lasts an hour, twice a week. For six and seven year olds the mentor splits the hour into short tasks with a small win every ten minutes. Parents receive a short note on what was built and what comes next. Our [online coding for kids page](/online-coding-classes-for-kids-germany) explains the format in more detail.

## How often did the Rhine at Koblenz drop below its zero mark?

The federal waterways administration measures the Rhine at Koblenz every fifteen minutes and anyone may reuse what it publishes. Children of ten to twelve take one month of those numbers to learn a question programmers ask all the time: am I counting readings, or am I counting events?

### The data

PEGELONLINE is the federal service that publishes water levels on German waterways under the Datenlizenz Deutschland Zero 2.0. On 3 November 2026 we downloaded the last 31 days for the Rhine gauge called KOBLENZ, at Rhine kilometre 591.49: 2,976 readings in centimetres, one every quarter of an hour, from the evening of 3 October 2026 to the evening of 3 November 2026.

They are raw readings, published as they arrive and not yet checked. We saved them as rhein_koblenz.csv with two columns: the time and the level.

### A strange number

Children are surprised that the level sometimes reads below zero. The river has not disappeared. A gauge measures from its own fixed mark, called the gauge zero, which at Koblenz sits 57.692 metres above sea level; at low water the surface can dip below that mark.

So the question is: in this month, how often did the Rhine go below zero? A first program says one thing, a second program says something very different, and both are correct about different questions.

**Version 1: count the readings** (Python)
```python
import csv

with open("rhein_koblenz.csv") as f:
    readings = [(row["time"], int(row["cm"])) for row in csv.DictReader(f)]

print("Readings:", len(readings))
print("Lowest:", min(cm for t, cm in readings), "cm  Highest:", max(cm for t, cm in readings), "cm")

below = [t for t, cm in readings if cm < 0]
print("Readings below the zero mark:", len(below))
```
It prints Readings: 2976, Lowest: -4 cm Highest: 101 cm and Readings below the zero mark: 157.

A child might say "the Rhine went below zero 157 times". But a reading every fifteen minutes means one long dip produces many readings. Picture a light that stays on for an hour: if you look every fifteen minutes you see it on four times, yet it was switched on once. To count switch-ons, you look for the moment it changes from off to on.

**Version 2: count the moments it crossed the line** (Python)
```python
dips = []
was_below = False
for t, cm in readings:
    now_below = cm < 0
    if now_below and not was_below:      # it has just crossed the line
        dips.append(t)
    was_below = now_below

print("Times the river went below the zero mark:", len(dips))
for t in dips:
    print("  ", t[:16])
```
It prints Times the river went below the zero mark: 5, then 2026-10-06T02:00, 2026-10-06T11:45, 2026-10-06T14:30, 2026-10-06T21:00 and 2026-10-07T15:30.

**The Rhine at Koblenz, 3 October to 3 November 2026: readings and events (our count)**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Readings in the month | 2,976 |
| Readings below the zero mark | 157 |
| Dips below the zero mark (events) | 5, all on 6 and 7 October 2026 |
| Lowest and highest reading | -4 cm and 101 cm |
| First reading at 50 cm or more | 25 October 2026, 12:00 |
| Back below 50 cm | 30 October 2026, 14:45 |

Data: Wasserstraßen- und Schifffahrtsverwaltung des Bundes, PEGELONLINE, gauge KOBLENZ (Rhine) and gauge KOBLENZ OP (Mosel), raw water levels, Datenlizenz Deutschland Zero 2.0, downloaded 3 November 2026. The counts are ours.

All five dips happened on 6 and 7 October 2026, when the river hovered just around its zero mark and wobbled across it again and again. Later in the month the level rose: it reached 50 centimetres at midday on 25 October 2026 and stayed above that until the afternoon of 30 October. That was one rise, one event, even though it lasted for hundreds of readings. On 25 October the file even holds 100 readings instead of 96, because German clocks went back an hour that night and the day had 25 hours.

There is a second river in the data. The Mosel gauge KOBLENZ OP, about two kilometres up the Mosel from where it joins the Rhine, read between 196 and 209 centimetres all month. That does not mean the Mosel is two metres deeper than the Rhine: each gauge has its own zero mark, and the Mosel's is at 63.075 metres above sea level. Comparing the two raw numbers would be like comparing two children's heights measured from different steps.

1. **Ages 6 to 8, no screen.** Switch a lamp on and off a few times while a friend looks every ten seconds and draws a tick when it is on. How many ticks? How many times was it switched on?
2. **Ages 8 to 10, Scratch.** Store a list of numbers, some below zero. Make a sprite say "dip!" only when a number is below zero and the one before it was not.
3. **Ages 10 to 12, Python.** Run both versions, then change Version 2 to count the times the Rhine rose above 50 centimetres. You should find one.
4. **Going further.** Work out how long each dip lasted, in minutes, by counting its readings and multiplying by 15.

## Can a Koblenz child take part in the Informatik-Biber?

Usually yes, if the school signs up. It is a half-hour quiz of logic puzzles on screen, no programming required, and the 2026 round starts within days.

### The basics

Bundesweite Informatikwettbewerbe organises the Informatik-Biber. Entry costs nothing, pupils from grade 3 to grade 13 may take part, and nobody needs to know anything in advance. A primary class gets nine puzzles and half an hour, working alone or two together. This year's Biber weeks are 9 to 20 November 2026.

Entry is through the school, which books its classes and runs the quiz during lesson time. A short question to the class teacher tells you whether a Koblenz school is joining.
