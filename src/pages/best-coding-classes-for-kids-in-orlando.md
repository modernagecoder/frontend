## Little facts about Orlando a kid can turn into code

From the City of Orlando&rsquo;s own website, read on 18 October 2026. Each fact comes with a tiny programming idea.

**City of Orlando facts and a coding idea for each (facts from orlando.gov; ideas are ours)**

| What the city says | A small coding idea |
|---|---|
| Orlando&rsquo;s history dates back to 1838 | Subtract years: how old is the story of Orlando in 2026? |
| The early settlement was known as Jernigan, with a post office established May 30, 1850 | Store a date and print it in three formats |
| In 1857, the U.S. Post Office adopted the name Orlando | Make a timeline list and sort it |
| The Town of Orlando was incorporated in 1875 with 85 inhabitants, 22 of whom were qualified voters | Work out a percentage, then round it |
| There are four stories that are told about where the name came from | A quiz game that picks a random story |
| As of 2026, the city&rsquo;s population is 339,999 residents | Compare it with 85 people in 1875 |
| More than 148 parks, gardens, recreation areas, neighborhood centers and playgrounds | A list of favourite parks, with a search box |
| A trail network of over 40 miles | A trip planner that adds up distances |
| The Lake Eola sidewalk circles the lake 0.9 miles in length | How many laps make a 5K? Loop and count |
| Swan Boats run Tuesday to Sunday and are closed on Mondays | An if statement: can we ride today? |
| GreenUp Orlando was created in 1985 to increase the tree canopy | Draw a tree with turtle graphics, then a forest |

This is how we like to start with young children: with something they already know. A child who has walked around Lake Eola understands 0.9 miles in a way no textbook number can match. Asking how many laps make five kilometres needs a loop, a counter and a stopping rule, which are three of the most important ideas in programming, wrapped inside a question about a place they have been.

## What Florida says young children should get

Our plain-English reading of section 1007.2616 of the Florida Statutes, as published by the Florida Legislature and checked on 18 October 2026.

Florida law defines computer science as &ldquo;the study of computers and algorithmic processes&rdquo;, including how hardware and software are designed, how they are used and how they affect society, and it says coding and programming are part of it. For younger children the statute is permissive rather than strict: school opportunities may include coding instruction in elementary school and middle school, and elementary and middle schools may set up digital classrooms where students practise skills such as coding and working with images. Firm course requirements start in middle and high school. Further up the ladder, fewer than half of Florida high schools had a foundational course in 2023 to 2024: 463 of 1,192, according to Code.org (2025), 2025 State of AI + CS Education. For an Orlando parent of an eight-year-old, that means early coding at school is possible but not guaranteed, which is where an outside class helps.

## How did Orlando grow? A decade-by-decade histogram

The City of Orlando publishes a record of every annexation, each time the city took in new land, with the year it happened. Children count them by decade and draw the bars themselves.

**Orlando annexation records by decade (our count of the city&rsquo;s Orlando Annexations dataset, downloaded 18 October 2026)**

| Decade | Annexation records | Text bar, one # per 10 records |
|---|---|---|
| 1950s | 83 | ######## |
| 1960s | 168 | ################ |
| 1970s | 178 | ################# |
| 1980s | 114 | ########### |
| 1990s | 112 | ########### |
| 2000s | 229 | ###################### |
| 2010s | 80 | ######## |
| 2020s, so far | 48 | #### |

### 1. Count the rows

The dataset holds 1,012 records from 1950 to 2026. A child loads the year column and checks the first and last years before doing anything else, because checking the edges is how good programmers start.

### 2. Make the bins

Integer division does the magic: year // 10 * 10 turns 1973 into 1970 and 2002 into 2000. A dictionary then counts how many records land in each decade.

### 3. Draw the bars

Printing one # for every ten records gives the text histogram above. Older children redraw it with matplotlib and compare: does the picture tell the same story as the numbers?

The busiest single years in the file are 1973, with 55 records, and 2002, with 48. The 2000s bin is the tallest, at 229. Then comes the question we always ask: does the shortest bar mean Orlando stopped growing in the 2020s? No. That decade has only run from 2020 to 2026, so its bin is unfinished. Spotting an incomplete bin is a grown-up data skill, and nine-year-olds get it once they see it.

### Our counts, not official statistics

These are our counts of records in one download, not the city&rsquo;s official history of its growth. One annexation record can be a tiny lot or a large tract, so more records does not always mean more land, and 78 of the records before 1970 list zero acres, which most likely means the size was not recorded. The City of Orlando states no licence on this dataset; we link to it and quote counts only.

That zero is a lesson for older kids too. If they add up the acres column, the early decades will look tiny, not because Orlando grew slowly but because the information is missing. A child who learns to ask whether zero means none or unknown has learned something many adults never do.

## Should an Orlando child learn to code if AI can do it?

Yes, for the same reason children still learn arithmetic after calculators.

A child who asks an AI to draw the annexation histogram will get a picture. They will not know that the last bar is unfinished, or that the zeros in the acres column mean missing information. A child who wrote the twelve lines of Python that make the bars will notice both, because they saw every number go in.

So we teach AI as part of coding, not instead of it. Children learn what an AI tool is, which ones are meant for their age, and how to check what it says. The tools come after the thinking, never before it.

The aim for a young Orlando coder is simple: build things, ask questions, and never take a confident answer on trust. The longer argument is in [is coding still worth learning in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
