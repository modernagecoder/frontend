Coding classes for kids in Canada

Most Canadian children meet code at school before a parent goes looking for a class. This page starts from what those provincial documents actually say, then shows how we place a child aged 6 to 12 in a live online class, what a ten year old can build with real city data, a set of puzzles to try at the kitchen table, and the contests open to Canadian children this school year.

What school already asks

Coding in Canadian elementary schools, province by province

Education belongs to each province, so a nine year old in Ottawa and a nine year old in Kamloops are on different plans. These are the lines we could read in each government's own documents on 22 October 2026. Where we could not reach a primary source, we say so rather than guess.

What the Ontario ladder means at home

Read the Ontario lines top to bottom and a sequence appears: first a set of steps in order, then steps that repeat, then decisions, then a program judged on how well it is written. The curriculum asks children to read, change and write code at each stage, inside math class, not in a separate computer period.

For a parent the useful question is which rung the child is on now. A Grade 4 student who cannot yet predict what a loop will do is not ready for the Grade 6 idea of efficiency, whatever the report card says.

What British Columbia and Alberta add

In the BC pages we read, coding shows up in a hands-on design module in Grades 6 and 7, where a child may build in a visual tool such as Kodu or Scratch. The ministry adds that "Schools may choose from among the modules listed below or develop new modules", so two BC children in the same grade can have very different years.

Alberta is mid-change. Its government says the new K to 6 math is in classrooms and that the draft for Grades 7 to 9 brings in computer science and artificial intelligence, with mandatory use planned for September 2027. A child now in Grade 4 or 5 in Calgary will meet that draft in junior high.

Sources: Ontario curriculum, elementary mathematics; BC curriculum, ADST 6; Alberta, curriculum: how we got here. All read 22 October 2026. Placing a child

What coding looks like at 6, at 9 and at 12

Age is a starting guess, not a rule. Reading speed, patience with a mistake and comfort with a keyboard decide more than the birthday. This is how our teachers place a child in the first class; the stages are ours, not a ministry's.

Our placement guide for children aged 6 to 12 (Modern Age Coders guidance)

Why we start younger children on blocks

A six year old can hold a sequence of steps in mind long before typing is comfortable. Blocks take spelling out of the way, so the lesson is about order and cause: this block runs, then this one, and the cat ends up in the wrong place because of that turn.

We keep sessions for the youngest short and busy, with the teacher reading every instruction aloud. A parent nearby helps, especially in the first month.

Why we move to typed code by about ten

Blocks hide the moment a program is read as text, and that moment matters. By ten, most children can type a short line accurately, and Python lets them read the line back like a sentence: if the ground is soft and the child is little, then print the name.

The switch is gradual. For a few weeks the same idea is built twice, once in blocks and once typed, until the typed version feels like the quicker one.

A real data project

The Edmonton playground picker: three questions joined with "and"

The City of Edmonton publishes a list of its playgrounds under an open licence that allows commercial use. We use it with children aged 10 to 12 to teach one idea properly: a computer can check several conditions at once, and the small word joining them changes everything. The program and every number below are ours, counted from the city's file downloaded on 22 October 2026.

The question the child answers: a family with a toddler who uses a wheelchair wants a playground with soft rubber ground. Which Edmonton playgrounds fit all three? The file has one row per playground, with columns such as Name, Surface Type, Accessibility, User Category and Neighbourhood Name.

How a class runs this project

Nobody types first. The teacher shows ten rows of the file on screen, and the children sort them by hand into "fits" and "does not fit", saying out loud which check each row failed. Only then do they write the three conditions, one per line, and name each one in plain words: little_kids, soft_ground, wheelchair.

Then they guess. Before running the program, each child writes down how many playgrounds they expect. Guesses are usually far off in one direction or the other, and the gap between guess and answer starts the liveliest conversation of the lesson.

What the child should be able to say afterwards

"And means every check must pass; or means one is enough." "An equals sign is too strict when a cell holds a list." "Unknown is not the same as no." Three sentences, each tested against real numbers the child produced.

Those are the same ideas the Ontario Grade 5 expectation calls conditional statements, met here on a question a real Alberta family could ask.

Try it at home

Six "and, or, not" puzzles for the kitchen table

We wrote these for parents and children to do together, with no computer. Each one uses the same idea as the playground program. Answers follow the list; cover them first.

  1. The mitten drawer. A drawer holds 4 red mittens and 3 blue mittens, and 2 of the red ones have holes. How many mittens are red and have no holes?
  2. Snow day rule. A made-up school closes if snow is over 20 cm or the temperature is below minus 35. Monday: 25 cm and minus 10. Tuesday: 5 cm and minus 38. Wednesday: 10 cm and minus 20. Which days does it close?
  3. The same rule with and. Now the school closes only if snow is over 20 cm and it is below minus 35. Using the same three days, which days close?
  4. Library line-up. Children may borrow a graphic novel if they are at least 8 and not owing a book. Maya is 9 and owes one. Liam is 7 and owes none. Ava is 10 and owes none. Who can borrow?
  5. Hockey cards. A child keeps a card if the team is from Canada or the player is a goalie. Out of 10 cards, 6 are Canadian teams and 3 are goalies, and 2 of those goalies play for Canadian teams. How many cards are kept?
  6. Write your own. Choose three facts about your street, such as "has a tree", "has a red door", "has a number over 50". Write one rule with and and one with or, then count the houses each rule keeps. Which rule kept more, and why must it?

Answers. 1: two (4 red minus 2 with holes). 2: Monday and Tuesday, because each passes one check. 3: none, because no day passes both. 4: Ava only; Maya owes a book and Liam is under 8. 5: seven; count the 6 Canadian cards, then add only the goalie who is not already counted (3 goalies minus the 2 already in). 6: the or rule always keeps at least as many houses as the and rule, because every house that passes both checks also passes one of them.

Puzzle 5 is the one adults get wrong most often: adding 6 and 3 to make 9 counts two cards twice. It is the same mistake a program makes when it counts a playground once for each condition it passes.

How a child enters the BCC

Through a school only. CEMC says "all students must participate through their school", so a parent's first step is an email to the child's teacher or principal, well before the October ordering deadline. CEMC suggests another local school if the child's own does not take part.

The BCC needs no programming. Its multiple-choice puzzles, in CEMC's words, only ask for familiarity with the math every Canadian province teaches, so it suits a child who has done some coding and one who never has.

Reading last year's numbers sensibly

CEMC's 2025 table shows 14 260 children writing the Grade 5/6 paper with an average of 37.22 out of 60, which is about 62 percent (our arithmetic: 37.22 / 60). A typical child got most questions right and some wrong, which is the point of a well-made puzzle contest.

We prepare children with puzzles we write ourselves and send families to CEMC's free past contests for the real thing. We never copy CEMC's questions onto our pages.

Thinking first

Children who explain their code, not children who copy it

Our promise to families anywhere in the world is the same: we teach children to think through a problem and solve it, not to copy an answer from a video or an AI tool. For a child of 6 to 12 that promise has a very practical shape.

Predict, then run

Before any program runs, the child says what it will do. A wrong prediction is the most useful thing that can happen in a lesson, because it shows exactly which idea is still fuzzy.

Read it back aloud

Every line a child writes, they can read back as a sentence. "If the ground is soft and the child is little" is a sentence; a line the child cannot say aloud is a line they do not own yet.

Fix your own bug

The teacher points to where a problem is, never types the fix. Finding the bug is the skill; the corrected program is only the receipt.

Where AI tools fit for children

Mostly, they do not, and the companies say so. OpenAI's help centre states that "ChatGPT is not meant for children under 13", and Anthropic's terms require Claude users to be at least 18. Children in our classes write their own code with a teacher. Our courses never ask a child to open an account on a service that does not allow their age, and the youngest see AI tools only in demonstrations the teacher runs.

Children do learn what AI is: how a program can learn a rule from examples and where it goes wrong. That is taught with tools and exercises chosen for their age, and our separate page on AI classes for kids covers it.

What a parent sees after each class

A short note: what the child built, the idea underneath it, and one question to ask at dinner, such as "what does or do in your program?" A child who can answer that has learned something a screen recording cannot show.

Over a term the notes add up to a picture of how the child thinks, which matters more than the number of projects finished.

Four rungs

From first block to first real program

The steps we watch for, whatever the child's age. They are ours, used to place a child fairly.

Our four rungs for children aged 6 to 12
RungWhat it looks like
1. OrderPuts steps in the right order and spots one that is out of place
2. RepeatUses a loop instead of copying the same block five times
3. DecideWrites a rule with if, and can say what happens when it is false
4. CombineJoins conditions with and, or and not, and tests each on purpose

For parents

A child can sit on one rung for a whole term and that is fine. Rushing to rung 4 makes children who type confidently and understand little.

If your child is in Ontario, rungs 1 to 3 line up roughly with the Grade 1 to Grade 5 coding lines in math.

For children

Say what your program will do before you press run. If you are wrong, you just found out something.

When you are stuck, read your code aloud slowly. Bugs are shy; they come out when they are read.

Coding for kids in Canada: questions from parents

What age can a child start coding classes?

Our Coding for Kids and Scratch courses take children from age 6, grouped by age. The youngest work in picture blocks with the teacher reading instructions aloud. Most children move to typed Python at about 10.

Do Canadian schools already teach coding to kids?

It depends on the province. Ontario puts coding in elementary math from Grade 1, under expectation C3.1. British Columbia has design modules in Grades 6 and 7 that can include visual programming such as Kodu or Scratch. Alberta's draft curriculum for Grades 7 to 9 adds computer science and AI.

Is Scratch or Python better for my child?

Scratch for most children under 9, because blocks take typing and spelling out of the way. Python from about 10, when a child can type a line accurately and read it back. Our teachers place each child in the first class rather than by age alone.

Will these classes follow my child's school curriculum?

They sit alongside it. For Ontario children we know which coding lines in math each grade meets, and we teach the same ideas in more depth. We are not connected with any ministry and do not replace school coding.

Can a child aged 6 manage a live online class?

Yes, with a short, busy session and a parent close by for the first few weeks. Six and seven year olds are grouped together, and the teacher keeps the instructions spoken and simple.

What is the Beaver Computing Challenge?

A short puzzle contest run by CEMC at the University of Waterloo. The Grade 5/6 level is for students in Grade 6 or below: 12 multiple-choice questions out of 60 in 45 minutes. It needs no programming, and children enter through their school.

Do kids use ChatGPT or other AI tools in your classes?

Not on accounts of their own. OpenAI says ChatGPT is not meant for children under 13, and Claude requires users to be 18 or older. Our courses never ask a child to sign up for a service that does not allow their age; children write and explain their own code and learn how AI works through teacher-run demonstrations and exercises.

What times are the classes for Canadian families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. We book every class in your own province's time zone.

How big are the groups?

A batch has fifteen to twenty children of a similar age with one regular teacher. One-to-one classes are also available for a child who wants a teacher to themselves.

What will my child actually build?

In the first months, small Scratch games with rules the child invents. Older children build programs that answer real questions, such as our Edmonton playground picker, which reads the city's open data and keeps the playgrounds that pass three checks.
