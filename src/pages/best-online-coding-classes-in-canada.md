## Seven criteria for judging a coding class in Canada

These are our criteria, written so that a parent can check each one without trusting anybody's marketing, including ours. A class does not need a perfect score on all seven, but the first and the last matter more than the rest.

**Seven criteria for a coding class, with the check a family can run (our framework)**

| Criterion | Why it matters | How to check it yourself |
|---|---|---|
| 1. A live teacher | A recording cannot notice that a child has stopped understanding. A person can, and can change the next question on the spot. | Ask whether the same teacher leads every class, and whether the child's screen is watched while they code. |
| 2. A stated class size | Size decides how often your child is asked to explain their own code. A provider that will not give a number is telling you something. | Ask for the maximum, not the average, and whether it changes in busy months. |
| 3. The right age band | A seven-year-old and a twelve-year-old need different tools, pace and kinds of project. | Look for a published age range for each course, and ask how batches are grouped. |
| 4. Curriculum fit by province | Ontario puts coding inside math from Grade 1; British Columbia, Alberta and others teach it in other places. A class that knows this can help with school, not just alongside it. | Name your child's grade and province, and ask what their school course expects this year. |
| 5. Published fees | You should know the monthly fee before a sales call, not after. | Look for a fee on the provider's own site. If there is none, ask for it in writing. |
| 6. A route to contests | Canada has free and low-cost contests, run through schools, that reward real problem solving. | Ask which contests the teacher has prepared students for, and how practice problems are chosen. |
| 7. Thinking over copying | A child who copies code, from a screen or from an AI tool, learns to type. A child who has to explain and change it learns to program. | Run the ten-minute test further down this page in the trial class. |

### Why live teaching comes first

Every other criterion depends on someone being there. A stated class size only matters if a teacher uses the time to ask questions; curriculum fit only matters if someone adjusts the lesson to it.

This is not a case against recorded courses. They are cheap, patient and good for practice. It is a case against treating them as teaching, especially for a child under twelve.

### Why thinking comes last but weighs most

The seventh criterion is the hardest to see from a website, and the one that decides whether a year of classes leaves anything behind.

Our promise to families is the same in Toronto, Calgary or Halifax as anywhere else we teach: we teach students how to think and solve problems, not to copy answers or AI output. The test below is how you hold us to it.

### Where in-person centres are strong

A centre gives a child a room, friends and a fixed after-school routine. For a six-year-old who needs to move around and be with others, that can matter more than any curriculum.

The trade-off is reach. A centre exists only where a franchise has opened one, the hours follow the centre's week, and a child in a smaller town may have nothing within driving distance.

### Where online formats are strong

Live online classes reach every province and territory equally, and the teacher can see the child's code as it is typed, which is harder across a room of desks.

The difference between an online group and online one-to-one is mostly attention. A group gives a child peers and a fixed rhythm; one-to-one gives every minute to one student and lets the plan follow their school course.

A note on free programs: Canada is well served by them, and a family on any budget should know they exist before paying anyone, including us. They are listed by name in the next section. Their limitation is continuity: a workshop or a club meeting is rarely a year-long path with one teacher who knows your child.

An Ontario child in Grade 3 is already expected to write code with repeating events in math class. A coding class that teaches loops with blocks or Python is practising the same idea, and a good teacher will say so to the child.

A contest problem cannot be memorised in advance and, at the CCC, cannot be handed to an AI tool. It shows what a student can do alone, on a problem they have never seen.

## The transfer test: how to tell whether a class teaches thinking

This is our test, and you can run it in any trial class, ours included. The idea is simple. A child who has really understood a program can change it when the rule changes. A child who copied it, from the teacher's screen or from an AI tool, cannot. Ask the teacher to let your child try both parts, and watch the second part closely.

Part 1: the morning bus. A school bus leaves its first stop at 7:05 and another leaves every 12 minutes. Print the time of every bus that leaves before 8:00. The program counts time in minutes after midnight, so 7:05 is 7 times 60 plus 5, which is 425.

**Part 1 · our program** (python)
```python
# Our transfer test, part 1: the morning bus (written by Modern Age Coders)
first_bus = 7 * 60 + 5      # 7:05, as minutes after midnight
gap = 12                    # a bus every 12 minutes
t = first_bus
while t < 8 * 60:           # every bus that leaves before 8:00
    print(f"{t // 60}:{t % 60:02d}")
    t += gap
```

**Part 1 · what it prints** (text)
```text
7:05
7:17
7:29
7:41
7:53
```

Part 2: the rule changes. Now the last bus may leave as late as 8:30, and no bus may leave between 7:30 and 7:45 while a crossing guard changes shift. Change the program. Do not start again from a blank file.

**Part 2 · one good answer** (python)
```python
# Our transfer test, part 2: the changed rule (written by Modern Age Coders)
first_bus = 7 * 60 + 5
gap = 12
closed_from = 7 * 60 + 30   # no departures from 7:30 ...
closed_to = 7 * 60 + 45     # ... to 7:45
t = first_bus
while t <= 8 * 60 + 30:     # the last bus may now leave at 8:30
    if not (closed_from <= t <= closed_to):
        print(f"{t // 60}:{t % 60:02d}")
    t += gap
```
Two changes do all the work: the loop condition becomes t <= 8 * 60 + 30, and one if skips the closed window. The 7:41 bus disappears and three new buses appear after 8:00. Both programs were run under Python on 22 October 2026 and printed exactly the output shown.

**Part 2 · what it prints** (text)
```text
7:05
7:17
7:29
7:53
8:05
8:17
8:29
```

### What a child who understood does

They go straight to the while line, because they know it decides when the buses stop. They can say why 8 * 60 + 30 means 8:30. They test the new program and notice that 7:41 has gone.

They may be slow, and they may make a mistake on the way, such as writing < where <= was needed so that an 8:30 bus would be dropped. A mistake they find themselves is a good sign, not a bad one.

### What a child who copied does

They retype the whole program, or ask for a new one. They cannot say what t holds, or why the program multiplies by 60. Asked to predict the output before running it, they guess.

None of that means the child is weak. It means the class has been teaching typing. The fix is a teacher who asks "why" before "what next", every lesson.

The same test works with blocks for a younger child: build a Scratch sprite that jumps three times, then change the rule so it jumps until it touches the ground. And it works for us. In our free first class, ask the teacher to change one rule in whatever your child has just built, and judge us by what happens next. Outside class, remember that ChatGPT needs a parent's permission for anyone under 18.

## How to use a free trial class to compare providers

Most providers, us included, offer a first class or session without charge. Used well, it answers most of the seven criteria in an hour. These steps are ours.

1. Before the class, write down three facts: your child's grade and province, what they have coded before (nothing is a fine answer), and what you hope they get from a year of classes.
2. Ask the size question in writing. "What is the most students a class can have?" A clear number is a good sign; "it depends" is worth a follow-up.
3. Ask for the monthly fee before the class, not after it. If the provider's site does not print one, ask for it by email so you can compare like with like.
4. Sit nearby for the first fifteen minutes, then leave. Notice how often the teacher asks your child to explain something, and how often your child simply watches.
5. Ask for one rule change near the end. This is the transfer test above. Watch whether your child can change their own work, with hints if needed.
6. Ask what the next eight classes would cover, and how they link to your child's school course in your province.
7. Afterwards, ask your child one question: "What did you make, and how does it work?" The answer is the best review any class can get.

### Comparing two or three trials

Score each trial against the seven criteria on the same evening, while it is fresh. A simple tick, cross or question mark per criterion is enough.

If two providers tie, choose the one your child wants to go back to. Twelve months of willing practice beats a better syllabus attended reluctantly.

## Four stages from first lesson to independent coder

Our stages, used in the first class to place a student honestly.

**Four stages of a young coder (ours)**

| Stage | What you see |
|---|---|
| 1. Following | Builds what the teacher shows, step by step, and can say what each step does |
| 2. Changing | Alters a working program when a rule changes, the transfer test in this page |
| 3. Planning | Breaks a new problem into steps before writing any code |
| 4. Independent | Solves unseen problems alone, tests them, and explains the method to someone else |

### For parents

Most children who come to us after a year of another class are at stage 1, whatever the certificate says. That is not their fault and it moves quickly once the teaching changes.
Stage 2 is the one to ask about after three months. If your child cannot yet change their own program, ask the teacher why.

### For students

When something works, change one thing and predict what will happen before you run it.
If you used an AI tool, close it and write the program again from memory. Whatever you could not rewrite is what you still need to learn.

## Choosing a coding class in Canada: questions and answers

### What are the best coding classes for kids in Canada?

There is no single answer, so we give criteria instead of a ranking. Look for a live teacher, a stated class size, the right age band, a link to your province's curriculum, published fees, a route to contests and teaching that makes the child explain and change their code.

### Are online coding classes as good as in-person ones?

They can be, and for coding they have one advantage: the teacher can watch the child's code as it is typed. In-person centres offer a room and friends, which matters for some young children. Judge the teaching, not the format.

### What class size should I look for?

Ask for the maximum, in writing. Smaller groups mean more questions per child. We teach batches of 15 to 20 and one to one, and we tell families which one we would choose for their child.

### At what age should a child start coding?

Our courses start at age 6 with block coding. In Ontario, the math curriculum already asks Grade 1 students to write and execute code with sequential events, so a gentle start at that age matches school.

### Is block coding enough, or should my child learn Python?

Blocks are the right start for young children. Most children of 9 or 10 who are comfortable with blocks are ready for typed Python, and staying in blocks for years can slow them down.

### Are there free coding programs in Canada?

Yes. Canada Learning Code describes itself as a national not-for-profit offering workshops at low to no cost, Digital Moment says its Code Club is free, and CEMC offers free resources and past contests. They are worth trying before paying anyone.

### Should a coding class follow my province's curriculum?

It should at least know it. Ontario teaches coding inside math from Grades 1 to 8, BC names Scratch in its Grades 6 and 7 module, and several provinces offer high school computer science. A class that knows this can support school instead of competing with it.

### Do coding classes help with the CCC or other contests?

A good one should. CEMC runs the Beaver Computing Challenge in November 2026 and the Canadian Computing Competition on Wednesday 17 February 2027, both entered through schools. Ask how a provider chooses practice problems.

### Should my child use AI tools while learning to code?

Only after they can write and explain the code themselves. The CCC rules forbid generative AI during the contest, and ChatGPT needs a parent's permission for anyone under 18. We teach students to think first and to check any AI output line by line.

### How can I tell whether a trial class was good?

Ask the teacher to change one rule in what your child built, and see whether your child can change the program. Then ask your child what they made and how it works. A clear answer is the best sign.
