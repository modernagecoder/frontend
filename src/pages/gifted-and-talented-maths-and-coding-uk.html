Gifted and talented maths and coding in the UK

Many UK parents search for a gifted and talented maths programme and find there is no national one. Ofsted noted in 2015 that there was no national definition of "most able", and schools usually decide for themselves which pupils count, mostly by attainment. What the national curriculum does say is clear: a quick learner should get richer problems before faster content. Modern Age Coders teaches that kind of stretch live online, in maths reasoning and in coding, for ages 6 to 16, through our olympiad course and our reasoning course for young children, as a group or 1-on-1. We do not test, label or certify children.

Is there a gifted and talented programme for maths in UK schools?

There is no single national gifted and talented maths programme in the UK, and no national label. Ofsted's 2015 report on the most able said: "There is currently no national definition for most able." Potential Plus UK notes that most UK children are identified as most able or more able on academic achievement alone. The national curriculum for England is specific about stretch: "Pupils who grasp concepts rapidly should be challenged through being offered rich and sophisticated problems before any acceleration through new content." Modern Age Coders teaches that depth-first stretch live online in maths and coding for ages 6 to 16, on our olympiad and reasoning courses, in groups of 15 to 20 or 1-on-1, with no label attached.

Is there a label?

Less than many parents expect. There is no national test and no national list, and the words schools use vary. Here is what the sources we read actually say.

What Ofsted found about Key Stage 3

In over half of the schools visited, Ofsted reported that the most able students it spoke to said "work in Key Stage 3 is too easy". Students described lessons that "covered what they had already learnt in Key Stage 2".

The contrast was the more effective schools, where students said "there is always a question we can't answer or a really interesting challenge." That sentence is a good test of any stretch, at school or outside it.

Who else works in this space

Ofsted's report cites two organisations: the National Association for Able Children in Education (NACE), which works with schools, and Potential Plus UK, which works with families. Potential Plus has announced it is joining the children's charity Coram and continues as Coram Potential Plus.

Both are better placed than a tutoring school to advise on identification. We do not assess, and we would not ask a family to pay for a label.

Depth first

How should a school stretch a child who is quick at maths?

By going deeper before going faster. The national curriculum for England says so twice, once for primary and once for Key Stage 3.

The curriculum's words

For Key Stages 1 and 2: "Pupils who grasp concepts rapidly should be challenged through being offered rich and sophisticated problems before any acceleration through new content."

For Key Stage 3 the same sentence ends "in preparation for key stage 4". Both sit beside the rule that decisions about progress should rest on "the security of pupils' understanding and their readiness to progress".

Why depth beats speed (our view)

A child who races into next year's methods is soon bored again, a year later, and has learned to expect maths to be easy. A child given a problem that resists for twenty minutes learns persistence, which no textbook supplies.

Depth also keeps a child with classmates in school, so stretch outside school adds to their week rather than making the classroom pointless.

Coding is the natural breadth for a mathematical child, and the computing curriculum asks for it: at Key Stage 3 pupils should "use 2 or more programming languages, at least one of which is textual, to solve a variety of computational problems". A child who already thinks in patterns usually finds a textual language such as Python a joy, because it lets them test a hunch on thousands of cases. Our KS3 computing page covers the school side.

Problems by age

What does a depth problem look like at ages 7, 11 and 14?

Three problems we wrote, each with a first answer a quick child finds fast and a second question that makes them slow down. That second question is where stretch lives.

Age 7 (ours): rectangles from 12 squares

Using 12 square tiles, how many different rectangles can you build? Three: 1 by 12, 2 by 6 and 3 by 4. The depth question: which number of tiles makes only one rectangle? 7, 11, 13: the primes. The child has found prime numbers without the word.

Age 11 (ours): the magic square centre

Put 1 to 9 in a 3 by 3 grid so every row, column and diagonal adds to 15. Many children find one by trial. The depth question: why must 5 be in the middle? Four lines pass through the centre; their total is 60, which is all nine numbers (45) plus the centre counted three extra times. So the centre is 5.

Age 14 (ours): perfect numbers in Python

A perfect number equals the sum of its proper factors: 6 is 1 plus 2 plus 3. Write a program to find all below 10,000. It prints 6, 28, 496 and 8128. The depth question: why are they all even? Nobody knows whether an odd one exists. A real open question, met at fourteen.

None of these needs content beyond the child's age. The rectangles use multiplication facts, the magic square uses addition, and the program uses division with remainders. What changes is the question asked once the answer is found. In our lessons that second question takes up most of the time, and the child explains their reasoning aloud or in writing before moving on.

All three problems, the program, its output and the explanations are written by Modern Age Coders. They are not items from any ability test, and we do not prepare children for gifted tests.

Talking to school

How can parents ask a UK school for more stretch?

Calmly, specifically and with the school's own words. These steps are our suggestion, built around what the curriculum already asks of schools.

  1. Write down two or three examples of work your child found too easy, with dates. Specific beats general.
  2. Ask whether the school has a policy or a named lead for more able pupils, and read it if it exists.
  3. Ask how the class teacher offers "rich and sophisticated problems" to quick learners, using the curriculum's own phrase.
  4. Ask about enrichment the school can nominate for, such as Royal Institution Masterclasses or UKMT challenges.
  5. Agree one small change and a date to review it, rather than asking for a label or a different year group.

What to avoid

Asking for your child to be "put on the gifted register" can stall a conversation, because many schools have no such register and there is no national one. Asking what the next harder problem is usually moves things on.

Comparing your child with classmates rarely helps either.

More able questions

What parents of quick learners ask

Is there a gifted and talented programme in UK schools?

Not a national one. Ofsted's 2015 report said there was no national definition for most able, and schools decide their own approach. Ask your child's school whether it has a more able policy or a named lead.

How are children identified as more able in the UK?

Usually by attainment at school. Potential Plus UK says most UK children are identified as most able or more able solely on high academic achievement rather than cognitive ability, so a quick child who is not yet top of the class can be missed.

What should a school do for a child who is good at maths?

The national curriculum for England says pupils who grasp concepts rapidly should be offered rich and sophisticated problems before any acceleration through new content. In practice that means harder problems on current topics rather than next year's textbook.

Should my child move ahead a year in maths?

The curriculum advises depth first, with progress based on the security of understanding. Speeding ahead often brings the same boredom back a year later; richer problems usually serve a quick learner better, though every child is different.

What is high learning potential?

The phrase Potential Plus UK has used since 2013 instead of gifted, talented, bright or clever. It describes children who understand quickly and learn faster than most, and the charity says this can be developed alongside traits such as motivation and persistence.

Is coding good for a gifted child?

Often, because it adds breadth rather than speed. The Key Stage 3 computing curriculum already asks for two or more programming languages, at least one textual, and a mathematical child can use code to test patterns on thousands of cases.

Do you test whether my child is gifted?

No. We do not assess, label or certify children. In a free first lesson we see what your child can do with a rich problem and tell you which stage we would teach them at.

Do you prepare children for gifted or ability tests?

No. We teach reasoning, number sense, spatial thinking and code for their own sake. That kind of broad thinking is the only honest preparation for any ability test, and we never use imitation test items.

Are you connected with Potential Plus, NACE or Ofsted?

No. We quote their published words so parents can follow them up, and we have no link with any of them.

When are lessons?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. All lessons are in UK time.
