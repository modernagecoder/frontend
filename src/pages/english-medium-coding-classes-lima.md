Your child already learns maths and science in English at school. A coding class in Spanish would mean learning every technical word twice. A live coding class is mostly listening and short spoken answers, which suits that profile. Mentors speak slowly, repeat an instruction in simpler words, and show each step on the shared screen before asking for it.

Speaking is built in small pieces: naming a block, reading a line of code aloud, then explaining in one sentence why a program failed. By the second month most children answer without being asked twice, and the code itself is the same in any language.

Some English, a few periods a week, and no computing subject at all. The national curriculum, the CNEB, sets the weekly timetable in teaching periods of 45 minutes.

A teaching period ("hora pedagógica") is 45 minutes; the minutes column is our own multiplication. Private bilingual and international schools set their own, usually much larger, English timetables.

### English, on paper and in practice

For primaria, the CNEB itself is cautious: it says English is phased in "dependiendo de la contratación de estos docentes", depending on whether the school has hired a specialist English teacher, and where it has not, the hours become free-choice periods.

So two Lima classmates of the same age, at different schools, may meet very different amounts of English. One at a bilingual school spends most of the day in English; one at a public school may have three periods a week, or none in the early grades.

### Computing, nowhere on the timetable

No area of the CNEB plan of studies is called computing or programming. Digital skills sit in competency 28, "Se desenvuelve en entornos virtuales generados por las TIC", which the CNEB lists among the "competencias transversales a las áreas": skills every subject is meant to touch, owned by none of them.

MINEDU has started to experiment. In July 2025 its Fábrica de Programadores pilot set out to train 1,000 pupils in 4th and 5th of secundaria in Lima Norte in Python, with the chance to take the PCEP exam at the end. That is a pilot, not a subject, and most Lima children will not meet it.

That is the gap these classes fill. A child at a bilingual school gets the English but rarely the coding; a child at a Spanish-medium school usually gets neither in any depth. Our classes add both, alongside school and never in place of it.

## What does a lesson look like? Sorting real English scores with Python

Here is a lesson we teach to learners from about 11, built on the EF city scores above. It teaches the two habits that catch most bugs in any program that sorts things into groups: put your checks in the right order, and test the edges.

### The task

EF puts every score into a band, and its page data draws the band lines at 600, 550, 500 and 450. We give the learner the eight city scores and ask for a small function, band(score), that names the band for any score.

Most first attempts look like Version 1. It reads naturally from the bottom up: above 450 is low, above 500 is moderate, and so on. It runs without an error message, which is exactly why it is dangerous.

### The surprise

Every city comes out "low", Lima included. The reason is that Python checks the conditions from the top and stops at **the first true check**. A score of 555 is at least 450, so the function answers "low" and never looks further.

The fix is not a cleverer condition; it is a better order. Version 2 keeps the band lines in a list, from the highest down, and walks through them.

**Version 1: reads well, answers wrongly** (Python)
```python
scores = {
    "Lima": 555, "Cusco": 520, "Trujillo": 519, "Arequipa": 512,
    "Huancayo": 499, "Piura": 484, "Chiclayo": 474, "Ica": 467,
}

def band(score):
    if score >= 450:
        return "low"
    elif score >= 500:
        return "moderate"
    elif score >= 550:
        return "high"
    elif score >= 600:
        return "very high"
    else:
        return "very low"

for city, score in scores.items():
    print(city, score, band(score))
```
It prints eight lines, every one of them low, starting with Lima 555 low and ending with Ica 467 low.

**Version 2: highest line first, then test the edges** (Python)
```python
LINES = [(600, "very high"), (550, "high"), (500, "moderate"), (450, "low")]

def band(score):
    for line, name in LINES:
        if score >= line:
            return name
    return "very low"

count = {}
for city, score in scores.items():
    count[band(score)] = count.get(band(score), 0) + 1
print("Fixed:", count)

for edge in (549, 550, 499, 500):
    print("Edge", edge, band(edge))
```
Run after Version 1, it prints Fixed: {'high': 1, 'moderate': 3, 'low': 4}, which matches EF's own bands for all eight cities, then Edge 549 moderate, Edge 550 high, Edge 499 low and Edge 500 moderate.

Then comes the part that turns a coder into a careful one. We ask the learner to change >= to > and run it again. The count of cities does not change at all: high 1, moderate 3, low 4. Only the edge tests move, and 550 and 500 drop a band. Two rules can agree on every real city and still disagree at the edge, and only a test written on purpose at the edge will show it.

EF's page does not say which side of a line a score of exactly 550 falls on, so neither rule can be proved right from the data. The honest answer is to write the assumption down in a comment, which is what we teach. It is the same habit a Diploma student needs in an internal assessment and a developer needs at work.

1. **Ages 6 to 8, no screen.** Sort ten toy cars into "small", "medium" and "big" boxes using a ruler. Then find a car exactly on a line. Which box does it go in, and who decided?
2. **Ages 8 to 10, Scratch.** Make a quiz that says "cold", "warm" or "hot" for a temperature. Try the numbers right on your dividing lines before you show anyone.
3. **Ages 11 to 14, Python.** Type Version 1, predict its output before you run it, then write Version 2 without looking at ours.
4. **Ages 14 and up.** Add a fifth line for "very low" below 450 and write four edge tests of your own. Then explain, in two English sentences, why the city count could not catch the bug.

## Will my child learn to think, or just copy answers from AI?

To think. We teach learners to reason through a problem before any tool writes code for them, and we check that they can explain every line they hand in.

### Explain before typing

Before a learner writes a program, they say in English what it should do and how they will know it works. The edge tests in the lesson above are part of that plan, not an extra.

### AI with age rules

Children do not use adult chatbot accounts in our classes. Claude, for example, is for users aged 18 and over, and ChatGPT needs a parent's permission for anyone under 18. Older learners use AI tools to check and question their own work, not to replace it.

### Depth over speed

We would rather a learner understand one program completely than copy ten. The same standard applies whether the learner is in Miraflores, Comas or Mumbai: one level of teaching, wherever you are.

Peru's own computing competition makes the same point. The Peruvian Informatics Olympiad, OPI, organised by UTEC and open free of charge to all secundaria pupils in Peru, bans generative AI for solving problems in its 2025 rules. A teenager who wants to try it needs to reason alone, in code, against the clock. We are not connected with UTEC or the OPI; we simply teach the kind of thinking it rewards, with problems we write ourselves.
