# KS1 coding lessons online, and what the computing curriculum asks

Computing has been a national curriculum subject from Year 1 since 2014, and the whole of the Key Stage 1 programme fits in six sentences. A child of six or seven should learn what an algorithm is, write and fix simple programs, predict what a program will do before running it, use technology to make things, notice computers in the world around them, and keep personal information private. None of that needs an expensive kit, and a good deal of it is best learned with a pencil, a floor and a patient adult. This page explains each sentence in plain words, what Ofsted's research review says about unplugged activities and block coding, what is changing in 2028, and how our live lessons teach six and seven year olds.

## What is taught in KS1 computing?

The statutory programme for Key Stage 1 is six statements. Three are about programming, three are about using technology well. Here is each one with what it looks like for a six year old.

**The Key Stage 1 computing programme of study, with our plain-English reading**

| Statement (DfE, statutory) | In plain words (ours) | A six year old might |
|---|---|---|
| Understand what algorithms are; how they are implemented as programs on digital devices; and that programs execute by following precise and unambiguous instructions | An algorithm is a set of steps. A program is those steps written for a computer, which does exactly what it is told and nothing else | Give a friend step-by-step directions across the playground, then type the same steps for an on-screen character |
| Create and debug simple programs | Make a short program, then find and fix what goes wrong | Build a three-block program, see the character walk off the edge, and change one number |
| Use logical reasoning to predict the behaviour of simple programs | Say what will happen before pressing go | Point to where the character will stop, then run it to check |
| Use technology purposefully to create, organise, store, manipulate and retrieve digital content | Make something on a device, save it, find it again and change it | Draw a picture, save it with a name, reopen it next lesson and add to it |
| Recognise common uses of information technology beyond school | Notice computers in everyday life | Spot the computer in a self-service till, a traffic light or a washing machine |
| Use technology safely and respectfully, keeping personal information private; know where to go for help | Keep your name, school and address to yourself online, and tell an adult if something worries you | Sort a set of cards into "fine to share" and "keep private" |

The national curriculum frames all of this with an ambitious purpose: "A high-quality computing education equips pupils to use computational thinking and creativity to understand and change the world." Its aims for the whole of school name the big ideas, "abstraction, logic, algorithms and data representation". In Key Stage 1 those ideas arrive in their simplest form: instructions that must be precise, and the habit of checking whether a program did what you meant.

### Is it compulsory?

The 2025 Curriculum and Assessment Review puts it simply: "Computing is a statutory foundation subject from Key Stages 1 to 4." Maintained schools must teach the programme in Years 1 and 2.

Ofsted's computing review adds the starting point: computing "is not part of the latest statutory framework for the early years foundation stage, but is part of the national curriculum from Year 1."

### Where maths joins in

The Year 2 maths programme suggests children practise quarter, half and three-quarter turns by giving each other instructions, and by programming robots with turns in right angles.

So the same lesson can serve two subjects: a child directing a floor robot is doing both geometry and computing.

## Do KS1 children need a screen to learn computing?

Not for all of it. Ofsted's research review says unplugged activities can help at the early stages, with one important warning about how they are chosen.

Unplugged means teaching computing ideas without a computer. Ofsted's 2022 review describes the approach and its value for young children: "Unplugged activities can be useful in introducing computing concepts to pupils at the early stages of their computing education." It is also careful. Teachers "should pay careful attention to how the activity will contribute to achieving curriculum goals", and the review warns that "unplugged activities to teach pupils about algorithms can be ambiguous", which defeats the point, since an algorithm is meant to be exact. A good unplugged game for a six year old is one where a vague instruction visibly fails.

**Home activities for Years 1 and 2, each tied to one statutory statement (all ours)**

| Activity (ours) | You need | What happens | Statement it practises |
|---|---|---|---|
| Robot Parent | One adult, one living room | The child gives instructions to walk the adult to the sofa. The adult follows them exactly, so "go over there" gets nowhere. | Precise and unambiguous instructions |
| Arrow Trail | Paper squares and a toy | Lay a path of arrow cards on the floor; the toy follows them square by square. One arrow is wrong: find it. | Create and debug |
| Where Will It Land? | A grid drawn on paper, a counter | Read out three moves; the child points to the finishing square before moving the counter to check. | Predict the behaviour of a program |
| Computer Spotting | A walk to the shops | Count the computers you can see: tills, card readers, lights, doorbells, the car. | Uses of IT beyond school |
| Private or Fine? | Cards with a pet's name, a favourite colour, a home address, a school name | Sort them into two piles and talk about why. | Keeping personal information private |

### Why the precision matters

The curriculum says programs run by "following precise and unambiguous instructions". Children usually find this funny the first time an adult robot walks into a wall because the instruction said "forward" and never said "stop".

That laugh is the lesson. It is the same reason an on-screen character walks off the edge.

### What young children can manage

Ofsted notes that "Several studies have demonstrated that young pupils are able to wrestle successfully with the core concepts of computing", including programming.

In our experience six year olds handle short sequences and simple bugs well. Long programs and abstract ideas come later.

## Should a six year old use ScratchJr, Scratch or something else?

Block-based tools are the normal choice in primary schools, and Ofsted explains both why they help and where they fall short.

Ofsted observes that "It is common for block-based languages such as Scratch to be used in primary schools". Blocks snap together, so a child cannot make a typing mistake, and the review agrees they "reduce the need to memorise syntax". It then adds the part parents rarely hear: they "do not necessarily help pupils with the semantic and conceptual difficulties". A child can drag blocks around until something moves without understanding why. That is why we ask children to predict and explain, not just to build.

**Common first tools for Key Stage 1, compared (facts from each maker's own site; judgements ours)**

| Tool | What its maker says | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| ScratchJr | "young children (ages 5 to 7) can program their own interactive stories and games" | A free tablet app for Year 1 and Year 2 stories at home | Easy to play with randomly; ask what each block will do first |
| Scratch | A free block-based language used widely in primary schools | Six and seven year olds ready to read short block labels with help | More blocks than a beginner needs; a teacher helps choose a few |
| A floor robot or a toy with arrow buttons | Varies by maker | Directions, turns and debugging in a real room | Can become a toy only; tie it to a task with a clear goal |
| Paper and a grid | Costs nothing | Prediction and precise instructions | Needs an adult to play the computer strictly |

### What is changing in 2028

The government's response to the Review commits to a revised national curriculum, aiming to publish it "by spring 2027" and to "fully implement the new full national curriculum for first teaching from September 2028".

For computing it says: "Through the reformed curriculum, pupils will know from a young age how computers can be trained using data and they will learn essential digital skills such as AI literacy."

### What it means for a Year 1 child now

Today's six sentences still apply for this school year and next. The new programme was not yet published when we read the sources in October 2026, so we do not guess its wording.

Precise instructions, debugging and prediction will matter under any version, which is why we teach them first.

## Five Key Stage 1 coding examples, worked through

Our own examples, in the order we would teach them. Each one names the statutory idea it serves.

### 1. An algorithm for a paper aeroplane? No: for a teddy (algorithms)

**Task (ours).** Write the steps to put a teddy in a box: "Pick up teddy. Put teddy in box."

**Test.** An adult follows the steps exactly and holds teddy above the box forever, because "put in" never said "let go".

**Fix.** "Pick up teddy. Move teddy over the box. Let go."

**The idea.** Precise and unambiguous instructions. The program did what it said, not what we meant.

### 2. The robot that missed the cheese (debugging)

**Task (ours).** A mouse starts in the bottom-left square of a 4 by 4 grid, facing up. The cheese is two squares up and one square right.

**Program.** Forward 2. Turn left. Forward 1.

**Bug.** The mouse ends up off the grid on the left. Which step is wrong? The turn: it should be right.

**The idea.** Debugging means finding the one step that differs from the plan, not starting again.

### 3. Point first, then press go (prediction)

**Task (ours).** A character stands in the middle of the stage. The program: move 10 steps, move 10 steps, turn around, move 10 steps.

**Predict.** Ask: "Where will it stop?" Most children point too far right the first time.

**Reason.** Twenty steps right, then ten steps back: it ends ten steps right of where it started.

**The idea.** "Use logical reasoning to predict the behaviour of simple programs", in the curriculum's words. Running it is the check.

### 4. Spotting the repeat (a peek at Year 3)

**Task (ours).** A program draws a square with: forward, turn, forward, turn, forward, turn, forward, turn.

**Notice.** "Forward, turn" happens four times.

**Shorter.** Repeat 4 times: forward, turn.

**The idea.** Repetition is a Key Stage 2 statement, but a confident Year 2 child often spots it. We let them, without rushing it.

### 5. Fine to share, or keep private? (online safety)

**Task (ours).** Sort: your favourite animal, your full name, your school, the colour of your bike, your street.

**Talk.** Favourite animal and bike colour are fine. Full name, school and street help a stranger find you, so they stay private.

**The idea.** "Keeping personal information private", and knowing to tell a trusted adult if something online feels wrong.

### Practice set (ours, with answers)

(1) Fix this: "Draw a face" as an algorithm for someone who has never seen one. (2) Forward 3, turn right, forward 1, from the bottom-left facing up: which square? (3) A program says move 5 three times; how far altogether? (4) Name two computers in a kitchen.

**Answers:** (1) many answers, such as "Draw a circle. Draw two dots near the top. Draw a curve below them."; (2) three up and one right; (3) 15 steps; (4) for example a microwave and a fridge with a display.

## How do online coding lessons work for a six year old?

Short, practical and spoken aloud. The child builds on screen, but the teacher keeps asking them to predict and explain.

1. **A free first class.** A few minutes of a direction game, then a first program of two or three blocks. The teacher sees how the child reads, clicks and reasons.
2. **Unplugged warm-up.** Each lesson starts away from the mouse: a grid on paper, arrow cards, or a "robot parent" instruction.
3. **Predict, run, fix.** The child says what will happen, runs the program, and fixes it if it surprises them. The teacher never fixes it for them.
4. **A small palette.** Only the blocks the lesson needs are introduced, so the screen does not overwhelm a six year old.
5. **Something to keep.** A short saved project, shown to a parent at the end, and one unplugged game for the week.

### Group or 1-on-1 at six?

A group of 15 to 20 children of a similar age suits a child who reads simple words and enjoys showing their work.

1-on-1 suits a child who is just turning six, who needs help with the mouse, or who wants to go further faster.

### What we will not do

Leave a six year old alone with a screen for long. We ask for an adult in the house, nearby, during lessons.

Use tools meant for older users, such as general AI chatbots, with Key Stage 1 children, or claim any link with a school or the Department for Education.

## Four steps through Key Stage 1 computing

Each step is something a child can show you, not a level on a chart.

**What a Key Stage 1 child should be able to do in computing (our steps, from the programme of study)**

| Step | Usually by | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Exact instructions | Early Year 1 | Gives three clear steps to move a person or toy to a target |
| 2. Short programs | Late Year 1 | Builds a sequence of four or five blocks that does one job |
| 3. Finding the bug | Year 2 | Spots which single step is wrong and changes only that one |
| 4. Predicting | End of Year 2 | Says where a character will stop before running the program, and explains why |

### Step three is the big one

A child who fixes one step instead of starting over has understood that programs are made of parts.
For the road beyond Year 2, the [coding roadmap](/coding-roadmap) shows each stage.

### Step four leads to Key Stage 2

Prediction is the root of reasoning about variables and selection later.
Lessons can pause during school holidays.

## What parents ask about coding in Years 1 and 2

### What is taught in KS1 computing?

Six statutory statements: what algorithms are and that programs follow precise instructions, creating and debugging simple programs, predicting what a program will do, making and saving digital content, noticing technology beyond school, and staying safe online.

### What is an algorithm, for a KS1 child?

A set of steps to do a job, written so exactly that someone, or a computer, could follow them without guessing. Brushing teeth or getting to the classroom door can both be algorithms.

### Is computing compulsory in Key Stage 1?

Yes for schools following the national curriculum. The 2025 Curriculum and Assessment Review states that computing is a statutory foundation subject from Key Stages 1 to 4.

### Is the KS1 computing curriculum changing?

A revised national curriculum is planned for first teaching from September 2028. The government says pupils will learn from a young age how computers can be trained using data, but the new wording was not yet published when we checked.

### What are good KS1 computing activities at home?

Games where a child gives an adult exact instructions, arrow paths on the floor with one deliberate mistake to find, and predicting where a counter will land on a paper grid. They need no screen at all.

### Is ScratchJr good for a five or six year old?

Its makers describe it for ages 5 to 7, and it is free. It works well alongside lessons, especially if you ask your child what each block will do before they run it.

### What age can my child start your coding lessons?

From age 6, which covers most children in Year 1 and every child in Year 2. Coding for Kids is listed for complete beginners aged 6 to 12, grouped by age.

### Do I need to buy a robot or a tablet?

No. A computer or laptop with a browser is enough for our lessons, and the home activities on this page use paper, toys and a floor.

### When are lessons?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Every time is in UK time.
