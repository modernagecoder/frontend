Year 2 maths tutor online: the year of derive and check

Ask a Year 1 child for 30 + 70 and many will count in tens on their fingers. By the end of Year 2 the national curriculum expects something smarter: a child who knows that 3 + 7 = 10 should see straight away that 30 + 70 = 100, and should be able to check a subtraction by adding the answer back. That is the heart of Year 2 maths, alongside two-digit calculations, the first three times tables, fractions such as three quarters, and time to five minutes. This page explains the shift from calculating to deriving and checking, sets out the whole Year 2 programme, lists the maths words children are expected to use, walks through five original examples, and describes our live online classes for six and seven year olds.

In short

Year 2 maths in England is set by the statutory Year 2 programme of study from the DfE, for children aged 6 to 7. Children recall addition and subtraction facts to 20 fluently and derive related facts up to 100, use tens and ones, add and subtract two-digit numbers, check answers with the inverse, learn the 2, 5 and 10 times tables, find thirds and quarters, tell the time to five minutes and read simple charts. We teach it live online through our Elementary Maths course, in a class of 15 to 20 or 1-on-1, and the opening class costs nothing.

Derive and check

What is the big change in Year 2 maths?

Children move from working every answer out to building new facts from old ones, and from trusting an answer to checking it. Both are written into the Year 2 programme.

Two statutory lines carry the change. Children should "recall and use addition and subtraction facts to 20 fluently, and derive and use related facts up to 100". And they should "recognise and use the inverse relationship between addition and subtraction and use this to check calculations and solve missing number problems". The first says: if you know 3 + 7, you also know 30 + 70. The second says: if you think 52 − 18 is 34, add 34 and 18 and see whether you get back to 52.

The curriculum's notes spell out how deriving is meant to look, using a whole family of facts: 3 + 7 = 10, 10 − 7 = 3 and 7 = 10 − 3 lead to 30 + 70 = 100, 100 − 70 = 30 and 70 = 100 − 30. They also link checking to order: children add numbers in a different order to check, which "establishes commutativity and associativity of addition". Those are long words for a simple habit, one every Year 2 child can learn: try it another way and see if you get the same answer.

Why this matters more than speed

A child who only counts gets slower as numbers grow. A child who derives gets faster, because each new fact rests on one already known.

Checking with the inverse also means a child catches their own mistakes, which builds confidence far better than being told an answer is wrong.

The test question

The end of KS1 tests are optional, and the DfE suggests May 2027 for any school that chooses them. The curriculum is the same either way.

A child who derives and checks is well prepared for any test a school uses, without special drilling.

The Year 2 content

What do children learn in Year 2 maths?

Eight areas, from number to statistics. This is a plain-English summary of the statutory Year 2 requirements, with one useful note from the curriculum for each.

Two notes are easy to overlook. Place value: Year 2 children "begin to understand zero as a place holder", the idea that the 0 in 40 is what makes it forty rather than four. And counting: the 3s appear in Year 2 counting even though the 3 times table is a Year 3 statement, because counting in threes prepares for thirds. The curriculum is quietly building the next year inside this one.

Maths words

Which maths words should a Year 2 child know?

More than parents expect. The curriculum wants children to talk about maths precisely, and new words arrive in nearly every area.

The Year 2 notes say that "Pupils extend their understanding of the language of addition and subtraction to include sum and difference." Shape brings sides, edges, vertices and faces; measures bring the units; position brings clockwise and anti-clockwise. A child who knows the word can follow the question, which matters even more when questions are read aloud.

Difference is the tricky one

Many children hear "difference" and add. Line up two towers of cubes and ask how many more one has: the gap is the difference.

Counting up from the smaller number is often easier than taking away, and it gives the same answer.

Time to five minutes

Year 2 asks children to "tell and write the time to five minutes, including quarter past/to the hour". Use a real analogue clock at home; a digital display does not teach it.

Count the minute hand round in fives from the 12. It doubles as 5 times table practice.

Worked examples

Five Year 2 examples, worked through

Written by us, and each one either derives or checks. They are written for a parent beside a child, objects first where it helps.

1. A fact to 20 becomes a fact to 100

Question (ours). 60 + 40, and then 100 − 40.

Known fact. 6 + 4 = 10.

Derive. Six tens and four tens make ten tens, which is 100. So 60 + 40 = 100, and taking 40 back leaves 60.

Why it matters. This is "derive and use related facts up to 100" in action. With ten-sticks, the child can see that only the word "ten" has been added.

2. Checking a subtraction by adding

Question (ours). A child works out 81 − 26 and gets 65.

Check. Add the answer to the number taken away: 65 + 26 = 91. That is not 81, so something slipped.

Find it. Partition: 81 − 20 = 61, then 61 − 6 = 55. Check again: 55 + 26 = 81.

Why it matters. The inverse turns every subtraction into its own answer key. The child finds the mistake, not the adult.

3. Adding three numbers the easy way

Question (ours). 8 + 5 + 2.

Spot a ten. 8 and 2 make 10. The order of adding makes no difference, so add those first.

Finish. 10 + 5 = 15.

Why it matters. Year 2 asks children to add three one-digit numbers, and to know order does not matter for adding. Together they make this quick.

4. A pictogram with a key of 5

Question (ours). In a pictogram, one star stands for 5 books. Class A has 4 stars and Class B has 3 stars. How many more books did Class A read?

Read it. Count each row in fives: Class A is 5, 10, 15, 20 books; Class B is 5, 10, 15 books.

Compare. The difference is 20 − 15 = 5 books, which is also just the one extra star.

Why it matters. Pictograms with a key of 2, 5 or 10 join the times tables to data. Spotting that one extra star means 5 more is the reasoning step.

5. Counting in quarters on a number line

Task (ours). Count in quarters from 1 to 2 on a number line.

Steps. 1, 1 and a quarter, 1 and two quarters, 1 and three quarters, 2.

Notice. 1 and two quarters is the same point as 1 and a half.

Why it matters. Counting in fractions is a Year 2 expectation, and it shows that fractions are numbers on a line, not just slices.

Practice set (ours, with answers)

(1) 70 + 30. (2) 100 − 30. (3) Check 64 − 27 = 37 by adding. (4) 7 + 6 + 3. (5) Write <, > or = : 5 tens and 3 ones □ 35. (6) A key shows 1 circle = 2 children; how many children do 6 circles show? (7) How many minutes are in an hour? (8) Count in threes from 0 to 15.

Answers: (1) 100; (2) 70; (3) 37 + 27 = 64, so it is right; (4) 16; (5) 53 > 35; (6) 12; (7) 60; (8) 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15.

Our Year 2 classes

When is a Year 2 maths tutor worth it?

When a child still counts for facts to 20, is unsure about tens and ones, or loses confidence. Year 3 brings column methods and three new tables, and they rest on Year 2.

  1. Free first class. The teacher asks a few derive-and-check questions and plays a place value game, to see what is secure.
  2. A small kit. Ten-sticks or bundled straws, single counters, and a clock with hands, kept next to the screen.
  3. Derive out loud. Children say which known fact they used. "I know 4 + 5, so 40 + 50 is 90."
  4. Check every time. Each subtraction is checked by adding, each addition by changing the order.
  5. Between classes. One short game and a handful of questions, plus a note for parents at the end of each block.

Group or 1-on-1 in Year 2?

Our groups of 15 to 20 work well for a Year 2 child who enjoys a lively class and is keeping up.

Pick 1-on-1 when one gap, such as tens and ones, needs closing before Year 3, or when a child works far ahead.

Year 2 questions

What parents of Year 2 children ask

What should my child be able to do in maths by July of Year 2?

Facts to 20 recalled fluently and related facts to 100, tens and ones, adding and subtracting two-digit numbers, checking with the inverse, the 2, 5 and 10 times tables, thirds and quarters, time to five minutes and simple charts.

What does derive related facts up to 100 mean?

Using a known fact to find a bigger one. If a child knows 3 + 7 = 10, they can derive 30 + 70 = 100 and 100 minus 70 = 30 without counting.

What is the inverse in Year 2 maths?

Adding and subtracting undo each other, so a child can check 52 minus 18 = 34 by adding 34 and 18. The Year 2 curriculum asks children to use this to check calculations and solve missing number problems.

Which times tables are in Year 2?

The 2, 5 and 10 times tables, with their division facts. Children also count in threes from zero, which prepares for the 3 times table in Year 3.

Is Year 2 maths harder than Year 1?

It moves faster. Numbers go to 100 in calculations, written methods start, and children are expected to explain and check. A secure Year 1 makes it much easier.

Are Year 2 maths worksheets enough practice?

They help with written calculation, but many Year 2 skills are spoken or practical: deriving facts aloud, checking by adding, reading a real clock and measuring. Mix worksheets with short games.

Does my Year 2 child have to sit SATs?

Not necessarily. The end of KS1 tests are optional, and the recommended window for schools that choose them is May 2027. Ask your child's teacher what the school plans.

How can I tell if my child needs extra help in Year 2?

Watch for counting on fingers for facts within 20, confusion over what each digit in a two-digit number is worth, or worry about maths. Any of these is worth a word with the teacher.

When are classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Times are shown in UK time.

Are you connected with my child's school?

No. We run our classes entirely separately from schools, local authorities and the Department for Education.
