Grade 7 in Ontario

Grade 7: when a Kitchener student’s code is built from reusable pieces

Kitchener schools teach the Ontario curriculum. Grade 7 math brings in sub-programs, the pieces most languages call functions. We read the wording from the ministry’s curriculum data on 22 October 2026; the quotes are the ministry’s and the reading is ours.

C3.1Code “that involves events influenced by a defined count and/or subprogram and other control structures”.
A defined count is a loop that runs a set number of times. A subprogram is a named block of code that does one job.
NoteThe ministry adds that sub-programs can be reused, or called more than once from one main program, and gives the example of a sub-program for the area of a rectangle.
Write it once, use it many times. This is the single biggest step from beginner code to real code.
FeelingsA student voice in the curriculum says: “I need to stay calm, knowing that we may have to make a few tweaks before we get it right.”
The ministry expects frustration. A good class plans for it, and celebrates the fix.
Grade 8The next year asks for code involving “the analysis of data in order to inform and communicate decisions”.
Which is what the playground project below is: data, checked, then turned into a careful sentence.

Here is the ministry’s rectangle idea as a Python function, written by us. It is defined once and then used twice: first for the area of a 6 by 4 rectangle, then inside the volume of a box 10 units tall. It prints 24 and then 240:def rectangle_area(length, width): return length * width print(rectangle_area(6, 4)) print(rectangle_area(6, 4) * 10)

Kitchener data project

A playground installed in 3013? Checking dates in the City of Kitchener file

The City of Kitchener publishes its playgrounds as open data, with a construction year and an installation date for each. Children in our classes, usually from about age 10, check those two columns before trusting either. Every figure here is our count, not the City’s.

Our check of the CONSTRUCTION_YEAR and INSTALLATION_DATE columns in the City of Kitchener Playgrounds CSV, downloaded 22 October 2026 (337 rows)
What we foundRows
CONSTRUCTION_YEAR left blank92
CONSTRUCTION_YEAR written as the word PRE72
CONSTRUCTION_YEAR usable as a number (1998 to 2024)173
No INSTALLATION_DATE at all240
INSTALLATION_DATE in the year 3013, on a row built in 20131
Rows with both a numeric year and a date that agree on the year48 of 77
Rows where installation comes before construction9

Our checker uses only the standard library. Each row is sorted into blank, PRE, future or usable, and any installation date after this year is printed for a human to look at:import csv this_year = 2026 problems = {"blank": 0, "PRE": 0, "future": 0} good = [] with open("Playgrounds.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as f: rows = csv.DictReader(f) for row in rows: year = row["CONSTRUCTION_YEAR"].strip() if year == "": problems["blank"] += 1 elif year == "PRE": problems["PRE"] += 1 elif int(year) > this_year: problems["future"] += 1 else: good.append(int(year)) installed = row["INSTALLATION_DATE"][:4] if installed and int(installed) > this_year: print("impossible:", row["INSTALLATION_DATE"][:10], "built", year) print(problems) print(len(good), "usable years, from", min(good), "to", max(good))On our download it prints one impossible date, 3013/06/18, on a playground built in 2013, then the problem counts, then “173 usable years, from 1998 to 2024”.

A typo, almost certainly

3013 sits next to a construction year of 2013, so a mistyped first digit is the likely story. But it is our guess, and a careful report says so instead of silently changing it to 2013.

What PRE probably means

Seventy-two rows say PRE instead of a year. The file does not define it. It may mean built before records began, but we do not know, so the script keeps those rows out of any average.

Two dates, two meanings

Nine rows show installation before construction. Perhaps the two columns record different things, such as equipment and the site. That is a question for the City, not something to fix in code.

A file that changes daily

The City describes the layer as updated daily, and it asks users to check each record’s CREATE_DATE and UPDATE_DATE. On 22 October 2026 the portal’s CSV had 337 rows while the live map service reported 340. Students learn to write which copy they used and when.

Coding and AI

Would an AI tool have noticed the year 3013?

Perhaps. It might also have quietly averaged it in.

Hand the Kitchener playgrounds file to an AI tool and ask for the average construction year, and it may skip the PRE rows, treat blanks as zero, or not mention the 3013 date at all. The number it prints will look reasonable either way. A student who has written the checker knows exactly which rows were left out and why, and can say so.

That is the habit we teach children: check the data, decide the rules, write the code, and explain the result in a sentence that is true. Our classes cover how AI works and where it goes wrong, but children do their own reasoning. At home, OpenAI requires a parent’s permission for ChatGPT users under 18.

Ontario’s Grade 8 expectation is about using data to inform decisions; a decision built on an unchecked 3013 is not informed. The longer argument is in our case for learning to code in 2026.

FAQ

Kitchener families ask

Common questions from Kitchener parents.

Do you run coding classes for kids in Kitchener?

Yes, though online rather than in a Kitchener room. Lessons are live: a teacher watches each screen, and a child learns either among 15 to 20 classmates or alone with the teacher.

Are CEMC's resources really free?

CEMC says there is no registration or fee required to access its resources, which include past contests, courseware and Python tutorials. Its courseware awards no certificate.

Why pay for a class if free material exists?

Pay only if the class adds something free pages cannot: a live teacher who watches your child work, feedback at the moment of confusion, and a steady plan. If free material is working, keep using it.

What does Grade 7 math ask about coding?

Grade 7 adds sub-programs, reusable pieces of code that most languages call functions, along with loops that run a defined number of times.

What age can my child start?

Six, with blocks. The courses for children cover ages 6 to 12; older students move on to our teen courses.

Can my child write a CEMC contest?

Yes, through their school. The Beaver Computing Challenge 7/8 level is open to Grade 8 or below, and Gauss is for Grades 7 and 8.

When are classes held?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. We give every time in Eastern time.

Do you copy contest problems?

No. We never reproduce CEMC problems or solutions. Our teachers write original practice problems and label them as ours.

Will my child use AI in class?

They learn how AI works and how to check its answers, but they write and explain their own code.

How much do classes cost?
