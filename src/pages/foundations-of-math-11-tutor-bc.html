Four big ideas

Similar “shapes and objects have proportional relationships that can be described, measured, and compared.” Optimization, defined as “a mathematical analysis used to determine the minimum or maximum output for a given situation”, “informs the decision-making process in situations involving extreme values.”

Logical reasoning, “the process of using a strategic, systematic series of steps based on valid mathematical procedures and given statements to form a conclusion”, “helps us discover and describe mathematical truths.” And statistical analysis “allows us to notice, wonder about, and answer questions about variation”.

Inquiry questions to use as revision

Three of the curriculum’s sample questions make good spoken revision: “How do the properties of 3D objects change in an enlargement or a reduction?”, “How do graphs aid in understanding a situation that is being optimized?” and “How does puzzle and game analysis help us in the world outside the math classroom?”

If your teen can answer each in a few sentences with an example, they understand the scale, optimization and reasoning strands. Our two worked problems below answer the first two.

Why the choice matters for Grade 12

Many UBC programs name Pre-Calculus 12 for BC applicants. When we read UBC’s requirements tool on 22 October 2026, Science and Commerce both listed it, Arts listed it for students who “intend to major in Economics”, and Media Studies said “Pre-Calculus 12 is strongly recommended”.

A Foundations 11 student who later wants one of those programs usually has extra ground to cover, since the Grade 12 pre-calculus course assumes Grade 11 pre-calculus algebra. Whether a school lets a student switch, and how, is the school’s rule; ask the counsellor before Grade 11 starts.

Who Foundations suits

Students who like arguing a point, analysing a game or making sense of data often enjoy Foundations 11 more than any math course before it. Its statistics strand is genuinely useful in every field, from biology to journalism.

If a student is unsure, our honest advice is to look at the Grade 12 courses their likely programs require and work backwards. For the algebra-heavy route, our precalculus tutoring page shows the kind of work involved.

Worked problem 1, ours

Optimization with a feasible region: the garden club

This problem is ours. It follows the curriculum’s optimization content, a feasible region and an objective function, then asks the question real plans always raise: what if the answer has to be a whole number? Technology does the graphing and the checking, as the course expects.

The problem. A school garden club grows tomatoes and kale in trays. A tomato tray needs 3 m2 of bed space and 2 volunteer hours and yields about 5 kg; a kale tray needs 2 m2 and 3 hours and yields about 4 kg. The club has 60 m2 of beds and 48 volunteer hours for the season. How many trays of each should it plant to harvest the most?

  1. Name the variables. Let t be tomato trays and k be kale trays.
  2. Write the constraints. Space: 3t + 2k ≤ 60. Time: 2t + 3k ≤ 48. And t ≥ 0, k ≥ 0, because trays cannot be negative.
  3. Write the objective function. Harvest H = 5t + 4k, to be maximized.
  4. Graph the feasible region. With graphing technology, shade the region that satisfies all four inequalities. It is a four-sided shape with corners on the axes and one where the two boundary lines cross.
  5. Find the crossing corner. Solve 3t + 2k = 60 and 2t + 3k = 48 together. Triple the first and double the second: 9t + 6k = 180 and 4t + 6k = 96. Subtract: 5t = 84, so t = 16.8 and k = 4.8.
  6. Test the objective at every corner. The maximum of a linear objective over a region like this occurs at a corner. The program checks all four, then checks every whole-number plan.

The corner answer and the real answer

Mathematically, the optimal point is the corner (16.8, 4.8), with 103.2 kg. But a club cannot plant 0.8 of a tray. The obvious fix, rounding to 17 tomato trays and 5 kale trays, needs 3(17) + 2(5) = 61 m2, one more than the club has. Rounding broke a constraint.

Checking every whole-number plan finds 18 tomato trays and 3 kale trays: exactly 60 m2 and 45 hours, for 102 kg. Only 1.2 kg below the corner value, and actually possible. That gap between the ideal and the feasible is the kind of judgement the optimization big idea calls decision making.

How graphs help, in the curriculum’s terms

The inquiry question asks how graphs aid in understanding an optimized situation. Here the graph shows at a glance that the time limit only matters near the top of the region, and that the winning corner is where both limits bite at once.

A competency elaboration asks students to judge the reasonableness of “optimal solutions”. Testing the rounded plan against the constraints, as above, is that judgement in practice.

Worked problem 2, ours

Scale models and the square-cube law

The scale-model content asks students to compare “the properties of similar objects (length, area, volume)” and names the “square-cube law”. This problem of ours shows why the three properties scale so differently.

The problem. A student builds a 1:25 scale model of a garden shed. The model is 12 cm long, its walls and roof use 0.18 m2 of card, and it encloses 2,000 cm3. Find the shed’s real length, surface area and volume, and explain why the surface area to volume ratio changes.

  1. Lengths scale by the factor. The scale factor is 25, so the real length is 12 × 25 = 300 cm, which is 3 m.
  2. Areas scale by the square of the factor. 252 = 625, so the real surface is 0.18 × 625 = 112.5 m2.
  3. Volumes scale by the cube. 253 = 15,625, so the real volume is 2,000 × 15,625 = 31,250,000 cm3. Since 1 m3 is 1,000,000 cm3, that is 31.25 m3.
  4. Compare the ratios. In the model, surface over volume is 1,800 cm2 / 2,000 cm3 = 0.9 per cm. In the shed it is 1,125,000 cm2 / 31,250,000 cm3 = 0.036 per cm, exactly 1/25 of the model’s.
  5. State the law. Scaling lengths by k multiplies area by k2 and volume by k3, so surface per unit of volume falls by a factor of k. That is the square-cube law.

Unit choice is half the problem

The competencies mention “scale calculations and unit choice” for good reason. Mixing square metres of card with cubic centimetres of volume is the fastest way to a wrong ratio. Converting everything to centimetres before step 4 (0.18 m2 is 1,800 cm2) keeps it honest.

A useful habit: write the unit after every number, every time, and check that both sides of an equation carry the same unit.

Why the law matters outside class

The square-cube law explains why a large building needs proportionally less heating surface per cubic metre than a small one, why ice cubes melt faster when crushed, and why a model bridge that holds up perfectly may fail when built full size: weight grows with volume, but the strength of a beam grows with its cross-section area.

So the sample question on 3D enlargements gets a numerical reply: every doubling of length leaves each cubic unit with half the surface it had.

Practice, ours

Twelve Foundations of Math 11 practice questions

Every question here is our own work, written for this guide rather than taken from a school, a district or an exam. They cover reasoning, angles, graphs, statistics and scale. Answer on paper, using a graphing tool where the course would.

  1. Deductive proof. Prove that the sum of any two odd integers is even.
  2. Counter-example. A student notices that n2 + n + 41 is prime for n = 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4, and conjectures it is always prime. Find a counter-example.
  3. Game analysis. Two players take turns removing 1, 2 or 3 counters from a pile of 15. Whoever takes the last counter wins. Should you go first, and how many should you take?
  4. Parallel lines. A transversal crosses two parallel lines, making alternate interior angles of (3x + 10)° and (5x − 30)°. Find x and the angle.
  5. Polygons. Find the sum of the interior angles of a hexagon, and each interior angle of a regular hexagon.
  6. Solution region. Is the point (2, 3) in the region defined by y < −x + 6 and y ≥ 2x − 3? Show the test.
  7. Quadratic graph. For y = −x2 + 6x − 5, give the vertex, the maximum, the axis of symmetry and the intercepts.
  8. Linear-quadratic system. Find where y = x2 − 4 and y = 2x − 1 meet.
  9. z-score. A class test has mean 72 and standard deviation 8. What is the z-score of a mark of 84, and what does it tell you?
  10. Confidence interval. A school survey reports that 62% of students walk or cycle to school, accurate within 4 percentage points, 19 times out of 20. State the interval and explain what 19 times out of 20 means.
  11. Scale. A 1:200 model of a building is 45 cm tall and its footprint is 0.06 m2. Find the real height and footprint.
  12. Square-cube law. Every length of a solid is tripled. By what factors do its surface area, volume and surface-to-volume ratio change?

Answers, ours

Answers, explained

We checked the numerical answers by computation and the proofs line by line. In Foundations 11 the explanation earns the marks, so each answer gives it.

  1. Proof. Any odd integer can be written 2m + 1 for some integer m. Two odd integers sum to (2m + 1) + (2n + 1) = 2(m + n + 1), which is 2 times an integer, so it is even. Checking examples is inductive; this argument is deductive, because it covers every case.
  2. n = 40. 402 + 40 + 41 = 1,681 = 41 × 41, which is not prime. (n = 41 also works.) Five true cases never prove a pattern; one counter-example disproves it.
  3. Go first and take 3. That leaves 12. Whatever your opponent takes (1, 2 or 3), you take enough to make the total for the round 4, leaving 8, then 4, then 0. Multiples of 4 are losing positions for whoever must move.
  4. x = 20, angle 70°. Alternate interior angles are equal: 3x + 10 = 5x − 30, so 40 = 2x.
  5. 720° and 120°. A polygon with n sides has interior angles summing to (n − 2) × 180°; for n = 6 that is 720°, and 720 / 6 = 120.
  6. Yes. First inequality: 3 < −2 + 6 = 4, true. Second: 3 ≥ 2(2) − 3 = 1, true. Both hold, so the point is inside.
  7. Vertex (3, 4), maximum 4, axis x = 3, x-intercepts 1 and 5, y-intercept −5. It factors as −(x − 1)(x − 5), and the vertex lies midway between the zeros.
  8. (3, 5) and (−1, −3). Set x2 − 4 = 2x − 1, so x2 − 2x − 3 = 0 and (x − 3)(x + 1) = 0. Substitute each x into the line.
  9. z = 1.5. (84 − 72) / 8 = 1.5: the mark is one and a half standard deviations above the mean. In a roughly normal distribution, that is well above most of the class.
  10. 58% to 66%. 62 ± 4. “19 times out of 20” is a 95% confidence level: if the survey were repeated many times in the same way, about 95% of the intervals produced would contain the true value. It does not mean the true value is 95% likely to be in this particular interval.
  11. 90 m tall; footprint 2,400 m2. 45 cm × 200 = 9,000 cm = 90 m. Areas scale by 2002 = 40,000, and 0.06 × 40,000 = 2,400.
  12. Area × 9, volume × 27, surface-to-volume ratio ÷ 3. Area scales by 32, volume by 33, and the ratio by 9/27 = 1/3.

Question 3 is the kind of puzzle the curriculum’s game analysis means, and it is also a programming exercise: a few lines of code can test the multiples-of-4 strategy against every possible reply. If that appeals, the senior coding courses are described on our Computer Programming 11 and 12 guide.

Preparing, ours

How to prepare for Foundations 11 tests and projects

Foundations 11 assessments often mix calculation with written explanation, and some teachers set a statistics project. The format is the school’s choice; this preparation plan is ours.

  1. Ask how the course is assessed. Tests, a statistics project, presentations, and which graphing tools are allowed.
  2. Learn the vocabulary of reasoning. Conjecture, inductive, deductive, counter-example, proof. Write one example of each.
  3. Practise proofs in a set format. Statement and reason, line by line, for angle proofs; the teacher’s preferred layout is the one to use.
  4. Get fluent with one graphing tool. Inequalities, quadratics and systems are graphed with technology in this course, so speed with the tool matters.
  5. For optimization, follow the same five steps every time. Variables, constraints, objective, region, corners. Then ask whether the answer must be a whole number.
  6. For statistics, start from the question. Pose it, decide what data answers it, collect, summarise with mean and standard deviation, and answer the question in a sentence.
  7. For scale, write units everywhere. Convert before comparing, and square or cube the factor for area or volume.
  8. Keep an explanation log. For each topic, one paragraph in your own words explaining why the method works.
  9. Do a mixed review every two weeks. Our twelve questions above make a starting set.
  10. Before a test, explain three answers out loud to someone who has not taken the course. If they follow, you are ready.

For parents

Ask your teen to prove one small fact to you, such as why two odd numbers add to an even one. How they explain it shows their stage.

Foundations 11 rewards talking about math. A teen who explains aloud at home tends to write better answers in tests.

For students

After every answer, add one sentence explaining why it makes sense. That sentence is often worth more marks than the number.

Look for a counter-example before trying to prove something. It saves time when the claim is false.

Foundations of Math 11: common questions

What is Foundations of Math 11 in BC?

It is a BC Grade 11 mathematics course. Its curriculum covers forms of mathematical reasoning and proof, angle relationships, graphical analysis of inequalities, quadratics and systems using technology, optimization with feasible regions, applications of statistics, scale models and financial literacy.

Do you grant Foundations of Math 11 credit?

No: we do not grant provincial credits. Think of us as a second teacher beside the class. The Foundations of Mathematics 11 credit itself has to come from a school that runs and marks the course, online or in person.

Is Foundations of Math 11 easier than Pre-calculus 11?

It is different. Foundations 11 has lighter algebra but asks for proofs, written reasoning, statistics and judgement about whether an answer makes sense. Its Grade 11 neighbour, Pre-calculus 11, leans harder on algebra and trigonometry. Students who find algebra hard often still find Foundations 11 demanding in its own way.

Can I go to university with Foundations of Math 11?

It depends on the program. When we checked UBC's requirements tool for BC applicants on 22 October 2026, Arts listed English Studies 12 or English First Peoples 12 and asked for Pre-Calculus 12 only from students planning an Economics major, while Science and Commerce listed Pre-Calculus 12. Check each program you are considering.

What comes after Foundations of Math 11?

The BC mathematics course list includes Foundations of Mathematics 12, the next course in the same line. Some students also take Statistics 12. Moving to Pre-calculus 12 instead usually needs Pre-calculus 11 algebra, and the school decides the rules.

Does Foundations of Math 11 include statistics?

Yes. The curriculum lists posing a question about variation, collecting and interpreting data, and measures of central tendency, standard deviation, confidence intervals, z-scores and distributions.

Do students graph by hand in Foundations 11?

The curriculum attaches the note "using technology only" to its graphical analysis content, which covers linear inequalities, quadratic functions, systems of equations and optimization. So students use graphing tools, and the skill being assessed is reading and explaining the graph.

How should my teen prepare for Foundations 11 tests?

Find out the assessment format, learn the vocabulary of reasoning, practise proofs in the teacher's layout, get quick with one graphing tool, follow fixed steps for optimization and statistics, keep units on every number, and practise explaining answers aloud.

What lesson times work in BC?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. The province dropped clock changes in 2026, which means your agreed lesson hour is the same in January as in June.

How much is Foundations 11 tutoring?

The first class is free. Ongoing classes are billed by the month for eight live lessons, with no term contract, and stop whenever a month ends. The fees section on this page lists the group and private amounts.
