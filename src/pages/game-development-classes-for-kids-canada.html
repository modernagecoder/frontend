Game development classes for kids in Canada

For many Canadian children, games are the first thing they want to make with a computer, and Roblox is often the first place they try. That is a good instinct: building a game needs every core coding idea, from repeating steps to keeping score. This page shows where game making meets the Ontario and BC curricula, sets out Roblox's own rules for young players and creators in Roblox's words, matches tools to ages, and walks through one original Roblox mechanic in Luau, including the bug almost every child writes first.

Why making games fits what Canadian schools teach

No province we read has a game design course for children, but the coding lines in their documents describe exactly what a game needs. These are the lines we read on 22 October 2026.

A game is a good teacher

A game gives instant feedback: the character either jumps or it does not. Children fix bugs because they want to play, not because a worksheet asks them to, and they meet ideas like events, variables and loops long before anyone names them.

The risk is that the game takes over and the learning stops. Our classes keep a simple rule: before adding a feature, the child says how it will work in words.

What children carry back to school

A child who has scripted a scoreboard has met variables; one who has made enemies patrol has met loops; one who has made a door open only with a key has met conditions. When those ideas appear in Ontario Grade 5 math as "conditional statements", they are already familiar.

We are not connected with any ministry, and our classes do not replace school coding. They give children a reason to practise it.

How our Roblox classes use these rules

Children build in Roblox Studio, test their games privately, and share them with classmates through the teacher. We ask parents to set up Roblox's parental controls first, and we never ask a child to chat with strangers, buy Robux or verify their age as part of a class.

Publishing a finished game more widely is a family decision. Where Roblox requires age verification or two-factor authentication for that step, a parent handles it.

What we leave out on purpose

Earning money from games is not part of our teaching for children, even though Roblox offers it to creators aged 13 and over. The aim is to learn to design and code; making a game fun for a friend is reward enough at this age.

Roblox Studio is described by Roblox as "a free application available on Windows and Mac", so a child needs one of those computers, not a tablet or Chromebook, for Studio work.

Why typed Luau is worth the jump

Roblox describes Luau as "the scripting language creators use in Roblox Studio", "derived from Lua 5.1". It is a real, typed programming language, so a child who learns it is learning text coding, not a toy. The same ideas, functions, events and variables, carry straight into Python later.

The jump from blocks to Luau is the hardest moment in a young game maker's path. We make it slowly: the same small mechanic is built in Scratch first, then in Luau, side by side.

Signs a child is ready for Roblox Studio

They can type a short line without hunting for every key, read an error message aloud, and explain what a Scratch "when this sprite is clicked" block does. If not yet, a few more weeks of Scratch games is time well spent.

Scratch itself is no small thing: the Scratch Foundation says millions of children have created over a billion Scratch projects.

An original Roblox mechanic

The Maple Leaf Collector: a scoreboard, a touch and one famous bug

Our own small game, designed for children of 9 to 12 in their first Roblox weeks: maple leaves float around a park, a player touches one to collect it, the scoreboard counts it, and the leaf grows back after five seconds. Two short scripts do all of it. They are written for Roblox Studio and were not run outside it, since Luau runs inside Studio; the Python simulation further down was run.

The famous bug. Roblox's own scripting guide warns that "the Touched event fires multiple times in quick succession based on subtle physical collisions." A first version without the collected flag adds a point every time, so a single step on a leaf can score several points. Roblox calls the fix a debounce: "a coding technique that prevents a function from running too many times or an input from triggering multiple times."

What the child learns from it

Events: code that waits for something to happen, here a touch, instead of running top to bottom. State: a variable, collected, that remembers what has already happened. Order: the flag must be set before the wait, or a second touch slips in during it.

These are the same ideas Ontario's Grade 3 and 4 math call concurrent, repeating and nested events, met in a form a child wants to get right.

How a class builds it

First on paper: what happens when a player touches a leaf, in four numbered steps. Then the scoreboard script alone, tested by watching the board appear. Then the leaf, deliberately without the debounce, so every child sees the score jump. Only then the fix.

Each child then changes one thing on purpose, such as the regrow time or a golden leaf worth five, and explains the change to the class.

Design before code

A one-page game design sheet to use at home

Our own worksheet. A child who can fill it in has a game they can build; a child who cannot needs more thinking, not more code.

  1. The goal in one sentence. "Collect 20 maple leaves before the snow falls." If it takes two sentences, the game is two games.
  2. What the player can do. List every action: walk, jump, collect. Each one becomes an event in code.
  3. What the game remembers. Score, lives, time left. Each one becomes a variable.
  4. How it is won and how it is lost. Both must be possible, or it is not yet a game.
  5. One thing that changes over time. Faster leaves, more enemies, less time. This is what keeps it interesting, and it is usually a loop.
  6. The first bug you expect. Write it down before building. Children who predict bugs find them faster.

Step 6 surprises parents. Asking a ten year old "what will go wrong?" before they start is the single habit that most separates children who finish games from children who abandon them.

Thinking, not copying

Children who script their own games

Our promise to families in Canada is the one we make everywhere: children learn to think a problem through and solve it, not to copy an answer, a tutorial or an AI's output. Game making is full of copy-and-paste scripts online, so we are strict about it.

Typed, not pasted

Every script in a child's game is typed by the child and explained line by line. Free models and scripts from the internet are studied, never dropped in unread.

Bugs on purpose

Like the missing debounce, the common bugs are shown deliberately, so children recognise them in their own games.

No AI shortcuts

Children under 13 do not use adult AI chatbots in our classes; OpenAI says ChatGPT is not meant for them, and Claude is 18 and over.

Parents in Canada ask

Game development and Roblox for kids: common questions

What age can a child start game development?

Our Game Development for Kids course starts at 8 with Scratch, and our Roblox Game Coding course takes children from 8 to 14. Children who are not yet comfortable reading usually start with Scratch.

Is Roblox safe for my child to learn coding on?

Roblox sets its own rules: every user must complete an age check to access chat, the Chat Tab is only for users 9 and over, voice chat with friends is off for all users under 13, and trusted friends for under-13s need a parent's permission. We ask parents to set up Roblox's parental controls before class.

Does my child need to buy Robux for your classes?

No. Nothing in our classes requires purchases. Roblox itself says only users who have reached the age of majority may buy Robux or make real-money transactions.

What language does Roblox use?

Luau. Roblox describes it as the scripting language creators use in Roblox Studio, derived from Lua 5.1. It is typed text code, so children learn real programming.

Can kids earn money from Roblox games?

Roblox says creators who are 13 or older and have earned at least 30,000 Earned Robux can use its Developer Exchange. We do not teach earning as a goal; our classes are about designing and coding.

What computer does my child need?

For Roblox Studio, a Windows or Mac computer, because Roblox describes Studio as a free application for Windows and Mac. Scratch runs in a web browser on most computers.

Should my child start with Scratch or Roblox?

Most children should start with Scratch, where blocks teach events, loops and variables without typing. Once a child types comfortably and can read an error aloud, Roblox Studio and Luau are the next step.

How does game design connect to school in Canada?

Ontario math asks for code with repeating events in Grade 3 and nested events in Grade 4, and BC's Grade 6 design module names visual programming tools such as Kodu and Scratch. Making games practises exactly those ideas.

When are game classes held for Canadian families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Every class is booked in your own province's time.

What will my child build?

Games they design themselves, starting with Scratch games and moving to Roblox mechanics like our Maple Leaf Collector, a scoreboard and a collectable leaf that must count each touch once.
