Western Australia is so wide that no single flat map grid can cover it without bending distances. Surveyors solve this in two ways. The national grid cuts the world into strips six degrees of longitude wide, and Western Australia straddles four of them. Then, for towns where engineers need plans that match the ground, Landgate keeps nineteen local project grids, each centred on its own meridian. Our learners work out which strip every grid belongs to, find the two towns less than half a degree apart that land in different zones, and learn why a coordinate can jump by hundreds of kilometres at an invisible line in the desert.

## Which map zone does each WA project grid sit in?

Landgate's nineteen project grids, each central meridian run through the standard UTM zone rule. Our calculation, 7 October 2026.

**Selected Landgate project grids and the national zone each central meridian falls in (our calculation)**

| Grid | Central meridian | Zone | Zone centre |
|---|---|---|---|
| Christmas Island | 105°37'30" E | 48 | 105° E |
| Carnarvon | 113°40' E | 49 | 111° E |
| Exmouth | 114°04' E | 50 | 117° E |
| Perth | 115°49' E | 50 | 117° E |
| Karratha | 116°56' E | 50 | 117° E |
| Goldfields | 121°30' E | 51 | 123° E |
| Broome | 122°20' E | 51 | 123° E |
| Kununurra | 128°45' E | 52 | 129° E |

### 1. Find the strip

Add 180 to the longitude, divide by 6, round down and add 1. A meridian of 115°49' gives zone 50. The code is one line; the tests are where the learning happens.

### 2. Count the zones

Of the 19 grids, 13 sit in zone 50, 3 in zone 51, and one each in zones 49, 52 and, for Christmas Island, 48. The mainland grids alone span four zones.

### 3. Find the edge

Carnarvon's grid meridian is 113°40' and Exmouth's is 114°04', only 0.4 degrees apart, yet the zone edge at 114° E runs between them. Karratha's sits just 4 minutes from the centre of its zone.

### Why towns get their own grid

Landgate says that within a project grid, a distance on a plan equals the ground distance to within 20 parts per million, about 2 cm in a kilometre. Each grid gets its own scale factor to make that true: Busselton's is 0.99999592 and the Goldfields grid's is 1.00004949. Learners see why one six-degree strip cannot promise the same everywhere inside it.

Zone numbers and distances to zone centres on this page are our calculations from Landgate's published central meridians. Landgate notes that MGA2020 is a Transverse Mercator projection that follows the Universal Transverse Mercator system; for full grid parameters, use its spreadsheet and the national technical manuals it points to.

## Five habits for any program that stores locations

Learned on WA map grids, then used on GPS tracks, delivery apps, farm records and anything with an easting in it.

**How to keep coordinates honest**

| Habit | In practice | What it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| Store the zone with the point | Keep zone 50 next to every easting and northing | Two points that look close but are hundreds of kilometres apart |
| Name the datum | Say GDA2020 or GDA94, never just "the coordinates" | Positions shifted by a datum change |
| Test the edges | Check a point just either side of 114° E | A rule that fails exactly where it matters |
| Keep plan and ground apart | Record whether a distance is on the grid or on the ground | Small scale errors adding up over long lines |
| Use the published tools | Check results against the official calculators | Trusting your own code with nobody checking it |

The first habit causes the most surprises. An easting of 400,000 metres in zone 50 and the same number in zone 51 are two different places a long way apart, so a spreadsheet that drops the zone column has quietly broken every distance it calculates afterwards.

## An AI tool can convert coordinates instantly. Why should a WA student understand zones?

Because a conversion that ignores the zone gives an answer in the right format and the wrong place.

Ask an assistant for the distance between two eastings and it will subtract them without blinking, whether or not they share a zone. Ask it to write a zone function and it may produce something that works for Perth and fails quietly for a point at exactly 114 degrees east. The numbers come back neat and plausible, which is precisely what makes the mistake hard to see.

A student who has placed Landgate's grids into their zones knows the questions to ask of any generated answer about location: which zone, which datum, plan or ground, and what happens at the edge? Those questions apply to mapping, mining, agriculture and logistics, all of which run on coordinates.

So the reason for a Western Australian student to learn to code in 2026 is not to out-type an AI tool. It is to know when its coordinates point to the right place. The longer argument is in [why teenagers should still learn to code in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
