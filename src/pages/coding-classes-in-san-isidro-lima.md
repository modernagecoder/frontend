## How old are the people who live in San Isidro?

Older than Lima as a whole. INEI's census of 2025 gives San Isidro a total population of 73,402, and among the 69,137 people it actually counted, the largest single age group is 60 and over, with 19,540 people.

### The age mix

By our own division of INEI's counted figures, people of 60 and over make up about 28.3% of San Isidro, against 15.5% across Lima Metropolitana. Adults of 45 to 59 (14,895 people) and of 30 to 44 (14,891) are each about a fifth of the district.

Children are a small share: 3,596 aged 6 to 11 and 4,048 aged 12 to 17, roughly 5.2% and 5.9% of the people counted. Women outnumber men in INEI's total, 40,065 to 33,337, and INEI estimates that 4,265 residents were missed by the count.

### Why that shapes this page

A district where most residents are adults, and a large share are past working age, needs a coding page that speaks to adults first. That is unusual on the internet, where nearly every coding offer is aimed at children.

It also explains why online classes suit San Isidro well. Plenty of adults would rather not drive across Lima for an evening course, and a small number of children of any one age makes a local specialist class hard to fill. Online, neither problem exists.

## Which age group took to the internet fastest? A Python lesson with a trap

We use the INEI table above with adult beginners in their first month. The question is simple, the code is short, and the bug it reveals is one that working analysts make too: asking a dictionary for its largest item and getting the largest label instead.

### The task

The learner types the 2023 and 2026 columns into two Python dictionaries, one entry per age group, and works out each group's rise in percentage points. Then come three questions: which group rose most, which rose least, and what is the order from top to bottom?

Version 1 answers them with max, min and sorted, the obvious tools. It runs without an error message, and the first answer is even correct.

### The trap

The rises are right: 15.9 points for people of 60 and over, 1.7 for those of 19 to 24. But "rose least" says 12 a 18, which rose 2.8. Called on a dictionary, max and min compare **the keys, not the values**: they look for the label that comes last or first as text.

So why was "rose most" correct? Luck. The label 60 y mas happens to come last when the labels are compared as text, and that group also happens to have the biggest rise. A program that is right by coincidence is the most dangerous kind.

**Version 1: the obvious tools, asked the wrong question** (Python)
```python
use_2023 = {"6 a 11": 60.1, "12 a 18": 89.7, "19 a 24": 95.6,
            "25 a 40": 90.9, "41 a 59": 76.5, "60 y mas": 40.8}
use_2026 = {"6 a 11": 68.2, "12 a 18": 92.5, "19 a 24": 97.3,
            "25 a 40": 95.2, "41 a 59": 86.0, "60 y mas": 56.7}

rise = {}
for group in use_2026:
    rise[group] = round(use_2026[group] - use_2023[group], 1)

print("Rises:", rise)
print("Rose most:", max(rise))
print("Rose least:", min(rise))
print("In order:", sorted(rise))
```
It prints Rises: {'6 a 11': 8.1, '12 a 18': 2.8, '19 a 24': 1.7, '25 a 40': 4.3, '41 a 59': 9.5, '60 y mas': 15.9}, then Rose most: 60 y mas, Rose least: 12 a 18 and In order: ['12 a 18', '19 a 24', '25 a 40', '41 a 59', '6 a 11', '60 y mas'].

The order line is the clue a careful learner spots. It is alphabetical, with "6 a 11" after "41 a 59", because text is compared character by character and "4" comes before "6". Nothing in that list has anything to do with the rises.

**Version 2: tell Python what to compare** (Python)
```python
# keep the two dictionaries and the rise loop from Version 1
most = max(rise, key=rise.get)
least = min(rise, key=rise.get)
print("Rose most:", most, rise[most])
print("Rose least:", least, rise[least])
print("In order:", sorted(rise, key=rise.get, reverse=True))
```
With Version 1's dictionaries and loop above it, it prints Rose most: 60 y mas 15.9, Rose least: 19 a 24 1.7 and In order: ['60 y mas', '41 a 59', '6 a 11', '25 a 40', '12 a 18', '19 a 24'].

Passing key=rise.get tells each function to rank the groups by their values. Now the least is 19 to 24, with 1.7 points, and the order runs from the oldest group down to young adults, who were already near the top in 2023 and had little room left to grow. That last observation is the real lesson for an analyst: a small rise can mean a group that is already almost full.

1. **First week, no code.** Rank the six groups by their 2026 figure, by hand. Then rank them by their rise. Which group changes place the most, and why?
2. **First month.** Type Version 1 and predict every line before running it. Write down which answers you trust and which you do not.
3. **Second month.** INEI's bulletin also prints 2024 and 2025. Add both years as dictionaries and find every group whose use fell from one year to the next. (There are two: 6 to 11 and 12 to 18, between 2024 and 2025.)
4. **Going further.** Work out each rise as a percentage of the 2023 figure instead of in points. Does the ranking change? Explain in two English sentences why both measures are honest and why they disagree.

## What about the children and grandchildren?

They learn with us too, in groups of their own age. In San Isidro the family pattern we see most is a parent or grandparent taking an adult course while a child takes a children's one, sometimes at the same hour on two computers.

Look through the CNEB's plans of study and you will not find computing as a subject at any grade. Digital skills sit in competency 28, a cross-curricular competency that every area is expected to touch and none owns, so most children never write a program at school.

Bilingual, Cambridge and IB schools in and around San Isidro sometimes offer computer science as an exam course. Where a child takes IGCSE or IB computer science, our courses follow that syllabus in English and leave assessment entirely to the school.

At 6 to 8, Scratch: blocks that snap together into animations and simple games. From about 9, typed Python with turtle drawings and small projects. From 13, Python, Java, web building or AI for teenagers, and the Cambridge or IB course where a pupil needs it.

Every class is in English. Children at bilingual schools find that natural; children at Spanish-medium schools find it harder at first and then, usually, rather proud of themselves.

## If AI can write code, why should an adult learn to program?

Because someone has to know whether the code is right. AI assistants write plausible programs quickly; the skill that matters is reading them, testing them and spotting the error, exactly as in the lesson above.

### Reason first

Before any tool is opened, the learner says in plain English what the program should do and how they will check it. That habit catches most mistakes before they are typed.

### Use AI as a reviewer

Adults in our courses use AI assistants, and learn where they go wrong: invented functions, quiet assumptions, answers that are right by luck. The tool becomes a second opinion, not an oracle.

### Same teaching everywhere

A learner in San Isidro gets the same depth and care as one in Mumbai, London or Arequipa. We teach people to think through problems, not to copy answers, whoever they are.

Children follow stricter rules. Claude's terms set a minimum age of 18, and ChatGPT asks for parental permission below 18, so children in our classes do not use adult chatbot accounts. They learn the reasoning first, which is what any later tool will rely on.

## Coding classes in San Isidro: questions and answers

### Are the classes in Spanish?

No. Every class is live, online and in English only. Mentors speak clearly and adjust their pace to each learner, so adults whose English is stronger on paper than aloud can follow comfortably. Python itself is written in English words, so the vocabulary of the class is the vocabulary of the code.

### I am over 60 and have never programmed. Is that a problem?

No. Our learners range from 6 to 67, and adults who start late often do very well because they know what they want to build. The first lessons go slowly over the basics of files and windows, and one-to-one classes are available if you prefer to learn without a group.

### Which course should a working adult choose first?

If you want to write programs, start with the Python masterclass. If your work is mainly spreadsheets and reports, the data and AI course for non-programmers is gentler. The free first class is a good place to decide, because the mentor can see what you already know.

### When could I take classes in San Isidro time?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. Peru keeps the same clock all year and India is ten and a half hours ahead, so 06:00 in San Isidro is 16:30 in India.

### Do you have an office in San Isidro?

No. We have no premises in San Isidro or elsewhere in Peru. Every lesson happens over live video with a mentor based in India, so the class is the same wherever in Lima you join from.

### Can my child learn while I take an adult course?

Yes. Children join their own age group, starting with Scratch from 6 or Python from about 9, and adults join adult groups. Many families book both, sometimes at the same hour on two computers.

### Will I be allowed to use AI tools?

Adults, yes: we teach you to use AI assistants and to test what they produce. Children do not use adult chatbot accounts, because Claude is for users of 18 and over and ChatGPT needs a parent's permission for anyone under 18.

### Is there a certificate?

Learners who complete a course receive our own certificate of completion. It is not a Peruvian qualification, and we are not connected with MINEDU, SUNEDU, any university or the Municipalidad de San Isidro.

### How are fees paid?

Monthly, by card, in US dollars. A card in soles works; the bank converts each charge, so the figure on your statement can move a little between months. The first class costs nothing and needs no card.
