## What Ontario asks a Richmond Hill teen to code, Grade 9 to Grade 12

The wording in the middle column comes from the Ontario curriculum service and EQAO as we read them on 22 October 2026. The right-hand column is our own reading of what each line means at home.

**Coding in Ontario secondary courses and assessments (Ontario Ministry of Education curriculum API and EQAO, read 22 October 2026; the last column is ours)**

| Grade and course | What the official text says | Why it matters at home |
|---|---|---|
| Grade 9, MTH1W | Students &ldquo;create code by decomposing situations into computational steps in order to represent mathematical concepts and relationships&rdquo; | Breaking a problem into steps is the core programming habit, and it starts inside math class |
| Grade 9, EQAO math | Window 12 January to 5 February 2027; &ldquo;Results of this assessment are to be included as part of a student&rsquo;s final course mark&rdquo; | The provincial math test lands three weeks before the CCC, so January is a busy month |
| Grade 10, ICD2O (2023, Open) | &ldquo;Students will learn and apply coding concepts and skills to build hands-on projects and investigate artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, and other emerging digital technologies&rdquo; | The first school course that names AI; it surveys the field rather than training models |
| Grade 11, ICS3U or ICS3C (2008) | No prerequisite for either in the ministry table | A teen can start computer science in Grade 11 with no earlier course on the transcript |
| Grade 12, ICS4U (2008) | &ldquo;Students will use modular design principles to create complex and fully documented programs&rdquo;; ICS3U comes first | Large programs split into functions and modules, with documentation another person can read |

Read top to bottom, the table describes a ramp. In Grade 9 a student edits and writes short pieces of code to model math. By Grade 12 the same student is expected to plan a program in parts and document it so that a stranger could maintain it. Nothing in that ramp is out of reach for a teen who writes Python regularly, but the jump from MTH1W snippets to an ICS4U project is steep if Grade 10 and Grade 11 pass with little practice. We see that most often with students who took ICD2O as a survey course and then met ICS3U as their first experience of a program longer than a page.

### Grade 9: write, then predict

We ask Grade 9 students to say what a loop will print before they run it. That habit carries the MTH1W coding strand and makes the EQAO questions that involve code far less mysterious.

### Grade 10: build what ICD2O describes

ICD2O talks about AI and cybersecurity; our Grade 10 students write the small programs behind those words, such as a password checker or a first classifier, so the vocabulary has code underneath it.

### Grades 11 and 12: the long program

ICS3U and ICS4U reward planning. Our companion course works on design before syntax: what the functions are, what each one takes and returns, and how to test a part before the whole exists.

### School credits stay at school

Our lessons run beside the school timetable, and we do not grant Ontario credits: MTH1W, ICD2O, ICS3U and ICS4U credits come only from the school. Modern Age Coders has no link to the Ontario Ministry of Education, to EQAO or to any York Region school board.

## Can a model guess how tall a Richmond Hill street tree is from its trunk and its age?

York Region publishes a record for each street tree it owns or maintains along its regional roads, including in Richmond Hill. Our students download the Richmond Hill rows and teach a computer to guess each tree&rsquo;s height band. All figures in this section come from our own code, not from the Region.

**Our results on York Region Street Trees, MUNICIPALITY = Richmond Hill (9,505 rows fetched 22 October 2026; 9,398 with a height band; 7,518 trees to learn from, 1,880 held back to test)**

| Model | How it guesses a test tree&rsquo;s height band | Share of the 1,880 test trees it gets right |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | Always says &ldquo;2 - 5 metres&rdquo;, the most common band in the training trees | 63.9 per cent |
| 1 nearest neighbour | Copies the band of the single most similar training tree by trunk diameter and planting year | 72.3 per cent |
| 15 nearest neighbours | Takes a vote among the 15 most similar training trees | 76.8 per cent |
| 101 nearest neighbours | Takes a vote among the 101 most similar training trees | 74.5 per cent |

The program below is the whole exercise in standard-library Python. It asks the Region&rsquo;s map service for the Richmond Hill rows 2,000 at a time, keeps the three fields it needs, shuffles the trees with a fixed seed so the result can be repeated, holds back one tree in five for testing, and scores two models. Run on 22 October 2026, it printed the baseline and the 15-neighbour figures in the table:

```
import json, random, urllib.request, urllib.parse
from collections import Counter

URL = ("https://ww8.yorkmaps.ca/arcgis/rest/services/OpenData/"
       "Biodiversity/MapServer/0/query?")
ask = {"where": "MUNICIPALITY='Richmond Hill'", "returnGeometry": "false",
       "outFields": "CURRENTDBH,YEARPLANTED,TREEHEIGHT", "f": "json",
       "orderByFields": "OBJECTID", "resultRecordCount": 2000}

trees, ask["resultOffset"] = [], 0
while True:
    reply = json.loads(urllib.request.urlopen(URL + urllib.parse.urlencode(ask)).read())
    if not reply["features"]:
        break
    for f in reply["features"]:
        a = f["attributes"]
        if a["TREEHEIGHT"]:
            trees.append((int(a["CURRENTDBH"]), a["YEARPLANTED"], a["TREEHEIGHT"]))
    ask["resultOffset"] += len(reply["features"])

random.seed(2026)
random.shuffle(trees)
cut = len(trees) * 4 // 5
train, test = trees[:cut], trees[cut:]

def guess(dbh, year, k=15):
    near = sorted(train, key=lambda t: ((t[0] - dbh) / 10) ** 2 + ((t[1] - year) / 10) ** 2)
    return Counter(t[2] for t in near[:k]).most_common(1)[0][0]

common = Counter(t[2] for t in train).most_common(1)[0][0]
base = sum(t[2] == common for t in test) / len(test)
knn = sum(guess(t[0], t[1]) == t[2] for t in test) / len(test)
print(len(trees), "trees,", len(train), "train,", len(test), "test")
print("always", repr(common), "->", round(100 * base, 1), "% right")
print("15 nearest neighbours ->", round(100 * knn, 1), "% right")
```

### 1. Clean what arrives as text

The diameter field, CURRENTDBH, is stored as text, so every value comes back as a string like &ldquo;5&rdquo; and has to be turned into a number. The height is not a number at all but a band such as &ldquo;2 - 5 metres&rdquo;, which makes this a classification problem rather than a regression.

### 2. Beat the lazy answer

Because nearly two thirds of the test trees sit in one band, a model that always says &ldquo;2 - 5 metres&rdquo; already scores 63.9 per cent. Any model worth keeping has to clear that line, and the 15-neighbour vote clears it by about thirteen points.

### 3. Choose k, then check it

One neighbour copies noise from a single odd record; 101 neighbours blur real differences. Fifteen did best on this split. Students then reshuffle with a new seed to see whether fifteen still wins or whether that was luck.

### Where the model goes wrong

Accuracy hides who gets ignored. Of the 57 test trees recorded as &ldquo;0 - 2 metres&rdquo;, the 15-neighbour model got only 16 right and called 38 of them &ldquo;2 - 5 metres&rdquo;. Of 150 trees in the &ldquo;10 - 15 metres&rdquo; band it got 61 right and placed 70 one band lower. Small classes get outvoted by big ones. That is a real lesson in machine learning, not a bug in the student&rsquo;s code, and it is why we always print the breakdown beside the headline number.

Three more honest notes. A diameter of 5 appears on 2,816 of the 9,505 rows, mostly on trees planted in recent years, and a handful of rows record round values such as 100; the field does not state its unit, so we do not guess one. Planting years run from 1920 to 2026, with 170 rows planted in 2026 itself. And the file covers only the trees the Region owns or maintains: its own description says it holds street trees &ldquo;within regional road right-of-ways&rdquo;, with only &ldquo;partial data available for Regional owned street trees in rural areas&rdquo;, so it is not a census of every tree in the city. The commonest species in the Richmond Hill rows is the Ivory Silk Lilac (925 rows), ahead of the Kentucky Coffee Tree (673), and Bayview Avenue carries more rows than any other road (1,751). We use it under the York Region Open Data Licence, whose terms say users may &ldquo;Use the Information commercially&rdquo;; the Region suggests the credit &ldquo;Contains public sector information made available under The Regional Municipality of York&rsquo;s Open Data Licence&rdquo;. The City of Richmond Hill&rsquo;s own map layers carry no reuse licence, which is why we used the regional file. Nothing on this page is endorsed by York Region.

For a Grade 9 or 10 student the practical question is not which chatbot is cleverest but what happens to their own thinking when one is always open. The tree model above is a good test. ## Why a Richmond Hill teen should understand the model before an AI tool explains it

Because the useful part of the tree project is the doubt, and a tool cannot doubt on the student&rsquo;s behalf.

A nearest-neighbours model is simple enough to explain on a whiteboard: find the most similar examples you already know, and copy what they say. A teen who has written one by hand knows exactly why it fails on rare cases, why the choice of k matters, and why a single accuracy figure can flatter it. That understanding transfers to every model they will meet later, including the large ones behind chatbots.

So our order is fixed. A student first describes the problem in plain words, then writes the steps, predicts the result, runs it and compares. Only after that, and only within the age rules above, might an AI tool come in as a second opinion to be checked. Students who learn in this order become the ones who can tell when a tool is wrong.

It is also how the CCC works: three hours, an editor and no assistant, so the reasoning on the page has to be the student&rsquo;s. The longer argument is in [whether coding is still worth learning in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
