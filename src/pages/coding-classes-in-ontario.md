## The fact this page turns on

In 2020 Ontario became the first province in Canada to write coding into the mathematics curriculum itself. It is not a technology option or an after-school club. Coding sits in the **Algebra strand** from Grade 1, next to patterns, variables and equality, because writing a sequence of instructions and reasoning about a pattern are treated as the same intellectual act.

Ontario's own grade expectations make this concrete:

- **Grade 1:** write code to order a sequence of steps
- **Grade 2:** develop code to move multiple objects from one location on a grid to another
- **Grade 3:** write code to perform a repeating operation, such as a repeating "add 3"
- **Grade 4:** write and read code to create geometric designs

Most coding providers ignore this entirely and sell a games course. That is a genuine mismatch with what an Ontario child is assessed on.

## OSSD requirements, and what we are not

The Ontario Secondary School Diploma requires a minimum of 30 credits.

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| Total credits | Minimum 30 |
| Grade 9 in 2023 or earlier | 18 compulsory, 12 optional |
| Grade 9 from 2024 to 2025 onward | 17 compulsory, 13 optional |
| Online learning | At least two of the 30 credits earned online |
| Literacy | Ontario Secondary School Literacy Test, the literacy course, or adjudication |
| Community involvement | 40 hours of community involvement activities |
| Financial literacy, from 2026 to 2027 | 70 percent or higher on the financial literacy modules, for students entering Grade 9 from 2025 to 2026 |

**We are not an Ontario Ministry of Education inspected private school. We cannot grant OSSD credits, and that includes the two online credits the diploma requires.** We teach the subject underneath so the credit-bearing course at school goes better. Treat any tutoring service claiming it can award OSSD credits with caution.

The financial literacy requirement is not new content. Financial Literacy has been a named strand of the elementary mathematics curriculum since 2020. What changed is that from 2026 to 2027 it carries a graduation consequence and a 70 percent bar.

## Secondary computer studies, Grades 10 to 12

After Grade 8, coding stops being part of mathematics and becomes its own subject, governed by The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 10 to 12: Computer Studies, revised in 2008.

| Code | Course | Grade and pathway |
|---|---|---|
| ICD2O | Digital Technology and Innovations in the Changing World | Grade 10, open. Replaced ICS2O from September 2023 |
| ICS3U | Introduction to Computer Science | Grade 11, university preparation |
| ICS3C | Introduction to Computer Programming | Grade 11, college preparation |
| ICS4U | Computer Science | Grade 12, university preparation |
| ICS4C | Computer Programming | Grade 12, college preparation |

The Grade 10 course was refreshed in 2023. The Grade 11 and 12 university-preparation courses, ICS3U and ICS4U, are the ones that matter for a university application, and they still sit on the 2008 revision. A student taking ICS4U in 2026 works to expectations written before the deep learning era and before most tools a working programmer now uses existed.

The 2008 document is not worthless: its treatment of program correctness, data types and software development process has aged well. The practical gap is narrower. It cannot tell a student what version control is for, why a test suite matters, or how to work with an AI coding assistant without being deskilled by it. Those are now entry requirements.

That gap is what an Ontario secondary student comes to us for: [Git and GitHub for teens](https://learn.modernagecoders.com/courses/git-github-version-control-course-for-teens), [AI tools mastery for teens](https://learn.modernagecoders.com/courses/teens-ai-tools-mastery-course), and for competitive computer science admissions, [problem solving and data structures](https://learn.modernagecoders.com/courses/problem-solving-dsa-masterclass-teens).

## The Ontario student data project

Each market gets a project on local data chosen for a specific way of being wrong. Ontario's is a modelling error, not a definitions error.

The Independent Electricity System Operator (IESO) publishes Ontario demand for every hour going back decades, the Hourly Ontario Energy Price, and consumption aggregated by **Forward Sortation Area**, the first three characters of a postal code. Students can pull figures for the postal code they live in.

The task: line hourly Ontario demand up against outdoor temperature and decide whether temperature drives electricity use. Every student expects a strong relationship. Then they fit a straight line and the correlation comes out near zero.

**The trap:** Ontario heats in winter and cools in summer. Demand is high at minus 20 and high at plus 30, lowest on a mild spring afternoon. The true shape is a V, and a straight line through a V has almost no slope. The statistic is computed correctly and reports nothing. A student who trusts it concludes temperature does not affect electricity demand in a province where people heat with electricity in February.

The fix is to split the data into heating and cooling regimes and fit each separately, or to transform the input into **heating and cooling degree days**, which is what energy analysts actually do. The number was not miscalculated. The model was the wrong shape for the world.

Students pull current IESO figures themselves rather than using numbers printed on a web page.
