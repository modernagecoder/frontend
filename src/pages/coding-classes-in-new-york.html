New York is retiring the exam it built its diplomas around.

The short answer

New York plans to sunset the Regents exam graduation requirement and move to a Portrait of a Graduate model, which rewards demonstrated capability over rehearsed papers. Modern Age Coders builds exactly that: live coding and mathematics for ages 6 to 67, ending in work a student can show and defend. Batches of five to eight or private tuition, USD 100 and USD 150 monthly, opening class free.

For three generations a New York diploma has meant Regents exams. That is changing. The State Education Department has set out a plan to sunset the diploma assessment requirements and replace them with a Portrait of a Graduate, and the practical effect for a student now in middle school is that nobody can tell them precisely what they will be assessed on. In that situation, one strategy is obviously safer than the others.

Build something real, and be able to explain it. This page sets out what New York currently requires, what is changing, and the state's own computer science standards, which name a concept most states leave out. The wider national view is on coding classes in the United States.

NY Inspires, November 4, 2024

What is changing, and what has not changed yet

On November 4, 2024 the State Education Department presented NY Inspires, a plan to transform education in New York State, carrying forward the recommendations of the Blue Ribbon Commission on Graduation Measures. Four transformations sit at its centre.

TransformationWhat it means for a student
Adopt the Portrait of a GraduateA menu of ways to demonstrate skill across areas including critical thinking, effective communication and innovative problem solving
Redefine creditsCredit tied to demonstrated learning rather than only to seat time
Sunset diploma assessment requirementsPassing Regents exams would stop being the gate to a diploma
Move to one diplomaThe tiered local, Regents and Advanced Regents structure would collapse into a single credential

Nothing has changed yet, and we will not tell you otherwise

Until the Board of Regents approves specific changes, the current graduation requirements remain in effect for every public school student in New York State. Reporting has pointed at the 2027 to 2028 school year for the exam requirement to end. Treat that as a direction of travel, not a date to plan a timetable around, and get the position for your child's cohort from their school.

Now the part that matters strategically. A family with a child in Grade 6 or Grade 7 today genuinely cannot know which assessment regime that child will graduate under. The instinct is to wait for clarity. The better response is to notice which kinds of preparation are robust to the change and which are not.

Coaching for a specific exam paper is fragile: it is worth a great deal if the paper survives and very little if it does not. Genuine capability is robust: it helps under the current Regents system, it is precisely what a Portrait of a Graduate model asks a student to demonstrate, and it is what a university or an employer was going to want either way. A portfolio of real work is the only artefact that pays out under every version of the future being discussed.

We are not claiming to have engineered our teaching around a 2024 policy announcement. We built it this way because it is how people actually learn to program. The New York reform simply makes the argument easier to have.

The other New York exam

One exam is being retired. Another still decides everything.

There is a contradiction running through New York education policy that is worth naming, because families live inside it.

While the state moves to sunset the Regents exams as a diploma gate, New York City still admits students to its most sought-after high schools on the basis of a single test. The Hecht-Calandra Act of 1971 requires admission to the specialized high schools to be determined solely by performance on the Specialized High Schools Admissions Test. It originally covered Stuyvesant High School, the Bronx High School of Science and Brooklyn Technical High School, and the SHSAT is now the sole criterion for eight of the nine specialized high schools. The ninth, LaGuardia, admits by audition or portfolio instead.

Two different games, at the same time

A New York City family may therefore be preparing a child for a future where the diploma rewards demonstrated capability, and simultaneously for an entrance exam where one morning's score is the entire decision. Both things are true. Anybody who tells you to ignore one of them is selling you something.

Our position is unglamorous and, we think, correct. Mathematics carries both. It is the substance of the entrance exam, it underpins every serious computer science pathway afterwards, and unlike exam technique it does not evaporate when a policy changes. A student who genuinely understands ratio, algebra and proportional reasoning is better placed on the SHSAT than one who has memorised question shapes, and is still better placed four years later when the exam no longer exists in anybody's plans.

That is why mathematics is not an upsell here. It runs on the same track as the coding, with the same teacher continuity, from middle school mathematics through high school mathematics. For students who want the competitive edge specifically, olympiad and competition mathematics is the harder road and the one that builds the most durable reasoning.

Computer Science and Digital Fluency, adopted December 2020

Five concepts, including one most states omit

The Board of Regents adopted these standards in December 2020 after a two-year statewide authoring and review process, organised into grade bands of K-1, 2-3, 4-6, 7-8 and 9-12, with full implementation expected by September 2024. Four of the five concepts will look familiar from other states. The fourth will not.

Concept

Computational Thinking. Breaking a problem down, spotting the pattern, and expressing the solution as steps a machine can follow.

Taught before syntax, because a student who cannot describe a process in words will not rescue it by learning a language. See Problem Solving and Computational Thinking.

Concept

Cybersecurity. New York's distinctive call, treating security as foundational rather than as an advanced elective.

A named track, not a safety talk. Students learn how systems are actually attacked and defended, with the ethics argued rather than asserted. See Cybersecurity for Teens.

Concept

Networks and System Design. How machines are connected, and how the design of a system shapes what it can do.

Taught by building something that has to survive being used by somebody else, which is where design decisions stop being theoretical. See HTML and CSS for Beginners.

Concept

Digital Literacy. Using tools competently and understanding what they are doing on your behalf.

The least glamorous concept and the one most enrichment providers skip entirely, which is exactly why we teach it properly. See Microsoft Office for Kids.

Concept

Impacts of Computing. What the technology does to people, work and society.

Argued with students old enough to disagree with us, particularly about AI tools, where the honest position is sometimes that a thing should not be built. See AI Tools Mastery for Teens.

New York data project

Which neighbourhood has the worst noise problem?

New York's project turns on the error that has settled more bad policy arguments than any other, and it hides inside one of the largest open civic datasets in the world.

Source: NYC Open Data, 311 Service Requests Level: Grade 6 and up Concept: Computational Thinking

New York City publishes every 311 service request it receives. The dataset runs from 2010 to the present, updates daily, and had passed forty million rows by the end of 2025. Each row carries a complaint type, the agency that responded, and a location. It is one of the largest open civic datasets anywhere, and a thirteen year old can query it.

The question is deliberately ordinary. Which part of the city has the worst noise problem? A student groups the noise complaints by borough, counts them, and produces a ranking. It looks like an answer. It is not one.

The trap

Boroughs are not the same size. Ranking by raw count largely ranks boroughs by population, and the student has produced an expensive way of discovering which borough has the most people. Divide complaints by residents and the order can change completely. The count was correct. The comparison was meaningless, because there was no denominator in it.

Then it gets more interesting, because the denominator is not obvious either. Per resident is one choice. Per household is another. For a noise complaint about a bar, complaints per licensed premises might be the honest measure, and for street noise it might be per mile of road. Each choice answers a slightly different question, and the student has to say out loud which question they are answering. That single habit, stating the denominator before computing the ratio, removes a large fraction of the bad statistics an adult will meet in their life.

There is a second layer that older students reach on their own. A 311 record is not a noise event. It is somebody choosing to report a noise event. Neighbourhoods differ in how likely people are to call the city, for reasons including trust, language, and whether anybody expects it to help. So even a perfectly normalised rate measures reported noise, not noise. Saying so in the write-up is not a weakness. It is the difference between an analysis and a claim.

StepWhat the student doesWhat it teaches
CountRank boroughs by raw noise complaints and commit to the answerWrite down the naive result before critiquing it
DivideRecompute per resident and compare the two rankingsA count without a denominator compares nothing
ChooseArgue for a denominator and justify it in one sentenceThe denominator encodes the question
DoubtExplain what a complaint record actually measuresData is generated by people, not by nature
StateAnswer with the caveat included rather than appendedHonest limits make a finding stronger

Students pull live data themselves rather than working from figures printed here, which is both better practice and the only way the exercise stays current. The finished piece is short, argued, and has a chart that earns its place.

The same habits carry into data analysis and, for students who would rather build models than audit them, AI and machine learning in the USA. Finished student work sits in the student labs.

Questions

New York questions, answered plainly

Is New York really removing the Regents exam requirement?

That is the announced direction. The State Education Department presented NY Inspires on November 4, 2024, carrying forward the Blue Ribbon Commission recommendations: adopt a Portrait of a Graduate, redefine credits, sunset the diploma assessment requirements, and move to a single diploma. Reporting has pointed to the Regents exam requirement ending around the 2027 to 2028 school year. Nothing has changed yet. Until the Board of Regents approves specific changes, current graduation requirements remain in force.

If the exams are going, what should a student aim at instead?

Something that survives a rule change. A Portrait of a Graduate model rewards demonstrated capability, so a student who has genuinely built things and can explain them is in a better position than one who has only rehearsed an exam paper. That is what we were doing anyway, and it is why every course here ends in a project rather than a mock paper.

What are the New York computer science standards?

The Board of Regents adopted the New York State Learning Standards for Computer Science and Digital Fluency in December 2020, after a two-year statewide authoring and review process. There are five concept areas: Impacts of Computing, Computational Thinking, Networks and System Design, Cybersecurity, and Digital Literacy. They are organised in grade bands of K-1, 2-3, 4-6, 7-8 and 9-12, and schools were expected to be fully implementing them by September 2024.

Why does New York name cybersecurity as its own concept?

Because it decided the subject was foundational rather than advanced, and it is one of very few states to make that call at the standards level. It changes what a serious course looks like: a student is expected to understand threats, defences and consequences, not merely to avoid weak passwords. We teach it as a named track rather than as a safety lecture bolted onto something else.

Do you teach across New York State or only the city?

The whole state. Live video means Buffalo, Rochester, Syracuse, Albany, Yonkers, Long Island and every borough are identical to us, and none of them involves a journey. A learner needs a laptop or desktop, a way to be heard, and a connection that holds a call. There is no hardware to receive and no software to install.

What times work for a family on Eastern Time?

The timetable is built around your week rather than ours. Weekend slots are the easiest to arrange on Eastern Time, and weekday slots are settled individually. Everything is confirmed on the demo call before any payment, because a slot agreed in principle and impossible in practice helps nobody.

What are the fees?

USD 100 a month for a group of five to eight, or USD 150 a month for private tuition. Each is two classes a week, eight a month. Nothing is charged to enrol, nothing has to be bought, and there is no minimum term, so any month can be your last. The opening class is free.

Which ages do you take?

From six to sixty-seven. The entry point is decided by what a learner can demonstrate in the opening class, not by a birthday or a school year. That cuts both ways: a fast ten year old is moved up, and a fifteen year old with gaps is placed where the gaps actually are rather than where pride would prefer.

How big is a class, and who teaches it?

Groups run between five and eight learners with one teacher who stays with them, or one to one for a student who needs the whole hour. We do not rotate staff between sessions. Teaching is a relationship before it is a curriculum, and a teacher who has to re-learn a student every week is starting from behind.

Take the free opening class. It is taught, not sold. A teacher gives the student real work, watches how they handle it, and then recommends a level honestly. If the recommendation is to wait, or to look elsewhere, you will hear it on that call rather than after a payment.
