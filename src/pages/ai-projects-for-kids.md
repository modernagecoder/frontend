## The ten projects and what each smuggles in

With a camera and a browser: 1. The rock-paper-scissors umpire, a no-sign-in classifier trained on three hand shapes; teaches that machines learn from examples. 2. The pet mood detector, sleepy cat versus alert cat from your own photos; teaches that the machine only knows the moods you showed it. 3. The sound switch, claps versus snaps triggering pictures; teaches that classification is not just for images. 4. The drawing guesser, the public browser game where a neural network guesses sketches; teaches pattern recognition at scale and its charming failures on original monsters.

In Scratch: 5. The rule-bot, a chat character whose every reply is an if-then block; teaches programmed versus learned behaviour, the most clarifying distinction in AI. 6. The face-paint mirror with sensing blocks; teaches detection without understanding. 7. The fairness scoreboard, where scoring must be made fair for a younger sibling; teaches that rules encode values.

No screen at all: 8. The breakfast robot, scripted below. 9. The biased sorting hat, where secretly skewed training examples make the sibling-robot misfile dinosaurs; teaches that biased data makes biased machines. 10. The confidence game, betting points on how sure you are; teaches probability as honest uncertainty.

The parent-driven eleventh: a story machine where the adult holds the account and types, the child directs and judges. The child practises precision and judgement, the platform terms stay intact.

## The breakfast robot, in full

A parent becomes a robot that only learns from examples. The child teaches: Monday, cereal, milk, spoon; Tuesday, the same; Wednesday, the same. On Thursday the robot serves it unprompted and the child beams: it learned. Then Saturday arrives, the family wants pancakes, and the robot serves cereal. "I was only ever shown weekdays. To me, every day is a weekday." The child goes quiet, then: "we have to teach it Saturdays too?" That sentence is the whole lesson: machines fail where their examples end, which is the idea behind most real-world AI mistakes. Every other project on the page is this game in a different costume.

## The concept map for grown-ups

Projects 1 to 3 teach that data is the teacher, the root of all machine learning. Project 4 teaches pattern recognition and its limits. Project 5 separates programmed from learned. Project 6 builds healthy scepticism about what machines know. Project 7 opens every future AI ethics conversation. Projects 8 and 9 teach generalisation and bias. Project 10 teaches confidence and probability. Sequencing for the full arc: concepts first, blocks second, text later, with no rush at any stage.

## Frequently asked questions

**Really no accounts?** Really. Browser training tools without sign-in, Scratch without saving online, unplugged games, and adult-held accounts where one genuinely helps.

**What ages?** Unplugged from 6, camera classifiers from about 7, Scratch builds 7 to 12 by reading level. Nothing has a cliff.

**Screen time?** Three projects use no screen, and the rest are production rather than consumption: experiments, predictions, revisions.

**Equipment?** Any laptop with a camera from the last eight years, paper, and a family member willing to be a robot.

**Privacy?** No child profile exists to leak; camera projects train on things and gestures, and nothing trained on family photos leaves the browser.
