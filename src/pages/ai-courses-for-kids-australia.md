What a good course teaches

## Five things every AI course for children should cover

Whatever the tools, a course worth paying for teaches these ideas in a way a child can explain back to you.

| Idea | What a child should be able to say | How it is taught |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AI learns from examples | "It looked at lots of labelled examples and found patterns." | Sorting games, then training a tiny model |
| Examples can be missing | "If it never saw something, it cannot recognise it." | Leaving a category out on purpose and watching it fail |
| AI can be confidently wrong | "A sure-sounding answer can still be a guess." | Checking AI answers against a trusted source |
| Fairness depends on data | "Lopsided examples make lopsided answers." | Training on an unbalanced set and comparing |
| People stay responsible | "I decide whether to use what it says." | Discussion and real decisions in projects |

### What to be wary of

Courses that are mostly typing prompts into a chatbot teach a narrow skill and often rely on tools with adult age rules. Ask exactly which tools are used and who holds the account.

Be wary too of promises that a child will "master AI" in a few sessions. Understanding grows with practice, like any subject.

### What a good sign looks like

Children build something, test it on new examples, and talk about its mistakes. They can tell you why it went wrong, not just that it did.

The course links AI to coding, so children can eventually build their own models instead of only using other people's.

A worked example

## The zero problem: why the sorter never says wallaby

Here is one AI failure explained completely. Children as young as eight understand it, and it changes how they see every AI tool afterwards.

Imagine training a picture sorter on lots of photos of kangaroos and koalas, each labelled correctly. It learns well, and on new kangaroos and koalas it is usually right. Now show it a wallaby. It has never seen one, and "wallaby" is not one of its possible answers, so it must choose between the only two labels it knows. Whatever it says, it is wrong. The Australian Curriculum names this exact issue the **zero problem**: a category with zero training examples.

| Shown to the sorter | Labels it can choose from | What it can answer | Can it ever be right? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A kangaroo | Kangaroo, koala | Kangaroo | Yes |
| A koala | Kangaroo, koala | Koala | Yes |
| A wallaby | Kangaroo, koala | Kangaroo or koala | No: "wallaby" is not on its list |
| A wombat | Kangaroo, koala | Kangaroo or koala | No, for the same reason |

### What ACARA says

The curriculum's own example is "a model with many examples of horses and no zebras in its training data is likely to classify all zebras as horses". Our sorter makes the same mistake with wallabies.

### Why it matters

The sorter does not say "I don't know". It gives an answer, sometimes a confident one, because nothing in its training taught it that something else exists.

### The fix

Add examples of the missing category, add an "unsure" answer for low confidence, and always test on things the model was not trained on.

### Where children see it in real life

A song recommender that only knows what you have already played will keep suggesting more of the same. A voice assistant may struggle with words it rarely heard in training. Each is a version of the zero problem.

Once children recognise the pattern, they start asking the right question of any AI tool: what examples was this trained on, and what might it never have seen?

### The fairness link

The curriculum also asks students to consider "ensuring that the data used to train an AI model minimises any potential biases in its output and is representative of the target audience". Missing or thin categories are a common way for unfair results to creep in.

In our lessons, children train a model with a category left out, watch it fail, then add examples and watch it improve. Seeing it happen is far more convincing than being told.

The two curriculum quotations come from elaborations of AC9TDI10P01 and AC9TDI8P01 in the ACARA Version 9.0 [Digital Technologies content for Years 7 to 10](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/content/dam/en/curriculum/ac-version-9/downloads/technologies/digital-technologies/technologies-digital-technologies-curriculum-content-7-10-v9.docx), downloaded 7 October 2026. The kangaroo, koala and wallaby example is ours.

## What some AI companies say about age

Before any course puts a child in front of an AI chatbot, check the company's own rules. Here are two, quoted exactly as published.

| Service | What the company states |
| --- | --- |
| Anthropic (Claude), consumer terms | "You must be at least 18 years old or the minimum age required to consent to use the Services in your location, whichever is higher." |
| Google (Gemini Apps), Google AI plan help page | "To use Gemini Apps with a Google AI plan in most countries, you must be 13 (or the applicable age in your country) or over." |
| Other services | Read each provider's current terms yourself. One major provider's terms page could not be read automatically, so we do not quote it. |

### What this means for an AI course

A course for primary-aged children should not depend on tools that set an older minimum age. If a course does use a chatbot, ask whose account it is, what the provider's age rules say, and how conversations are supervised.

Age rules change, and they can differ by country and by product, so check the provider's current terms rather than relying on any summary, including ours.

Our AI courses for children do not require any adult chatbot account. Children learn with age-appropriate tools and with programs they write themselves in Scratch and Python.

How to choose

## A checklist for comparing AI courses

Use these questions with any provider, including us. A good course will answer all of them clearly.

| Ask | A good answer | A worrying answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which tools will my child use? | Named, age-appropriate tools and their own code | Vague, or adult chatbots |
| Whose accounts are they? | No accounts needed, or the family's with settings explained | Accounts created for the child by the provider |
| Will my child build something? | Yes, and test it on new examples | Mostly watching or prompting |
| How do you teach AI mistakes? | Children cause, find and fix them | Mistakes are not discussed |
| How does it connect to coding? | It leads into Scratch and Python | It stands alone |
| What happens in a trial? | A real lesson with your child doing the work | A sales presentation |

If your child is ready to type code, our [Python AI course for kids](/python-ai-course-for-kids-australia) shows a full worked example of a model learning from sentences. Younger children often start with [coding games](/coding-games-for-kids-australia) and Scratch before any AI work.

At school

## AI in the Australian Curriculum

AI is not an add-on in Australian schools. Version 9 of the curriculum mentions it repeatedly in the Digital Technologies elaborations.

### What students meet

In Years 7 to 10 the elaborations include training data issues such as the zero problem, bias in training data, machine learning that classifies images, and using AI tools responsibly.

A good out-of-school AI course should prepare children for these ideas in a gentler form, so they arrive at secondary school already understanding them.

### What we do not do

We never complete school assessment tasks, and we teach children to say when they have used AI help, as many schools now expect.

We also do not claim that an AI course raises school results. What it builds is understanding, and the habit of checking.

Progress

## Four stages of AI understanding for children

Most children move through the first three stages over a school year of weekly lessons.

| Stage | What your child can do |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Notice | Spot AI in everyday tools and describe what it is doing |
| 2. Explain | Say how a model learns from labelled examples, in their own words |
| 3. Test | Train a tiny model, test it on new examples and find where it fails |
| 4. Question | Ask what a model was trained on, what it never saw and who checks it |

### What age should my child start AI?

Children from about six can start noticing and sorting, without screens or with simple tools. Hands-on model building usually suits ages eight and up.

The free first class helps us pick the right starting point.

### Does my child need to code first?

Not for the first stages. To build real AI projects, some coding helps, which is why our AI courses lead into Scratch and Python.

Children who already code can go straight to building models.

## What Australian parents ask about AI courses for kids

### What should an AI course for kids teach?

How AI learns from examples, how and why it can be wrong, how training data affects fairness, and how to build and test small AI projects. Typing questions into a chatbot should be a small part at most.

### What is the zero problem in AI?

It is when a category has no examples in the training data, so the model cannot recognise it. A sorter trained on kangaroos and koalas but no wallabies will label every wallaby as a kangaroo or a koala.

### What age can children start learning about AI?

From about six with sorting and noticing activities, and from about eight with hands-on model building. Typed Python AI projects usually suit children from about nine.

### Are AI chatbots allowed for children?

It depends on the provider. Anthropic's consumer terms require users to be at least 18, and Google's help page says Gemini Apps with a Google AI plan requires users to be 13 or over in most countries. Always check current terms.

### Does my child need an AI account for your courses?

No. Our children's AI courses use age-appropriate tools and programs the children write themselves, with no adult chatbot account.

### Does the Australian Curriculum cover AI?

Yes. The Australian Curriculum v9 Digital Technologies elaborations include machine learning, training data issues such as the zero problem, bias and responsible use of AI tools.

### Will learning AI help my child at school?

It prepares them for the AI ideas in the Digital Technologies curriculum and builds the habit of checking information. We do not promise better marks.

### Does my child need to know how to code?

Not to start. Coding becomes useful for building real AI projects, so our courses lead naturally into Scratch and Python.

### How is this different from your Python AI course?

This page helps you choose an AI course. The Python AI course is one of the options, for children ready to type code and build models.

### What does it cost?

The first lesson is free and no card is needed. Monthly fees for groups and private lessons are set out in the fees section further up.
