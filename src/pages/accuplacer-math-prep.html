ACCUPLACER math prep

ACCUPLACER is the College Board placement system that many community colleges, universities and training providers use to decide where a new student starts in math. It has three multiple-choice math tests, each adaptive and usually untimed, and none of them is pass or fail. What the score changes is the first course on your timetable, and with it how many semesters stand between you and the class your program actually needs. This page sets out what College Board says about each test, how scores work, and how to prepare, with problems we wrote ourselves.

In short

ACCUPLACER is College Board's computer-adaptive placement test used by colleges and workforce training providers. Its math side has three tests: Arithmetic; Quantitative Reasoning, Algebra, and Statistics (QAS); and Advanced Algebra and Functions (AAF). Each is scored 200 to 300, and each college sets its own placement scores. The three math tests

What is on the ACCUPLACER math test?

There is no single ACCUPLACER math test. College Board publishes three, each with its own list of skills, and your college decides which one, or which sequence, you sit.

Quantitative Reasoning, Algebra, and Statistics (QAS)Rational numbers; ratio and proportional relationships; exponents; algebraic expressions; linear equations; linear applications and graphs; probability and sets; descriptive statistics; geometry conceptsPre-algebra to Algebra 1
Advanced Algebra and Functions (AAF)Linear equations; linear applications and graphs; factoring; quadratics; functions; radical and rational equations; polynomial equations; exponential and logarithmic equations; trigonometryAlgebra 1 to precalculus

Source: What's on the Tests, ACCUPLACER, read 31 October 2026. The "usual level" column is our reading of the skill lists, not College Board's wording. We are not connected with College Board or ACCUPLACER.

What Arithmetic really tests

College Board describes the Arithmetic test as focused on "computation, order of operations, estimation and rounding, comparing and ordering values in different formats, and recognizing equivalent values across formats." That last phrase matters: many questions ask whether a fraction, a decimal and a percent are the same number.

The test specifications manual adds that no-calculator questions "assess fluency in rational number arithmetic", which is why students who lean on a phone calculator in class often find this test harder than its content looks.

Where QAS and AAF overlap

Linear equations and linear applications and graphs appear on both the QAS and the AAF lists. The difference is what sits around them: QAS adds ratio, probability, statistics and basic geometry, while AAF adds factoring, quadratics, logarithms and trigonometry.

A student who is solid on lines and slope therefore has a head start on either test. A student who is shaky there will feel it on both.

Which test you sit

Which ACCUPLACER math test will I take?

Usually the one your planned major needs. College Board's test specifications describe who each test is built for, and colleges route students from there.

QAS for most non-STEM programs

The next-generation test specifications manual says the QAS test is "suited for students entering many non-STEM fields of study or for students who are undecided on a major." Think liberal arts, business support, many health certificates and general studies.

For those students the gateway course is often statistics or quantitative reasoning rather than college algebra, which is why QAS leans on proportional reasoning, probability and reading data.

AAF for STEM and math-heavy majors

The same manual says AAF is "suited for students entering STEM fields of study, as well as students entering non-STEM fields of study that require some advanced math (e.g., medicine, economics, accounting)."

If you are heading for engineering, computer science, nursing tracks that need college algebra, or any calculus sequence, expect AAF, and expect it to decide whether you start in college algebra, precalculus or calculus.

Colleges can also add their own material: College Board's educator pages note that "ACCUPLACER can house locally authored departmental exams." So two colleges that both say "ACCUPLACER" may not give exactly the same sequence. The honest first step is always to ask your college's testing center which math test, or which combination, you will see.

  1. Ask the testing center which math test you will sit. Arithmetic, QAS, AAF, or a sequence that moves you between them.
  2. Ask which course each score range places you into. The placement table belongs to the college, not to College Board.
  3. Ask whether you can retest, and when. Get the answer in writing before you test, so a disappointing first score has a plan behind it.
  4. Ask whether other evidence counts. Some colleges also weigh high school grades or other scores; College Board's educator pages describe how multiple factors improve placement decisions.
  5. Book the appointment. College Board notes that some schools allow walk-in testing, but tells students to contact the school to see if an appointment is necessary.

How the test runs

Is the ACCUPLACER math test timed, and can you use a calculator?

Usually it is not timed, and you cannot bring your own calculator. A calculator appears on screen only for questions that allow one.

How ACCUPLACER math is delivered (College Board student pages and the next-generation test specifications manual, read 31 October 2026)
FeatureWhat College Board says

Adaptive and untimed: what that means for you

Because each answer sets the difficulty of the next question, an early careless slip pulls the next question down a level. College Board's own advice is "to give each question as much thought as you can before selecting your answer."

Untimed is a gift if you use it. Check each answer by substitution or estimation before you click; there is no clock rewarding speed.

Calculator questions and no-calculator questions

The specifications manual explains that no-calculator questions check that "students who earn high scores ... do not lack the ability to perform calculations manually", while calculator questions check "strategic use of the tool."

So practise both ways: fraction and percent arithmetic by hand, and a graphing calculator for the questions that offer one, so the TI-84 style tool is familiar on the day.

Scores and placement

What is a good ACCUPLACER math score?

There is no national answer. Each math test is scored 200 to 300, and each college decides which score sends you to which course.

The scale and the seven bands

College Board says the Reading, Writing, Arithmetic, QAS and AAF tests "each have a score range of 200 to 300", broken into seven bands: 200 to 220, 221 to 230, 231 to 240, 241 to 250, 251 to 260, 261 to 270 and 271 to 300.

For each band, a College Readiness Skills Insight statement describes what a student scoring there is likely to know and be able to do. Reading the statement one band above your practice score is the clearest study list College Board offers.

Who sets the cut score

College Board's educator guidance is direct: "Each college, university, or career and workforce education and training provider should establish its own course placement scores." It adds that "ACCUPLACER doesn't recommend course placement scores or in any way mandate the scores that any school or state system should use."

So a score that places you into college algebra at one campus may place you a course lower at another. Check the placement table at your own college before you set a target.

Colleges usually set those scores in one of two ways College Board describes: matching the skills a course needs to the Skills Insight statements, or a "bookmark" standard setting in which faculty review test items ordered from easiest to hardest and mark the point a successful student should reach. Either way, faculty at your college, not College Board, draw the line.

  1. Find your college's placement table. It is often on the testing center or math department page; if not, ask.
  2. Take a free official practice test. College Board offers practice tests that need a free account, plus downloadable sample questions.
  3. Read the Skills Insight statement one band above your practice result. Those skills are your first study list.
  4. Study by skill, not by test. Fix the two weakest skills from the list, then retake a practice test.
  5. Bring the right ID. College Board lists acceptable IDs, including a driver's license, a passport and a middle or high school ID.

Worked examples

Three ACCUPLACER-style problems, worked by reasoning

These are our own problems, one at the level of each test. They are not College Board items and do not imitate any.

Worked example 1, Arithmetic level (ours): which is larger, 3/8 or 0.4, and by how many percentage points?
StepReasoning
1. Pick one formatTurn everything into decimals: comparing like with like avoids mistakes
2. Convert 3/83 / 8 = 0.375, because 1/8 = 0.125 and 3 x 0.125 = 0.375
3. Compare0.4 = 0.400, which is larger than 0.375
4. Percentage points0.400 = 40% and 0.375 = 37.5%, a gap of 2.5 percentage points
5. Sanity check3/8 is a little under 3/7.5 = 0.4, so 0.4 being larger makes sense
Worked example 2, QAS level (ours): 12 workers finish a job in 15 days. At the same rate, how long do 9 workers take?
StepReasoning
1. Spot the relationshipMore workers means fewer days: this is inverse proportion, not direct
2. Find the total work12 workers x 15 days = 180 worker-days
3. Share it out180 worker-days / 9 workers = 20 days
4. Sanity checkFewer workers should take longer, and 20 is more than 15
Worked example 3, AAF level (ours): solve 2^(x + 1) = 32
StepReasoning
1. Write both sides with the same base32 = 2^5, so 2^(x + 1) = 2^5
2. Match exponentsEqual powers of the same base have equal exponents: x + 1 = 5
3. Solvex = 4
4. Check2^(4 + 1) = 2^5 = 32
5. If the bases do not matchTake logs: x + 1 = log 32 / log 2, which still gives 5

The common thread is choosing a form before calculating. In example 1 the form is decimals; in example 2 it is total worker-days; in example 3 it is a shared base. ACCUPLACER rewards that habit because, with no clock, the student who chooses well and checks every answer gives the adaptive engine accurate signals from the first question.

ACCUPLACER math: common questions

What kind of math questions are on the ACCUPLACER?

It depends on the test your college gives. Arithmetic covers whole numbers, fractions, decimals, percent and equivalent values; QAS covers ratio, exponents, linear equations, probability, statistics and basic geometry; AAF covers factoring, quadratics, functions, exponential and logarithmic equations and trigonometry.

Is the ACCUPLACER math test hard?

It is adaptive, so it gets harder as you answer correctly and easier after mistakes. Most students find it fair if they know which test they are taking and have reviewed its skill list. Because it is usually untimed, careful checking matters more than speed.

How many questions are on the ACCUPLACER math test?

College Board's test specifications list a total of 20 questions for each math test, with a note that the values are not final. Your college may give more than one math test in a sequence.

Can you use a calculator on the ACCUPLACER math test?

You cannot bring your own calculator, unless an accommodation allows it. Some questions show an onscreen calculator, marked by an icon in the top-right corner; others are deliberately no-calculator.

What is a passing score on the ACCUPLACER math test?

There is no pass or fail; College Board says so directly. Each math test is scored 200 to 300, and each college sets its own placement scores, so check your college's table.

Is the ACCUPLACER timed?

College Board says there is no time limit on the tests and that in most instances they are untimed, with the test center confirming. Plan for a full sitting anyway.

How do I prepare for the ACCUPLACER math test?

Find out which test you are taking, take a free official practice test, then study the skills you missed and retake a practice test. Review fractions, percents and linear equations first, because they support every level.

Can high school students take the ACCUPLACER?

Yes, where a college uses it for dual enrollment. College Board's list of acceptable IDs includes a middle or high school ID. Ask the college about appointments and any voucher.

When are your ACCUPLACER classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

Are you connected with College Board or ACCUPLACER?

No. We are an independent online school. We describe the tests from College Board's published pages and use its free practice materials as they are.
