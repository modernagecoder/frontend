### Read the shape

One big step between 2018 and 2020, three years of standing still, a second step in 2022 to 2023, then a small slip. That is not a steady climb, and a straight trend line through it would hide the plateau.

### Read the gap

Code.org prints the national share as 60% for 2023 to 2024. Missouri&rsquo;s 56.3% sits a few points below it, so the state&rsquo;s position is close to the middle rather than at either end.

### Read the blank

There is no Missouri figure for 2024 to 2025 in this release. We leave the cell empty. Filling it with a guess, even a sensible one, would turn a published series into our own invention.

## Did Kansas City trim more trees each year? A trend project on city open data

Kansas City, Missouri publishes a small public-domain table of trees its crews trimmed and removed, quarter by quarter. Sixteen rows are enough to teach the three habits every trend analysis needs.

**Trees trimmed and removed per fiscal year (our sums of the four quarterly rows, from the city&rsquo;s file downloaded 18 October 2026)**

| Fiscal year label | Trimmed (our sum) | Removed (our sum) |
|---|---|---|
| FY 2012-2013 | 2,853 | 1,741 |
| FY 2013-2014 | 5,834 | 3,634 |
| FY 2014-2015 | 5,766 | 2,257 |
| FY 2015-2016 | 7,222 | 2,771 |

### 1. Put time in order

The file does not list quarters in sequence: FY 2012-2013 Q4 sits fifteenth of sixteen lines, and Q3 of 2014-2015 comes before Q1. Students parse the label into a year and a quarter and sort on both before drawing anything.

### 2. Aggregate by year

Summing four quarters per fiscal year gives the table above. Trimming rises from 2,853 to 7,222 across the four years by our arithmetic, while removals peak in FY 2013-2014 and fall back.

### 3. Ask what is missing

The rows stop in 2016, and a second column headed Fiscal Year is empty in every row. A trend that ends nine years ago says nothing about today, and a careful analyst writes that sentence before any conclusion.

Students then compare a single quarter with its year. The busiest quarter for trimming was FY 2015-2016 Q4, with 2,321 trees; the busiest for removals was FY 2013-2014 Q1, with 1,710. Plotting quarters instead of years shows how much the yearly totals smooth away, which is exactly the judgment they will need when they read a state access series like the one higher up this page.

### Whose numbers these are

The quarterly counts are the city&rsquo;s, published under a public domain licence. The yearly sums and every comparison are ours, made for teaching. We do not know how the city defines its fiscal year, so the page keeps the city&rsquo;s labels instead of converting them to calendar years.

## Central Time from St. Louis to Joplin

The IANA time zone database lists Missouri under US central time, the zone it calls America/Chicago.

### Summer offset

During Central Daylight Time, India is 10 hours 30 minutes ahead of Springfield and Columbia. Our teachers absorb that gap; families simply pick a Central Time slot.

### Winter offset

Under Central Standard Time the gap is 11 hours 30 minutes. Bookings are always written in Central Time so no one has to convert anything.

### This year&rsquo;s changes

Missouri moves back an hour on November 1, 2026 and forward an hour on March 14, 2027. A class time confirmed in Central Time stays put for the family on both dates.

**The tree file and the clock teach one idea** Dates are data. A quarter label that sorts wrongly and a reminder that drifts by an hour in November are the same kind of bug: treating time as plain text or a fixed number instead of using a proper date or zone such as America/Chicago.

## An AI tool will draw the trend line. Will it notice the rows are out of order?

Often it will not, and that is the case for learning to code yourself.

Hand the Kansas City file to a chatbot and ask for a chart, and you may get a neat line that zigzags because the quarters were plotted in file order. Nothing in the output warns you. A student who has sorted dates by hand sees the problem at once.

The same applies to the state figures above: a model asked for Missouri&rsquo;s 2024 to 2025 access rate might produce a confident number where the published data has none. Knowing what a dataset contains, and saying so, is a skill only practice builds.

Missouri students who learn to check a trend before trusting it will use AI tools well; the ones who skip that step will be used by them. The longer argument is in [the case for coding skills when AI drafts the code](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
