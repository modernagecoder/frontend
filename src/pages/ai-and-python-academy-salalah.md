## The number that shapes everything in Dhofar

Dhofar is not a suburb of anything. Between Salalah and Muscat lies most of a country, and the practical consequence is that anything advertised as national tends in practice to happen in the capital.

When the Ministry of Education inaugurated the National Olympiad for Scientific Innovations, Robotics and Artificial Intelligence for 2025/26 on 1 December 2025, it did so at the Oman Convention and Exhibition Centre in Muscat, with more than 250 students and 108 supervising teachers and specialists drawn from directorates across the governorates, entering 70 projects across four fields.

A Dhofari student on that stage has therefore travelled to be there. That is not a complaint about the Olympiad, which has to be somewhere. It is a description of an extra cost that a family in the far south quietly carries.

**Here is the distinction we want to be scrupulous about: teaching this subject online removes the distance entirely, and competing in it does not.** A student in Salalah can be taught by the same person, in the same batch, at the same standard as a student anywhere on earth, for the price of a connection. If they then want to stand on a national stage, someone still has to book a flight.

What we can do about the second half is make the travelling worth it. A student who arrives with a project that runs, that states its own error rate and that is readable on GitHub is in a different category from one who arrives with a demonstration.

One more thing needs saying, because an argument built on distance can slide into an insult. Dhofar is not short of institutions or of capable students. It has universities and colleges of its own and schools that send students to national competitions. The specific shortage this page addresses is narrower: the absence of a weekly, sequenced route from Python through machine learning to agents, taught by the same person across a year, at a level above introductory robotics.

## The five rungs, and the one where distance stops mattering

| Rung | Roughly | What the student actually does |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Python by hand | Age 9 to 12 | Real syntax rather than blocks: loops, functions, files, errors read and fixed |
| 2. Data | Age 11 to 14 | A messy file loaded, cleaned, plotted, and the poisonous row found |
| 3. Machine learning | Age 12 to 16 | A model trained, its error stated in a real unit, compared against a baseline |
| 4. Agents | Age 13 to 18 | A program given a goal that picks its steps, calls tools, and fails loudly |
| 5. GitHub and the write-up | Any | The code published with an honest account of what it does and where it breaks |

**Rung five is the one that travels.** A public repository does not care where it was written. An admissions officer, a competition judge or an employer in Muscat opens the same link a teacher in Salalah opened, sees the same commits, and reads the same honest note about what the project cannot do. That is a genuinely different asset from a certificate, and it is the one thing a student a thousand kilometres from the capital accumulates at exactly the same rate as anybody else.

## How the classes run

Oman holds Gulf Standard Time all year and India, where the teaching operation sits, holds Indian Standard Time all year. The difference is a permanent **thirty minutes**, so a slot agreed once never has to be renegotiated because a season changed.

| Slot, Oman time | Who it is for |
|---|---|
| 16:30 to 18:00 | Primary age children, straight after school |
| 18:30 to 20:00 | The main teenage block, after dinner and school work |
| 20:30 to 22:00 | University students and working adults |

Ninety minutes per session, twice a week, screens shared both ways, five to eight students or one to one, in English. Work is committed to GitHub during the term rather than handed in at the end of it.
