# Reception maths online: a parent's guide to the early learning goals

Search for Reception maths and you meet a wall of worksheets, apps and printable packs. The official picture is much smaller and kinder. In England, a Reception child's maths is described by two early learning goals, Number and Numerical Patterns, six short sentences in all, and the class teacher judges them from what the child does in everyday play, with no test to sit and no written evidence needed. This page explains those six sentences in plain words, the two checks that happen in Reception, what the government's 2026 screen advice says about under-fives, and maths play that fits into a normal day. It also says honestly when a live online class helps a four or five year old, and when it does not.

## What maths should a child know by the end of Reception?

Two early learning goals cover maths, with three sentences each. They describe a child "at the expected level of development" at the end of the EYFS, usually the end of Reception.

The framework that applies from September 2026 opens its maths section with one clear idea: "Developing a strong grounding in number is essential so that all children develop the necessary building blocks to excel mathematically." It asks settings to give children frequent, varied chances to count and to handle quantities, naming the equipment in a phrase every parent can copy at home: "using manipulatives, including small pebbles and tens frames for organising counting". The goals themselves are short, and they are worth reading in full.

**The EYFS mathematics early learning goals, word for word (DfE statutory framework, September 2026)**

| Goal | Children at the expected level of development will | What it looks like at home (our gloss) |
|---|---|---|
| Number | Have a deep understanding of numbers to 10, including the composition of each number. | Knows that 7 can be 5 and 2, or 4 and 3, or 6 and 1, and can show it with objects |
| Number | Subitise (recognise quantities without counting) up to 5. | Glances at four buttons and says "four" without pointing at each one |
| Number | Automatically recall (without reference to rhymes, counting or other aids) number bonds up to 5 (including subtraction facts) and some number bonds to 10, including double facts. | Says straight away that 3 and 2 make 5, and that 5 take away 1 leaves 4 |
| Numerical Patterns | Verbally count beyond 20, recognising the pattern of the counting system. | Counts on past twenty: twenty-one, twenty-two, and notices it repeats the ones |
| Numerical Patterns | Compare quantities up to 10 in different contexts, recognising when one quantity is greater than, less than or the same as the other quantity. | Tells you which plate has more grapes, even when the smaller pile is spread out |
| Numerical Patterns | Explore and represent patterns within numbers up to 10, including evens and odds, double facts and how quantities can be distributed equally. | Finds that 6 socks pair up and 7 leave one over, and shares 8 sweets fairly between two |

Three things in the framework change how a parent should read that table. First, the goals are not a syllabus: "The ELGs should not be used as a curriculum or in any way to limit the wide variety of rich experiences that are crucial to child development." Second, the judgement is a whole picture of the child, what the framework calls a "holistic, best-fit judgement". Third, there is no pile of evidence to build: "Recorded, written or photographic evidence is not required." The teacher draws on knowing the child.

### Maths is one area of seven

The framework sets out "seven areas of learning and development", and mathematics is one of four specific areas, alongside literacy, understanding the world, and expressive arts and design.

The three prime areas, communication and language, physical development, and personal, social and emotional development, come first. A child who can listen, take turns and hold a pencil is better placed for every maths goal.

### Attitude is written into it

The maths programme asks that children look for patterns, spot connections, "'have a go', talk to adults and peers about what they notice and not be afraid to make mistakes".

That last phrase is the one to keep in mind at home. A four year old who happily guesses and checks is doing exactly what the framework hopes.

## Is there a maths test in Reception?

Not one that a child can pass or fail. There are two separate checks in the Reception year, and they do quite different jobs.

Early in the year comes the Reception Baseline Assessment. The framework describes it as "a short assessment, taken within the first six weeks of a child starting reception", covering early mathematics, literacy, communication and language. Its purpose is narrow: the results are compared with the same children's Key Stage 2 results seven years later, to measure a school's progress. The framework is blunt about what it is not: "The data, including numerical scores, is not shared with external bodies, including schools, teachers, pupils or parents/carers." Teachers receive short narrative statements instead, and "There is no expected standard and children cannot pass or fail."

**The two Reception checks side by side (EYFS statutory framework, September 2026, section 2 and Annex B)**

|  | Reception Baseline Assessment | EYFS Profile |
|---|---|---|
| When | Within the first six weeks of starting Reception | In the final term, no later than 30 June |
| How long | Not timed, designed to take around 20 minutes | Built up over the year from everyday observation |
| Who does it | A trained adult the child knows | Usually the Reception class teacher |
| What it covers | Early maths, literacy, communication and language | All 17 early learning goals, including the two for maths |
| What families get | Nothing: scores are not shared with parents | The results, with a chance to discuss them |
| What it is for | A starting point for primary progress measures | A picture of the child and their readiness for Year 1 |

The EYFS Profile is the one that matters to a family. For each goal the teacher notes whether the child is meeting the expected level or is "not yet reaching expected levels", which the framework calls "emerging". Providers "must share the results of the Profile with parents and/or carers", and the framework adds that "Year 1 teachers must be given a copy of the Profile report", so the next teacher starts September already knowing the child. An emerging judgement in maths is information, not a label: it tells the Year 1 teacher where to begin.

### Good questions for the teacher

Which of the six maths statements does my child show easily, and which are still emerging? What does that look like in class?

What do you use in class for counting and bonds, such as ten-frames or pebbles, so that we can use the same at home?

### On starting school

A child reaches compulsory school age on whichever of 31 December, 31 March or 31 August comes first after their fifth birthday, as the framework's footnote notes. Most children start Reception before that date.

In exceptional cases, and only with the parents' agreement, a child can stay in EYFS provision longer; the Profile is then completed once, before Year 1.

Source: DfE EYFS statutory framework, paragraphs 2.11 to 2.21 and Annex B, statutory guidance for the Reception Baseline Assessment, read on 31 October 2026. The count of 17 goals is ours, made by listing each goal heading in the framework.

## Is online maths suitable for a four or five year old?

Sometimes, in small doses, with a parent beside them. The government-commissioned 2026 advice on under-fives is the right yardstick, and we would rather say so than sell more screen time.

In March 2026 the Early Years Screen Time Advisory Group published an independent report for the government. Its suggested message to parents of two to five year olds is to keep screen time to no more than an hour a day, "ideally through short chunks (30 minutes or less) and not during mealtimes or near to bedtime. Keep solo screen use to a minimum." The same report found that an adult watching and talking alongside a child, which it calls co-viewing, "improves language and thinking skills". It also says plainly that it did not set out to advise early years educational settings.

**How we apply that advice to a live maths class for a Reception child (our policy)**

| The advice says | So in our early maths sessions |
|---|---|
| Short chunks, 30 minutes or less | A Reception session is short, and the teacher ends it early if attention has gone |
| Keep solo screen use to a minimum | We ask for a parent or older sibling in the room for the whole session |
| Interaction helps; passive watching does not | The child talks, counts aloud and moves real objects; there are no videos to sit through |
| Balance with play away from screens | Each session ends with one off-screen game for the days in between |
| Not near bedtime | We suggest an after-school or weekend morning slot, never late evening |

## What Reception maths can we do at home instead of worksheets?

One small game for each of the six goal sentences. All are our own, need nothing you have to buy, and take five minutes.

**Six games of ours, one per early learning goal statement**

| Game (ours) | You need | How to play | Goal statement it serves |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hide and Seek Five | Five pebbles and a cup | Hide some pebbles under the cup. The child sees what is left and says how many are hiding. | Composition of numbers, and bonds to 5 |
| Flash Plate | A paper plate and dried pasta | Show a plate with 1 to 5 pieces for two seconds, then cover it. "How many did you see?" | Subitise up to 5 |
| Ten-Frame Breakfast | An egg box with two rows of five | Fill it with grapes or blueberries. How many spaces are empty? What makes 10? | Deep understanding of numbers to 10 |
| Stair Count | A staircase or a long hallway | Count each step aloud, then keep counting past 20 in the hallway: twenty-one, twenty-two. | Count beyond 20 |
| More or Fewer? | Two handfuls of buttons | Spread one pile out wide and bunch the other. Which has more? Line them up to check. | Compare quantities up to 10 |
| Sock Pairs | The laundry basket | Pair the socks. Did every sock get a partner? That number is even. One left over means odd. | Evens, odds, doubles and fair sharing |

Worksheets are not wrong, but most of the six statements are things a child does or says, not things they write. Subitising is a glance; comparing a spread-out pile with a bunched one is a judgement about real objects; recall "without reference to rhymes, counting or other aids" is something you hear in a quick answer. A sheet of drawn dots can only show some of that. If your child enjoys colouring sums, keep it. If they resist, the games above cover the same ground.

### Words worth using every day

More, fewer, the same, altogether, left over, double, half, first, next, and how many. The framework's maths programme stresses vocabulary as well as number.

Ask "How do you know?" and wait. A four year old explaining that three and three is six because "they match" is reasoning, not just recall.

### Signs to mention to the teacher

Still counting each object one by one for two or three items late in the spring term, or unsure which of two small groups is bigger.

Not yet counting past ten in the summer term. Teachers see a wide range at this age, and a short conversation usually helps more than extra practice.

## Four Reception maths moments, step by step

Our own examples, written as a parent and child might meet them. The child talks and moves objects; nobody writes anything down.

### 1. Six in three different ways (composition)

**Set-up (ours).** Six toy cars and two "garages", a pair of shoeboxes.

**Play.** Park all six in one garage: six and nothing. Move one across: five and one. Keep moving one at a time: four and two, three and three.

**Talk.** "Did the number of cars change?" The child sees that six stays six however it is split.

**Why it matters.** This is the framework's "composition of each number". It is the root of every later bond.

### 2. Seeing five without counting (subitising)

**Set-up (ours).** Two dice, one showing the five pattern and one with five pasta pieces scattered.

**Ask.** "Which is quicker to see?" Most children say the dice pattern straight away.

**Connect.** The dice five is four corners and one in the middle: four and one more.

**Why it matters.** Recognising small amounts at a glance frees attention for bigger questions later.

### 3. Double four on two hands (doubles)

**Question (ours).** "Show me four fingers on this hand, and four on that one. How many?"

**First time.** The child counts all eight fingers. That is fine.

**Later.** Repeated over a few weeks with real objects, double four becomes a known fact: eight, said at once.

**Why it matters.** The goal asks for doubles recalled without counting or aids. Practice with objects gets a child there; drilling the words alone does not.

### 4. Sharing nine strawberries (fair sharing)

**Question (ours).** "Share these nine strawberries fairly between you and me."

**Play.** One for you, one for me, until one is left.

**Talk.** "Is it fair? What could we do with the last one?" Children often suggest cutting it, which is a lovely early idea of a half.

**Why it matters.** It meets "how quantities can be distributed equally", and nine leaving one over is the odd number pattern.

**Practice set for a parent to try (ours, with answers).** (1) Put 5 pebbles on the table and hide 2 in your hand; how many is the child looking at? (2) Show 4 buttons for two seconds; how many? (3) "What do I need with 3 to make 5?" (4) Count aloud from 17; what comes after 20? (5) Which is more, 7 spread out or 8 bunched up? (6) Can 10 apples be shared fairly by two people? **Answers:** (1) 3; (2) 4; (3) 2; (4) 21; (5) 8, which the child can check by lining them up; (6) yes, 5 each, so 10 is even.

## How does an early maths class work for a Reception child?

Short, spoken and hands-on, with a grown-up in the room. The teacher leads the play; the parent learns the games to use all week.

1. **A free first class, kept short.** The teacher plays a few counting and hiding games to see where the child is with the six goal statements. Nothing is written down and nothing needs preparing.
2. **A small tray beside the screen.** Ten pebbles or buttons, an egg box cut to two rows of five, and a pair of dice. The child moves these while the teacher does the same on camera.
3. **One idea per session.** A whole session might be the many ways of making 6, or comparing two piles. Young children learn most when one idea is met in several ways.
4. **A game to keep.** Each session ends with one off-screen game for the days in between, so most of the maths happens away from the screen.
5. **A word with the parent.** A short note on what the child can now do, which goal statement it connects to, and what to try next.

### 1-on-1 or a group at this age?

For a four or five year old we usually suggest 1-on-1, so the teacher can follow the child's pace and stop when attention fades.

A group of 15 to 20 suits an older or very sociable child, often in the summer of Reception or in Year 1, where the energy of other children helps.

### What we will not do

Give a Reception child test papers or timed drills. Neither of the Reception checks is a test to prepare for.

## Four stepping stones across Reception

Roughly how the six goal statements build over the year. Children move at very different speeds at four and five.

**Early maths stepping stones in Reception (our sequence, built on the EYFS goals)**

| Stone | Often around | What a parent might notice |
|---|---|---|
| 1. One number for each thing | Autumn term | Touches each object once while counting, and knows the last number said is how many |
| 2. Small amounts at a glance | Late autumn to spring | Says "three" for three items without counting, up to five |
| 3. Inside the numbers to 10 | Spring term | Shows a number as two parts in more than one way, and fills a ten-frame |
| 4. Quick bonds, and past 20 | Summer term | Recalls bonds to 5 and doubles, compares groups, and counts on beyond 20 |

Matching one count to one object sounds simple and is a real achievement. Rushing past it causes trouble later.

## What parents of Reception children ask about maths

### What maths should my child know by the end of Reception?

Children at the expected level understand numbers to 10 and how each is made of smaller parts, see up to 5 at a glance, recall bonds to 5 and some to 10 including doubles, count beyond 20, compare groups up to 10, and explore odds, evens and fair sharing.

### What are the early learning goals for maths?

There are two: Number, and Numerical Patterns. Each has three statements in the EYFS statutory framework, and they describe the expected level of development at the end of the EYFS.

### Is there a maths test in Reception?

No test that a child can pass or fail. The Reception Baseline Assessment early in the year has no expected standard and its scores are not shared with parents, and the EYFS Profile at the end of the year is the teacher's judgement from everyday observation.

### When will I hear how my child is doing?

The EYFS Profile must be completed no later than 30 June, and schools must share the results with parents and explain how to discuss them. Year 1 teachers also receive a copy.

### What does emerging mean on the Profile?

It means the child is not yet at the expected level for that goal. It helps the Year 1 teacher know where to start, and it is common for some goals at this age.

### Are Reception maths worksheets a good idea?

They are fine if your child enjoys them, but most of the maths goals are things children do and say, such as seeing small numbers at a glance or comparing real piles. Short games with objects cover more of them.

### How much screen time is right for a four year old?

The independent report published for the government in March 2026 suggests no more than an hour a day for two to five year olds, ideally in chunks of 30 minutes or less, with little solo use. Our sessions are short and need an adult in the room.

### Is online maths tuition suitable for a Reception child?

For some children, in small doses, with a parent alongside. Our Early Maths Foundations course is listed for ages 4 to 7, and we will tell you honestly in the free session if waiting until Year 1 would suit your child better.
