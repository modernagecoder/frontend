Families who educate children at home, for a year, for a move, or for longer, usually find maths and coding the hardest subjects to run well alone. Both reward a teacher who can see a child's working and ask the right question at the right moment. This page explains what Peru's General Education Law says, what we could not find, and how live classes can carry the coding and maths part of a home week.

The legal text

Is homeschooling allowed in Peru? What the law says

We can only report what we read, and we are not lawyers. The General Education Law, Ley 28044 of 17 July 2003, published by MINEDU on gob.pe, is the starting point. It does not use the word "homeschooling". These are the articles a family will meet first.

Four articles of Ley 28044, Ley General de Educación, and what they say
ArticleWhat it says, in our EnglishKey words in the Spanish text
Art. 4Education is a public service, free at every level and modality when the State provides it"cuando lo provee el Estado es gratuita"
Art. 12Education is compulsory for inicial, primaria and secundaria; parents must ensure timely enrolment and continued attendance in educational centres and programmes"asegurar la matrícula oportuna de los estudiantes y su permanencia en los centros y programas educativos"
Art. 27Distance education is a modality of the system, using technology, applicable to all stages under the relevant rules, to complement, reinforce or replace face-to-face education"complementar, reforzar o reemplazar la educación presencial"
Art. 54The family is responsible in the first place for the integral education of the children; parents must educate their children and ensure they finish their education"responsable en primer lugar de la educación integral de los hijos"

Source: Ley N.° 28044, Ley General de Educación, in the copy MINEDU publishes on gob.pe (cdn.www.gob.pe), read on 8 and 9 November 2026. The English summaries are ours. This is not legal advice.

What we could not verify

We searched gob.pe on 9 November 2026 for "homeschooling" and "educación en casa" and found no MINEDU norm, procedure or guidance on home education. That does not prove none exists; it means we cannot point you to one.

So we do not say home education is permitted, forbidden or regulated in any particular way. Article 12 speaks of enrolment in "centros y programas educativos", and Article 27 recognises distance education "de acuerdo con la normatividad en la materia", meaning under rules we have not reviewed.

What a family can do

Ask the local UGEL or MINEDU directly, and take advice from a Peruvian lawyer before deciding how a child's schooling is registered. Families using a foreign online school should also ask that school how its studies are recognised.

What we can say with confidence is narrower: our classes are not a school, are not a distance-education programme under Article 27, and award no Peruvian grade or certificate. They teach two subjects, live and in English, alongside whatever schooling the family has arranged.

What home learners miss

What does a child learning at home miss in coding and maths?

In coding, less than you might expect, because Peruvian schools teach no computing subject. In maths, a teacher who sees the working. And in competitions, the school that enters you.

Coding: little

The CNEB, which MINEDU applies to every modality of basic education, has no computing or programming area; digital skills sit in a competency shared across subjects. A child at school would mostly need outside classes for coding too.

Maths: the second pair of eyes

A parent can explain a method; it is much harder to spot, from finished answers, where a child's reasoning went wrong. That diagnosis is what a maths teacher adds.

Competitions: the entry route

ONEM is entered through the school stage. The OPI 2025 rules required contestants to be enrolled in a Peruvian school in 2025 or to have finished in 2024. Check each organiser's current rules.

There is also the social side. In a group class a home learner works with fifteen to twenty children of a similar age, answers questions in front of others and sees how their peers think. Families who want that choose a group; families who want a pace set entirely by their child choose one to one. A weekly plan

How can a homeschool family plan coding and maths each week?

With two fixed live sessions and a little structured practice around them. Here is a plan we often suggest; it is ours, and families change it freely.

  1. Two live classes a week. One coding, one maths, or two of one subject, at daytime hours that suit a home routine. A Lima morning is an Indian evening, so daytime classes are easy to arrange.
  2. Twenty minutes of practice on two other days. Short problems set by the mentor and checked at the next class, so the parent is not the marker.
  3. Rotate the four maths competencies. The CNEB names four: quantity; regularity, equivalence and change; movement, form and location; data and uncertainty. A home plan drifts towards whichever one the parent is most comfortable with; the program below catches that.
  4. One project a month. A game, a chart from real data, a geometry drawing in Python. Something the child can show the family.
  5. A monthly note from the mentor. What the child can now do, and what comes next. Families who keep records for any purpose can file it.

If the family follows the Peruvian school calendar, the 2026 year ran from 16 March to 18 December 2026 for regular basic education under RM 501-2025-MINEDU, while the same norm gave alternative basic education (EBA) two periods, from 13 March to 26 July and from 10 August to 20 December. Home learners can follow either rhythm, or none: our classes run all year. For children around ten, our Spanish page programación para niños de 10 años suggests where to start.

A real lesson

How can I check that a home maths plan covers everything?

Write down which competencies each week touched and let Python compare them with the full list. It is a lesson we give children of about 11 and over, and parents find the tool useful in its own right.

The plan

A family logs four weeks of home maths. Week 1 was all number work. Week 2 added patterns. Week 3 was patterns only. Week 4 mixed number work with a little data. It felt balanced. The plan is invented for the lesson.

Each week becomes a Python set of competency labels, and the four CNEB competencies become one more set.

What sets answer

Union answers "what did we touch at all?". Difference answers "what did we never touch?". Intersection answers "what did we touch every single week?".

The program shows that geometry, the "movement, form and location" competency, never appeared, that nothing appeared every week, and that data appeared only once.

Sets fit this job because they ignore order and duplicates: a competency touched three times is still one member of covered. The first line uses set().union(*weeks.values()), where the star unpacks every week's set into one call. The last line counts, for each competency, how many weeks contain it, using the fact that True counts as 1 in a sum.

  1. Fix the plan. Change week 3 so the program reports nothing missing. What is the smallest change?
  2. Add coding. Make a second set of coding skills (loops, conditions, functions, lists) and track those too.
  3. Find the favourites. Print the competencies that appeared in at least three weeks.
  4. Explain it. In two English sentences, tell a parent what COMPETENCIES - covered means.

The teaching language

Can a home-educated child in Peru learn coding and maths in English?

Yes, and many home-educating families choose English on purpose. Programming is written in English keywords, and maths is mostly symbols; the English needed for both is small, repeated and learnt quickly.

How classes handle it

Our mentors, based in India, speak clearly and slowly, type every new word in the chat and keep the work on screen. A child can answer with a number, a line of code or a few words.

On EF's 2025 index of adult English, Peru as a whole sits at 519 (moderate) while the capital reaches 555 (high). Children tend to settle faster than adults, and one free lesson usually tells a parent how their child copes.

At home

What does a home learner need for live classes?

A computer, a connection and a quiet corner. In Peru the computer is the hard part: INEI found that 36.4% of households had at least one in the second quarter of 2026.

A computer

A laptop or desktop for coding from about age 9; a tablet will do for block coding and for maths with younger children.

A routine

The same two slots every week. Home learners who keep fixed class times progress more steadily than those who move them often.

No adult AI accounts

Vendor terms set the floors (18 for Claude; parental permission under 18 for ChatGPT), so our children's classes simply leave them out.

Families in the capital interested in English-medium learning more broadly can read our page on English-medium coding classes in Lima. Whatever the family's setting, the promise is the same: the same quality of teaching anywhere in Peru, and children who learn to think through a problem rather than copy an answer.

Questions from home-learning families

Homeschool coding and maths in Peru: questions and answers

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. We teach both subjects only in English, live and online. A home learner whose English is still developing can lean on the screen: the mentor types new words, and the code or working stays visible throughout.

Is homeschooling legal in Peru?

We cannot answer that and are not lawyers. Ley 28044 makes education compulsory and asks parents to ensure enrolment in educational centres and programmes; it also recognises distance education under its own rules. We found no MINEDU norm on home education on gob.pe. Ask your UGEL or a lawyer.

Can your classes count as my child's schooling?

No. We are not a school or a distance-education programme, and we award no Peruvian grades, certificates or school records. We teach coding and maths alongside whatever schooling the family has arranged.

Do you follow the Peruvian curriculum in maths?

We plan maths around the CNEB's four competencies, so a family following Peruvian school can see the link. We also teach alongside foreign curricula such as Cambridge or the IB. The school or programme the child is registered with assesses them, not us.

Can a homeschooled child enter ONEM or the OPI?

Both work through schools. ONEM starts with a school stage, and the OPI 2025 rules required enrolment in a Peruvian school in 2025 or graduation in 2024. Check each organiser's current rules; we have no connection with them.

What time can classes be?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India is ten and a half hours ahead of Peru all year, so a 10:00 class in Lima is 20:30 in India, an easy hour for mentors.

Should we choose a group or one to one?

A group of fifteen to twenty gives a home learner peers and the habit of thinking aloud in front of others. One to one lets the child set the pace entirely. Many home-learning families start in a group and add one to one for the harder subject.

Is there a certificate?

Children who pass a course's final exam receive our own completion certificate. For a home learner it is a useful record of a finished course, but it is not a school document and carries no Peruvian status.

Will my child use ChatGPT?

No. Our classes for under-18s never use adult AI accounts: Claude requires users to be 18, and ChatGPT needs a parent's permission under 18. The thinking stays with the child.

How do we pay?

By card, monthly, in US dollars; your bank converts the charge into soles. The first class is free and needs no card.
