Coding and AI classes in Victoria

What makes a coding and AI class the best fit for a Victorian learner?

One that knows the Victorian calendar and the VCE, and teaches numbers properly as well as code. Victoria counted 6,503,491 residents at the 2021 Census. We teach learners aged 6 to 67 live online, from Prep-age Scratch to Python, data, AI and VCE computing technique, in a small level-matched group or one to one, and the opening lesson is free.

Add up the eighteen age-group percentages the Bureau of Statistics publishes for Australia in 2021 and you do not get 100. You get 99.9. Victoria's own column for the same year happens to reach exactly 100.0, but its 2016 column comes to 100.1. Nobody has made a mistake: each share was rounded on its own, and rounding errors do not cancel neatly. Round those shares again to whole numbers and the choice of rule starts to matter, because the Australian figure for people in their early forties sits at exactly 6.5. Victorian learners write the code, test two rounding rules and find out why accountants, statisticians and Python itself disagree with what most of us were taught at school.

Signature project

Why do published percentages add up to 99.9?

The 18 age-group shares from the 2021 and 2016 QuickStats, added and then rounded again under two rules. Our calculation, done with exact decimal arithmetic.

Adding the published age-group percentages (our calculation, 7 October 2026)
ColumnSum as publishedExact ties at .5Whole numbers, half upWhole numbers, half to even
Victoria, 2021100.0One: 35 to 39 years at 7.5100100
Victoria, 2016100.1None100100
Australia, 202199.9One: 40 to 44 years at 6.59998

1. Rounding once

The Bureau rounds every share to one decimal place on its own. Small errors in each of 18 rows can push the total a tenth away from 100 in either direction, which is exactly what the 2016 and Australian columns show.

2. Half up

The rule most of us learned says a 5 always goes up. Under it, the Australian 6.5 becomes 7, and the 18 whole numbers add up to 99.

3. Half to even

The rule used in banking and statistics sends an exact half to the nearest even number. The Victorian 7.5 still becomes 8, but the Australian 6.5 becomes 6 and the total drops to 98.

Python already has an opinion

Ask Python's built-in round() for round(6.5) and it answers 6; round(7.5) gives 8. It rounds half to even, and on these 18 Australian shares it also produces a total of 98. Learners who expected 7 discover why the documentation spends a paragraph on it, and why money code uses the decimal module and says which rule it applies.

None of these sums is a figure anyone should publish: they exist only to show what rounding does. The real totals are the Bureau's own counts, shown in the Census table above.

Five rounding habits worth keeping for life

Learned on Census shares, then used on marks, budgets, survey results and anything a dashboard shows as a percentage.

How to stop rounding from misleading a reader
HabitIn practiceWhat it prevents
Round lastKeep full precision until the final display stepErrors that pile up through a calculation
Name the ruleWrite "half up" or "half to even" in the code and the reportTwo systems quietly disagreeing
Use exact decimals for moneyPython decimal, not floating point, for anything pricedCents that drift with binary fractions
Expect 99.9 and 100.1Add a note when rounded parts are shown with their totalReaders assuming a figure is wrong
Never rebuild a total from partsQuote the published total itselfTotals that differ from the official one

The fourth habit is the one Victorians meet most often without noticing: a school newsletter, a sports club report or a council survey with percentages that refuse to make 100. Once learners have built it themselves, they stop distrusting the table and start reading its footnotes.

Coding and AI

An AI tool can summarise a table in seconds. Why should a Victorian student still check its arithmetic?

Because a summary that says the shares "add up to 100" may be describing what it expected, not what the table says.

Give an assistant the Australian age table and ask for the total and it may reply with a confident 100, because that is what percentages are supposed to do. Ask it to round the figures and it may use half up in one sentence and half to even in the code it writes, without saying so. Nothing crashes. The report simply carries a quiet inconsistency into a meeting, a newsletter or an assignment.

A student who has added the published shares and watched 99.9 appear knows the questions to ask: which rounding rule, rounded at which step, and is this a published total or one rebuilt from parts? Those questions carry over to every generated answer that contains a number, and they are exactly the questions VCE data and computing studies expect students to ask of their own work.

So the case for a Victorian student learning to code in 2026 is not about typing faster than an AI tool. It is about being the one who notices that the numbers do not add up, and knowing why. The longer argument is in why teenagers should still learn to code in 2026.

Victorian coding class questions

What Victorian families ask us first.

How many people live in Victoria?

The 2021 Census counted 6,503,491 people in Victoria, with a median age of 38, the same as the national median.

What is round half to even?

Round half to even, sometimes called banker's rounding, is a rule that sends a value exactly halfway between two whole numbers to the even one, so 6.5 becomes 6 and 7.5 becomes 8. It avoids the upward drift that always rounding halves up creates over many values.

What is the Victorian rounding project?

Learners add the 18 published age-group percentages from the 2021 and 2016 Census, find totals of 100.0, 100.1 and 99.9, then round to whole numbers under two rules. For Australia in 2021, half up gives a total of 99 and half to even gives 98.

When are the 2027 Victorian school terms?

Term 1 runs from Wednesday 27 January to Thursday 25 March, with government school students starting on Thursday 28 January. Term 2 is Monday 12 April to Friday 25 June, Term 3 Monday 12 July to Friday 17 September and Term 4 Monday 4 October to Friday 17 December.

Do you help with VCE computing and maths?

Yes, as teaching of technique and understanding. We have separate pages for VCE Algorithmics, Applied Computing and the maths studies. School-assessed coursework is the student's own work under VCAA rules, and we never write it.

Do you prepare students for the Victorian selective entry exam?

We teach the maths and reasoning skills the exam draws on as part of ordinary lessons, and our selective entry maths page explains the format. We give no admissions advice and make no claims about offers.
