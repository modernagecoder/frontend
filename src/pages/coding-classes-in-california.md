## Standards without a mandate

The state containing Silicon Valley does not require its schools to teach computer science.

On September 6, 2018 the State Board of Education adopted California's first K-12 computer science standards, developed by a multidisciplinary team drawing on the **K-12 Computer Science Framework**, the Computer Science Teachers Association, NSF-funded work, and industry input. They are good standards.

They are also **voluntary**. The stated intent is to give substantive guidance while allowing flexibility across local education agencies, each deciding how best to incorporate computer science based on local capacity and context. In plain terms: your district decides, and your district's budget decides your district.

This is the sharpest contrast in the cluster. New Jersey requires every public high school to *offer* the subject. Texas lets computer science *substitute* for a language requirement. California publishes excellent standards and obliges nobody to follow them. Families near the industry often assume the reverse, and find out late.

## The A-G rule families get wrong

The University of California subject requirement, universally called A-G, is the spine of a California college plan.

| What families often believe | What the requirement says |
|---|---|
| Computer science counts as a lab science for UC | Only as an **additional** science, third year and beyond, not toward the core two years |
| Any computer science course counts | It must appear on that high school's **UC-approved a-g course list** |
| Area G is where computer science belongs | Area G is one year of college-preparatory elective, and competes with other subjects |
| Taking computer science lightens the science load | It does not. Biology, chemistry and physics still have to be done |

**Area D** asks for two years of laboratory science providing fundamental knowledge in at least two of biology, chemistry and physics, with a third year recommended. Computer science, engineering and applied science courses may be used in Area D as an additional science only.

Compare Texas, where two computer science credits can be selected in place of the two Languages Other Than English credits outright. California is the stricter system.

## The Algebra II reversal

In July 2023 the University of California's Board of Admissions and Relations with Schools ruled unanimously that existing policy does not allow currently approved data science courses to validate Algebra II, and that the validation should cease without delay. The change applies to admission from autumn 2025.

The workgroup's finding was blunt: courses labelled data science were closer to data literacy, and none came near the standard required of a genuinely more advanced course.

**An introductory data science course, and AP Statistics, cannot substitute for Algebra II** for University of California or California State University admission. A student on a pathway that assumed otherwise should check with a counsellor now rather than in Grade 12.

Two lessons sit inside this. A subject can be genuinely valuable and still not do the credential job somebody promised it would. And the mathematics does not become optional because the coding is exciting. That is why coding and mathematics run on one track here: [complete high school mathematics](https://learn.modernagecoders.com/courses/complete-high-school-mathematics-mastery), and [statistics and probability](https://learn.modernagecoders.com/courses/statistics-probability-maths-course) taught with the algebra underneath intact.

## The California student data project

Each market gets a project on local data containing a particular way of being wrong. California's is what happens when you average a distribution that has no typical member.

CAL FIRE publishes every incident with the acreage it burned. The question: how big is a typical California wildfire?

In the 2020 season nearly 10,000 fires burned more than 4.2 million acres, which divides out to roughly four hundred acres per fire. Arithmetically perfect. Then the student sorts the list.

**The trap.** The August Complex alone exceeded one million acres, the first California fire to pass that mark and the reason the word **gigafire** entered use. One incident accounts for close to a quarter of the entire year, while most fires on the list are tiny. The mean sits far above the typical fire and far below the fire that mattered. It describes almost nothing in the dataset, and there is no arithmetic error anywhere.

The sharper follow-on: comparing mean acreage between two years mostly measures whether a megafire happened, not whether the typical fire changed. A headline saying average fire size doubled may be reporting one event.

**The repair.** Report the median beside the mean and watch the gap. Plot the distribution instead of summarising it. Recompute with the largest fire removed. When a distribution is this lopsided, the shape is the finding.

Students pull current CAL FIRE figures themselves.
