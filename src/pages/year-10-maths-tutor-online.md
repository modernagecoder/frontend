## What changes in maths when a student starts Year 10?

The content stops being Key Stage 3 and becomes the GCSE, and from now on it comes in two speeds. The DfE marks those speeds by typeface.

Year 10 is the start of Key Stage 4. The gov.uk national curriculum guide describes it in four words, "Some children take GCSEs", because a few schools enter some students early, but for most students Year 10 is the first of two years leading to the exams in Year 11. The Key Stage 4 programme of study, dated July 2014, sets the default pace: "The expectation is that the majority of pupils will move through the programme of study at broadly the same pace."

The DfE's GCSE subject content is printed in three kinds of type, and those three styles are the clearest way to understand what Year 10 is teaching and to whom.

**How the DfE GCSE subject content separates the tiers (quoted from the DfE document; the right-hand column is our plain-English reading)**

| Type in the DfE content | Who is assessed on it (DfE wording) | What it means in a Year 10 classroom |
|---|---|---|
| Standard type | "All students will develop confidence and competence with the content identified by standard type" | Taught to everyone, usually first in Year 10 |
| Underlined type | "All students will be assessed on the content identified by the standard and the underlined type" | On both tiers; the harder end of Foundation |
| Bold type | "Only the more highly attaining students will be assessed on the content identified by bold type." | Higher tier only, often started in Year 10 by top sets |

Pearson's 1MA1 specification applies the same rule in its own words: Foundation students "will not be assessed on content identified by bold type", and Higher students are assessed on all three. The practical consequence for Year 10 is that a class can look as if it is "doing the same topic" while two students in it are being prepared for very different questions. Two students learning quadratics, for example, may be heading for factorising alone on Foundation, or for the quadratic formula and completing the square on Higher.

### The class of 2028

Using the gov.uk year table, a student aged 14 to 15 in Year 10 during 2026 to 2027 reaches Year 11 in 2027 to 2028 and normally sits GCSE Maths in summer 2028. That arithmetic is ours.

Ofqual decided in 2026 that, for exams "taken from 2028 onwards", boards must keep providing formulae sheets "for the remaining lifetime of the current specifications".

### What the sheet does not do

Ofqual requires each sheet to be published "by 1 September the year before each exam series", so the 2028 sheet is due by 1 September 2027, at the start of Year 11.

Boards must also "not set questions that can be answered solely by transferring information from the formulae or equations sheets." A sheet on the desk helps only a student who can tell which of its lines a question is asking for.

Sources: [DfE, GCSE mathematics: subject content and assessment objectives](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/gcse-mathematics-subject-content-and-assessment-objectives); [DfE, Mathematics programmes of study: key stage 4](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study); [Ofqual, decisions on formulae sheets from 2028](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/proposed-changes-to-the-assessment-of-mathematics-physics-and-combined-science-gcses). All read on 31 October 2026.

## How is GCSE Maths weighted at Foundation and at Higher?

Very differently. Pearson publishes topic weightings for each tier of 1MA1, and they show the centre of gravity moving from number to algebra as the tier rises.

**Pearson Edexcel GCSE Mathematics 1MA1: topic area weightings by tier, from the specification (Issue 2, June 2015)**

| Topic area | Foundation tier | Higher tier |
|---|---|---|
| Number | 22 to 28% | 12 to 18% |
| Algebra | 17 to 23% | 27 to 33% |
| Ratio, proportion and rates of change | 22 to 28% | 17 to 23% |
| Geometry and measures | 12 to 18% | 17 to 23% |
| Statistics and probability | 12 to 18% | 12 to 18% |

Two rows explain most Year 10 tier conversations. On Foundation, number is weighted at 22 to 28% and algebra at 17 to 23%. On Higher, those almost swap: number falls to 12 to 18% and algebra rises to 27 to 33%, the largest single share on either tier. A Year 10 student who is strong at arithmetic but uneasy with algebra will therefore feel much more comfortable on Foundation papers than on Higher ones, whatever their overall percentage in a test. The other boards in England do not all publish weightings in this form, but they teach the same DfE content.

**The three assessment objectives and their weights, from the DfE GCSE subject content**

| Assessment objective | What it asks | Higher | Foundation |
|---|---|---|---|
| AO1 Use and apply standard techniques | Recall facts, use notation, carry out routine procedures | 40% | 50% |
| AO2 Reason, interpret and communicate mathematically | Chains of reasoning, arguments and proofs, interpreting information | 30% | 25% |
| AO3 Solve problems within mathematics and in other contexts | Turn a problem into mathematical steps and interpret the result | 30% | 25% |

The assessment objectives matter in Year 10 because they explain why a student can know every method and still lose marks. At Higher, routine technique (AO1) is only 40% of the marks; reasoning (AO2) and problem solving (AO3) are 30% each. At Foundation, technique is 50%, so a Foundation student who knows the methods well can bank half the paper, but the other half still rewards explanation and unfamiliar problems. Pearson's specification adds that "Individual papers are not graded": the grade comes from the total across all three papers.

Sources: [Pearson Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Mathematics 1MA1 specification](https://qualifications.pearson.com/en/qualifications/edexcel-gcses/mathematics-2015.html) and the DfE GCSE subject content, both read on 31 October 2026. Our explanation of what the weightings mean for a Year 10 student is ours.

## How much do Year 10 maths mocks matter?

They do not count towards the GCSE at all. They matter because schools use them to set groups and to start the tier conversation, and because they are the first full-length evidence of where marks go.

Mocks are a school practice, not a board requirement, so their timing and format vary. Most English schools run some form of end-of-Year-10 exam, often using past GCSE papers or papers built from them. The result is not a GCSE grade, even when it is reported as one, because a grade depends on grade boundaries set for a real series and on the whole course having been taught. A Year 10 student sitting a full past paper has usually covered only part of the content.

What a mock can do, very well, is show where marks are lost. We use a method we call the mock autopsy, built around the three assessment objectives in the DfE table above.

1. **Collect the marked paper.** Ask the school for the script, or at least the question-by-question marks. A single percentage is almost useless.
2. **Remove the untaught questions.** Cross out any question on content the class has not reached yet. What remains is a fair test of Year 10 so far.
3. **Sort every lost mark by objective.** AO1 if the method was not known or went wrong; AO2 if the answer lacked a reason, a conclusion or a clear chain; AO3 if the student could not see how to start an unfamiliar problem.
4. **Count the three piles.** The biggest pile decides the next term. A large AO1 pile is a content gap; a large AO2 pile is a writing habit; a large AO3 pile is a problem-solving skill.
5. **Pick three topics and one habit.** Never more. Three content topics from the AO1 pile, and one habit from the other two.
6. **Retest in six weeks.** A short paper on the same three topics and the same habit, marked the same way.

### What the piles usually say

A Foundation-leaning student's autopsy is often dominated by AO1: fractions, percentages, ratio. That is good news, because AO1 gaps respond fastest to teaching.

A Higher-leaning student's autopsy is often dominated by AO2 and AO3, which take longer: writing a full argument, or spotting that a geometry question is really an algebra question.

### November is not an option yet

A Year 10 student cannot fix a poor mock by sitting GCSE Maths in November. Pearson's specification says "November entries will only be available to students who were at least 16 on the previous 31 August", as Ofqual's rules require.

For almost every Year 10 student, the only GCSE series ahead is the summer of Year 11.

## Three Year 10 questions of ours, solved in full

These are original questions at the level most Year 10 classes reach. Each one is labelled with the assessment objective it leans on, so the mock autopsy has something to practise on.

### Example 1, compound decrease (AO1)

**Question (ours).** A laptop loses 18% of its value each year. It is worth 950 units now. What is it worth after 3 years, to the nearest unit?

**Think first.** Losing 18% leaves 82%, so each year multiplies the value by 0.82. Three years means three multiplications, not 3 × 18% = 54% off.

**Working.** 950 × 0.82³ = 950 × 0.551368 = 523.7996, so about 524 units.

**Check.** The simple-interest mistake gives 950 × 0.46 = 437, which is too low: each year's 18% is taken from a smaller amount, so less is lost. The DfE lists this formula among those that "should be derived or informally understood".

### Example 2, simultaneous equations in context (AO3)

**Question (ours).** Four pens and three rulers cost 27 tokens. Two pens and five rulers cost 24 tokens. Find the cost of one pen.

**Set up.** Let p be a pen and r a ruler: 4p + 3r = 27 and 2p + 5r = 24. Writing the two equations is the AO3 step; many students stop before it.

**Working.** Double the second: 4p + 10r = 48. Subtract the first: 7r = 21, so r = 3. Then 2p + 15 = 24, so p = 4.5.

**Check in both.** 4(4.5) + 3(3) = 18 + 9 = 27, and 2(4.5) + 5(3) = 9 + 15 = 24. A pen costs 4.5 tokens.

### Example 3, a Venn diagram argument (AO2)

**Question (ours).** In a year group, 60% of students study French, 45% study Spanish and 20% study both. Sam says the probability that a student studies French or Spanish is 1.05. Explain why Sam is wrong and find the correct value.

**Reason.** A probability cannot exceed 1, so 1.05 is impossible. Sam has added 60% and 45%, counting the 20% who study both twice.

**Working.** P(F or S) = P(F) + P(S) &minus; P(F and S) = 0.60 + 0.45 &minus; 0.20 = 0.85.

**Conclusion.** The probability is 0.85. The explanation, not the number, earns the AO2 marks: it names the double counting.

### Why the labels matter

Example 1 is pure technique once the multiplier is seen. Example 2 is mainly about turning words into equations. Example 3 is about saying why something is wrong.

Practising all three kinds in Year 10, not only the first, is the most useful single change a student can make before the Year 11 papers, because AO2 and AO3 are each weighted at 25% or more on both tiers.

**Practice set (ours, answers at the end).** (1) A population of 12 000 grows by 3% a year; what is it after 4 years, to the nearest whole number? (2) Solve 3x + 2y = 16 and x &minus; y = 2. (3) Expand and simplify (2x + 3)(x &minus; 4). (4) Factorise x² + 7x + 12. (5) In a class, P(plays football) = 0.5, P(plays tennis) = 0.3 and P(both) = 0.1; find P(football or tennis). (6) A trapezium has parallel sides 7 cm and 11 cm and height 4 cm; find its area. **Answers:** (1) 13 506; (2) x = 4, y = 2; (3) 2x² &minus; 5x &minus; 12; (4) (x + 3)(x + 4); (5) 0.7; (6) 36 cm².

## How should a Year 10 student plan the year in maths?

Our outline for Year 10 in 2026 to 2027, written around the way most schools run the year. We have assumed a weekly live lesson plus a couple of brief home sessions between lessons.

1. **September to October: find the floor.** A short check of the Key Stage 3 algebra and number that Year 10 builds on. Any shaky line is taught before the first Year 10 topic test.
2. **November to December: algebra early.** Because algebra is the heaviest Higher area and a large Foundation one, we put expressions, equations and graphs first.
3. **January to February: the first full paper.** One past paper, on content covered so far, run as a practice for the mock autopsy.
4. **March to April: reasoning practice.** A weekly AO2 question: explain, prove or criticise a claim, with the reason written out.
5. **May to June: the Year 10 mock.** Revise from the autopsy piles, sit the school mock, and collect the marked script.
6. **July: the autopsy and the summer list.** Sort the lost marks, choose three topics and one habit, and plan the summer around them.
7. **September 2027: Year 11 begins.** The 2028 formulae sheet is due from the boards by 1 September 2027; use it in practice from the first week.

The plan is ours and is a starting shape, not a promise of any grade. A student entering Year 10 well behind needs more weeks on the floor; a top-set student may move to bold-type content by Christmas. The autopsy keeps the plan honest, because it is built from that student's own marks. ## What parents ask about Year 10 maths

### Is Year 10 the start of GCSE Maths?

Yes, for most students. Year 10 is the first year of Key Stage 4 and of the two-year GCSE course, with the exams at the end of Year 11. Some schools begin GCSE topics in Year 9, but Year 10 is when the full course runs.

### When will a Year 10 student sit GCSE Maths?

Normally in the summer of Year 11. A student in Year 10 during 2026 to 2027 will usually sit the papers in summer 2028; the boards have not yet published 2028 dates, so check with the school later in Year 11.

### Do Year 10 mocks count towards the GCSE?

No. Mocks are set by the school and do not form part of the GCSE grade, which comes only from the final papers. Schools often use them to set groups and to begin the tier conversation, so they still deserve proper preparation.

### What is the difference between Foundation and Higher in Year 10?

Foundation leads to grades 1 to 5 and Higher to grades 4 to 9, and Higher adds the content the DfE prints in bold. Pearson's weightings also differ: number is 22 to 28% of Foundation but algebra is 27 to 33% of Higher.

### Will there be a formulae sheet in my child's GCSE Maths exam?

For students sitting GCSE Maths in 2028 or later, yes. Ofqual decided in 2026 that boards must keep providing formulae sheets for the remaining lifetime of the current specifications, published by 1 September the year before each series.

### Can a Year 10 student sit GCSE Maths in November?

Almost never. Pearson's specification, following Ofqual's rules, limits November entries to students who were at least 16 on the previous 31 August, which rules out nearly all Year 10 students.

### What are AO1, AO2 and AO3?

They are the DfE's assessment objectives. AO1 is standard techniques, AO2 is reasoning and communication, and AO3 is problem solving; at Higher they carry 40%, 30% and 30% of the marks, and at Foundation 50%, 25% and 25%.

### My child got a low grade in a Year 10 mock. Should we worry?

Look at the script before the grade. A Year 10 mock often includes content not yet taught, and grade labels on mocks are estimates. Sort the lost marks into technique, reasoning and problem solving, then work on the largest group.
