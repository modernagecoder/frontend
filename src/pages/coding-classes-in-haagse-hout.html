Haagse Hout today

Four wijken, a forest through the middle, all of it read

Haagse Hout was formed in 1988 and covers 9.04 square kilometres, of which 8.82 are land and 0.22 water, holding 27,986 dwellings at a published 5,613 people to the square kilometre. It has four wijken, Bezuidenhout, Benoordenhout, Mariahoeve en Marlot and the Haagse Bos, and the last of those is an elongated urban forest, by far the largest green area in the district, which divides it in two. This is the first Hague stadsdeel in this series whose wijken were all read.

Three populated wijken, and one that is trees

Bezuidenhout holds 19,254 residents on 193 hectares at 10,181 to the square kilometre. Mariahoeve en Marlot holds 15,759 on 309 hectares at 5,156. The Benoordenhout holds 15,393 on 274 at 5,683. Wijk 24 Haagse Bos holds 486 people on 128 hectares, 117 of them land and 11 water, at 415 to the square kilometre.

Three wijken, three different ages

Bezuidenhout is a wijk of young adults: 8,115 of its 19,254 residents are between twenty-five and forty-five, 42 percent, with only 12 percent under fifteen and 12 percent over sixty-five. The Benoordenhout is the reverse, 4,155 residents over sixty-five at 27 percent against 3,130 aged twenty-five to forty-five. Mariahoeve en Marlot sits between them at 31 and 21 percent.

And three different kinds of housing

The Benoordenhout has 7,886 dwellings at an average 654,000 euro, 5,441 owner-occupied, 69 percent, with an average income of 60,100 euro per resident for 2024. Mariahoeve en Marlot has 8,931 dwellings at 301,000 euro, 39 percent owner-occupied, income 34,400. Bezuidenhout has 10,853 at 390,000 euro and 11,305 households, 6,150 of which hold one person.

What a district of four wijken means when one of them is a wood

The Haagse Bos is a wijk in the administrative sense and a forest in every other. Its 486 residents are about one percent of the stadsdeel, its 419 dwellings about one and a half percent of the 27,986, and it has no primary establishment at all. Nothing on this page averages across the four wijken or treats them as four comparable units, because they are not four comparable units and the arithmetic would be meaningless before it was misleading. The Haagse Bos appears here because it was read, because it is genuinely one of the four, and because its very small counts are what make the argument below visible.

Signature project

How many of those digits would you defend?

A percentage resting on fifteen people, two totals for one year, and the question that separates a measurement from its typography.

1. Find what the last digit rests on

The 9 in 3.09 percent is the difference between fifteen children and about fifteen and a bit. There is no such thing as a bit of a child. That digit is not reporting a measurement; it is reporting the outcome of a division, carried out to whatever width the formatter was set to.

2. Ask what would move it

One family. A single household arriving in the Haagse Bos moves the printed share by more than a tenth of a percent, which is more than the whole quantity the third digit is claiming to resolve. When one ordinary event exceeds your stated resolution, the resolution is decorative.

3. Then print what you can defend

Three percent. Possibly about three percent, of a very small wijk. That is less satisfying to look at and it is the honest width, and the discipline is being willing to publish the less impressive-looking number when it is the true one.

The digits are a claim, and you should be able to defend the last one

Every digit you print is an assertion that you know the quantity to that resolution, and readers believe it, because there is no other convention available to them. That is the whole difficulty: precision and accuracy look identical on a screen. A number computed from two reliable counts and a number computed from two guesses come out of the same formatter with the same number of decimal places, in the same font, and no reader can tell them apart without opening the source. So the burden has to be taken up deliberately by whoever is printing, and it almost never is, because the defaults are against you. A language will hand you fifteen significant figures for a division that deserved two. A spreadsheet will carry every one of them into the next cell. A dashboard will render them because they arrived. Nothing in the pipeline asks the only question that matters, which is what would have to happen in the world to change the last digit, and if the answer is one family moving house, or one late row arriving, or the same query being run four minutes later, then that digit is not a fact, it is a residue. The habit worth building is small and slightly embarrassing to perform in public: before publishing a number, say out loud what the last digit rests on. If you cannot finish the sentence, cut the digit. You will publish figures that look less authoritative and you will be right more often, and in a long enough career those two things stop feeling like a trade.

Coding and AI

An AI will repeat 3.09 percent back to you. Why should a teenager in Bezuidenhout ask three point zero nine percent of what?

Because the model reproduces the formatting exactly and has no way to know the formatting was never earned.

Hand a model a table and it will carry the digits through faithfully, which sounds like a virtue and is the problem. It will write that 3.09 percent of the Haagse Bos is under fifteen, and it will be quoting correctly, and the sentence will be misleading in a way that no fact-check can catch, because every character of it matches the source. It will not add that the figure rests on fifteen children, that one family moving changes it, or that the neighbouring wijken get their shares to one decimal place or none. Nor will it notice that a stadsdeel total for 2022 exists in two published versions 534 people apart, because it will have seen whichever one was nearer to hand and had no reason to look for a second.

So the learner practises going the other way, from the printed figure back to what it rests on, and the cheapest place to build that habit is code, where the machine will happily hand over fifteen significant figures for a division that deserved two and never once mention it. A fifteen-year-old in Mariahoeve who has had to write a function that refuses to print a digit it cannot justify will be the person at thirty who looks at a dashboard tile reading 12.7 percent and asks how many rows that came from before anyone acts on it. Models will keep reproducing precision because precision is what is in the text. Asking what it rests on stays a person job.

The case for a child in Haagse Hout learning to code in 2026 is therefore not about statistics. It is that a computer will give you as many digits as you ask for and take no view whatever on whether you were entitled to them, and learning that early, on something small, is worth a great deal later. The longer argument is in why a child should still learn to code in 2026.
