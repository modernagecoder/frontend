Coding competitions for high school students

A high schooler in the US can spend this school year on algorithm contests, a computer science league run through school teams, a cyber defense competition, capture the flag events, or a build challenge judged on a finished app. They reward different skills and run on different calendars, so picking two that suit the student beats entering everything. This page describes eight of them, each from the organiser’s own website as read on 17 October 2026, then gives a practice problem and a month-by-month plan from our teachers.

How to read the table

The first split is solo versus team. USACO, the USA-NA-AIO and the ACSL tests are taken alone; CyberPatriot, FIRST and the build challenges are team efforts where communication matters as much as code.

The second split is speed versus depth. A timed algorithm contest rewards clean thinking under a clock, while an app or SI project rewards a student who can keep improving one idea for weeks.

Picking two, not eight

A sensible pair for most students is one individual contest that runs on its own schedule, such as USACO or ACSL, and one team or build event that fits the fall, such as CyberPatriot or the Congressional App Challenge.

Students who already enjoy maths proofs tend to like USACO. Students who like taking systems apart often prefer capture the flag. Students who want something to show people usually want a build challenge.

USACO

USACO: free online algorithm contests in four divisions

The USA Computing Olympiad runs free online algorithm contests in four divisions. Its rules page is short and specific, which makes it easy to prepare for precisely.

USACO contest rules, from the official instructions page (read 17 October 2026)
TopicAs published by USACO
Who"All are welcome to participate in online USACO contests and training." Selection for the summer camp and the IOI and EGOI teams is limited to pre-college students in the USA.
Cost"There is never any fee for participation."
DivisionsBronze, silver, gold and platinum. "All new participants start out in the bronze division"; full marks during a contest moves a student up straight away.
LanguagesSolutions in C, C++, Java or Python. All bronze-level problems are meant to be fully solvable in Python; some higher-division problems may not be.
LengthTypically 3 or 4 problems. The personal timer of about four to five hours starts when the student opens the contest, at any point in the window.
Time limitsFor most contests, 2 seconds per input case for C and C++, and 4 seconds per input case for Java and Python.
FeedbackGreen for a correct test case, and X, T, !, E or M for a wrong answer, a time limit, a crash or memory limit, an empty output and a missing output.
Integrity"Use of generative AI (e.g., services like copilot or ChatGPT) is prohibited." No pre-written code or templates, no teamwork, no VPNs.

The 2025-26 season, as a guide to 2026-27

On 17 October 2026 the USACO home page still showed the 2025-26 schedule: online contests on January 9 to 12, January 30 to February 2 and February 20 to 23, 2026, then a proctored US Open on March 28, 2026. The site describes that season as somewhat transitional.

No 2026-27 dates were posted on the read date. Students should plan for contests in the winter and check usaco.org before the new year rather than rely on last season’s weekends.

What the time limit really means

Two seconds sounds generous until the input has 100,000 numbers. The skill USACO rewards most is estimating how many steps a solution will take before typing it, which is exactly what the practice problem further down this page teaches.

Our longer guide to USACO coaching covers the divisions in more depth, and the blog post on moving from bronze to silver covers the most common sticking point.

If your school has no ACSL team

Because any teacher, parent or other adult with some CS experience can act as advisor, a parent can register a small team. ACSL’s FAQ says there is no minimum number of students, and it answers yes to homeschoolers directly.

One rule to know: a student in an after-school program whose full-time school already has an ACSL team must compete on the school’s team.

Why the short answer half matters

Topics range from number systems to Boolean algebra and digital electronics, and the 30 minutes go quickly. Students who only practise programming often score well on the 72-hour problem and lose the contest on the theory half.

Our practice sessions alternate: one week of short answer drills on the ACSL wiki topics, one week of a full programming problem with tests.

The AI question

What each competition says about AI tools

This is an easy rule to get wrong, because it changes completely from one event to the next. Breaking it in USACO can mean a lifetime ban; ignoring it in the Presidential SI Challenge means missing the point of the contest.

AI and outside help in each competition, from the organisers’ own pages (read 17 October 2026)
CompetitionRule on AI or outside help
USACOGenerative AI is prohibited during contests; violations lead to a lifetime ban from all USACO activities
ACSLTests are individual; HackerRank plagiarism checks apply, and the Finals run in a secure mode with full-screen enforcement and webcam image analysis
USA-NA-AIOPart 1 runs with app lockdown; Part 2 allows Google Colab while monitoring software and a screen and face recording stay on
Congressional App ChallengeAI tools are permitted if all AI usage is fully disclosed in the submission materials, and AI must not be the entirety of the technical development
Presidential SI ChallengeThe project is about applying SI; teams are asked how they tested or verified the accuracy of SI-generated outputs
CyberPatriotTeams may ask technical support questions during a round, but not about the vulnerabilities themselves

One habit that works everywhere

Our students practise contest problems with AI switched off from the first week, so a ban never comes as a shock. For build projects, they keep a short log of every AI suggestion they kept and why, which turns disclosure into a five-minute task.

ACSL puts the reason plainly on its Finals page: "It is not a contest to see if the student can get the answer using AI, friends, parents, or random internet sources".

Age limits on the tools themselves

Contest rules are only half of it. Each AI product publishes its own age floor, and a few refuse anyone under 18 outright, so a teenager should check that floor first and involve a parent when opening an account.

Our post on studying with AI without cheating covers the line between help and doing the work for you.

Security contests

CyberPatriot and picoCTF: two very different security competitions

Both are about cybersecurity, but one is defense on a deadline as a team, and the other is a puzzle hunt you can practise alone all year.

CyberPatriot in brief

Teams have a coach, who is an approved adult, and between two and six competitors, with at most five competing at any one time. Each team picks one continuous 4-hour block inside the competition weekend window.

For CyberPatriot 19, teams had to be created by October 1, 2026 and competitors added to a roster by October 21, 2026. A student who missed this season can still use the archived training materials and plan to join a team for next season.

picoCTF, now CyLab Security Academy

The picoCTF site now points visitors to CyLab Security Academy, Carnegie Mellon University’s free platform. Its competitors page describes mini-competitions through the year plus an annual flagship for US middle and high school students.

Events allow teams of up to five or are solo, set event by event. Eight domains are covered, from general skills and cryptography to web exploitation, forensics and an AI category on prompt injection. Flagship dates for 2027 were not posted on the read date.

Sources: CyberPatriot competition overview, schedule and registration pages; CyLab Security Academy competitors page; all read 17 October 2026.

The name change

Families searching for the Presidential AI Challenge will find the same program under a new name. The official site says an executive order of September 29, 2026 replaced "Artificial Intelligence" with "Super Intelligence" in the executive branch, so the contest is now the Presidential SI Challenge.

The rules also set a Trailblazer Track for projects that are novel and have nationwide impact; every other project goes in the Standard Track.

Doing both

The deadlines are two months apart, so a student can enter the Congressional App Challenge in October and then turn to an SI project for December. The Presidential rules require original work created after August 1, 2026, so plan a separate project rather than reusing the app.

Our Congressional App Challenge guide walks through that rulebook in detail, and the Presidential AI Challenge guide covers the SI Challenge, renamed on 29 September 2026.

Try one

A practice problem in the style of an algorithm contest

This problem is ours, written for this page, not taken from any contest. It shows the single idea that separates a passing solution from one that times out.

The reading log. A student logged the pages read on each of N days. You are then asked Q questions, each giving two days a and b, and you must print the total pages read from day a to day b inclusive. N and Q can each be as large as 100,000. Input: N and Q on the first line, the N page counts on the second, then Q lines of a and b.

Why the obvious solution fails (our estimate of the work, worst case)
ApproachWork per questionTotal steps when N = Q = 100,000Verdict
Add up the days for every questionUp to N additions100,000 x 100,000 = 10,000,000,000Far too slow for a 4-second limit
Build prefix sums once, subtract per questionOne subtractionAbout 100,000 + 100,000 = 200,000Comfortably fast, even in Python

The habit this builds

Before writing a line, multiply the input sizes. If the answer is in the billions, the plan needs to change. Our rough rule of thumb in class is that plain Python manages somewhere in the tens of millions of simple steps per second, so ten billion is hopeless.

Prefix sums, sorting first, and counting with a dictionary are the three tricks that rescue most early contest problems.

Two follow-ups to try

Change the question to the most pages read on any single day between a and b. Prefix sums no longer work; why not?

Change it to the number of days between a and b with at least 10 pages. Can you build a prefix count instead of a prefix sum?

Where students start

Four stages on the way to contest-ready

Every would-be competitor sits somewhere on this ladder.

From first program to contest-ready (ours)
StepSigns a student is here
1. CoderWrites small programs with loops and lists, but not to a time limit
2. SolverSolves easy contest problems with enough time and a few hints
3. EstimatorChecks the input size first and picks an approach that will run in time
4. CompetitorSolves under a clock, tests edge cases, and reviews every contest afterwards

For parents

The jump from stage 2 to stage 3 is usually the hardest one, and it is a thinking habit, not a new language.

Ask your teen what the input size was in their last problem. If they do not know, that is the habit to build.

For students

Keep a notebook of every problem you could not solve and the idea that unlocked it.

Read the official rules for each event yourself; they are short and they change every season.

High school coding competitions: common questions

What coding competitions can high school students enter in the US?

For 2026-27 the main ones are USACO, ACSL, CyberPatriot, picoCTF (now part of CyLab Security Academy), the Congressional App Challenge, the Presidential SI Challenge, the USA-NA-AIO and the FIRST robotics programs. Each has its own eligibility and calendar.

Which coding competition should a beginner in grade 9 start with?

A student who can write basic Python can start USACO in the bronze division or ACSL in the Intermediate Division, which ACSL describes as suited to high schoolers with little or no programming experience.

Is USACO free?

Yes. The USACO rules page says there is never any fee for participation, and anyone can take the online contests.

Can I use ChatGPT or Copilot in a coding competition?

It depends on the event. USACO prohibits generative AI during contests. The Congressional App Challenge allows AI if all use is disclosed. The Presidential SI Challenge is about applying SI, with accuracy checks. Always read the current rules.

When does ACSL start in 2026-27?

ACSL Contest #1 opens on October 19, 2026 and closes on January 10, 2027. Team registration by an advisor closes on December 31, 2026, and the online Finals are on May 29, 2027.

Can I still join CyberPatriot this season?

For CyberPatriot 19, teams had to be created by October 1, 2026 and rosters finalised by October 21, 2026, so a student without a team should plan for the next season and use the training materials now.

Which language should I learn for coding competitions?

Python is fine for USACO bronze and for ACSL. USACO notes that C++ is the only language supported at the IOI, so students aiming for the top divisions usually move to C++ or Java.

Is the Presidential AI Challenge still running?

Yes, under a new name. The official site now calls it the Presidential SI Challenge. For 2026-27, high school teams sign up by December 18, 2026, and their projects are due five days later, on December 23, 2026.

Do coding competitions help with college applications?

Colleges decide for themselves how they weigh any activity, so we make no promises. What a competition reliably gives is practice under a deadline and real problems to talk about.

When are your classes held?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

Are you connected with USACO, ACSL or any of these competitions?

No. Modern Age Coders is an independent online school. We teach the skills these contests test, and we read each organiser's published rules so our students know what to expect.
