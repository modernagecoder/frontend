| Course | The province&rsquo;s wording | What the student actually does |

A Pre-calculus 11 student who cannot apply the cosine law usually has a Grade 9 gap in similar triangles or a Grade 10 gap in rearranging an equation. A Pre-calculus 12 student lost in radians often never felt comfortable with angles in standard position the year before. Our first lesson with a Burnaby teenager therefore starts one course back, briefly, before it moves forward.

### Special angles

Pre-calculus 11 lists &ldquo;special angles, as connected with the 30-60-90 and 45-45-90 triangles&rdquo;. Students who can sketch those two triangles from memory rarely need a calculator for exact values, and Pre-calculus 12 assumes they can.

### Identities in Grade 12

Pre-calculus 12 asks students to use &ldquo;identities to reduce complexity in expressions and solve equations&rdquo;: Pythagorean, quotient, double angle, reciprocal, sum and difference. Proving an identity and solving an equation are different tasks, and we teach them as such.

## Solving a triangle drawn between three Burnaby SkyTrain stations

The City of Burnaby publishes the positions of the SkyTrain stations in the city as open data, in metres. Pre-calculus 11 students turn three of them into a triangle and solve it completely. The distances and angles below are our own calculations.

The layer is called SkyTrain Station, and the City describes it as points of ingress and egress for the Skytrain network. Our query on 23 October 2026 returned 11 stations, from Gilmore and Brentwood Town Centre in the north to Royal Oak and Edmonds in the south. Each comes with an easting and a northing in metres, so the distance between two stations is simply the Pythagorean theorem applied to the differences.

**Triangle Metrotown (A), Brentwood Town Centre (B), Lougheed Town Centre (C): our straight-line distances between the City of Burnaby station points and our angles from the cosine law. City of Burnaby open data, queried 23 October 2026**

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Side c, Metrotown to Brentwood Town Centre | 4,517.9 m |
| Side b, Metrotown to Lougheed Town Centre | 8,184.9 m |
| Side a, Brentwood Town Centre to Lougheed Town Centre | 7,877.8 m |
| Angle at Metrotown, A | 69.96&deg; |
| Angle at Brentwood Town Centre, B | 77.44&deg; |
| Angle at Lougheed Town Centre, C | 32.60&deg; |
| Each side divided by the sine of its opposite angle | 8,385.6 m |
| Area, by one half of bc sin A | 17.37 km&sup2; |

Two checks are built into the table. The three angles add to 180 degrees, and the sine law ratio comes out at 8,385.6 m for all three sides, exactly as it must. The area agrees too: one half of b times c times the sine of A gives 17.37 square kilometres, the same as the shoelace formula on the three coordinates. Here is the Python that computes the sides and angles:

```
import json, math

with open("skytrainstn.json", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    points = {feat["attributes"]["NAME"]: (feat["geometry"]["x"], feat["geometry"]["y"])
              for feat in json.load(f)["features"]}

A = points["METROTOWN STATION"]
B = points["BRENTWOOD TOWN CENTRE STATION"]
C = points["LOUGHEED TOWN CENTRE STATION"]
a, b, c = math.dist(B, C), math.dist(A, C), math.dist(A, B)

angle_A = math.degrees(math.acos((b*b + c*c - a*a) / (2*b*c)))
angle_B = math.degrees(math.acos((a*a + c*c - b*b) / (2*a*c)))
angle_C = 180 - angle_A - angle_B
print("sides (m):", round(a, 1), round(b, 1), round(c, 1))
print("angles:", round(angle_A, 2), round(angle_B, 2), round(angle_C, 2))
print("sine law:", round(a / math.sin(math.radians(angle_A)), 1))
```

### Closest and farthest

With 11 stations there are 55 pairs. The closest pair in our calculation is Metrotown and Patterson, 764.3 m apart; the farthest is Gilmore and Lougheed Town Centre at 8,679.9 m. Students find both with a loop before they guess.

### Angles in standard position

Measured from east, anticlockwise, Brentwood Town Centre sits at 88.0 degrees from Metrotown, almost due north, and Lougheed Town Centre at 18.1 degrees. In Pre-calculus 12 terms those are 1.536 and 0.315 radians.

### Negative angles

Edmonds lies south-east of Metrotown, so its angle in standard position comes out as minus 25.1 degrees. Students then find the positive coterminal angle, 334.9 degrees, which the Pre-calculus 11 content asks for by name.

### What these numbers are not

Every distance here is a straight line between two points on the City&rsquo;s map, not the length of the track and not a travel time. The trains follow a curving guideway, so a rider covers more than 4,517.9 m between Metrotown and Brentwood Town Centre. A good answer says which distance it means.

## Worked problem: the ambiguous case, settled by a map

Our own Pre-calculus 11 problem. It uses two sides and an angle from the station triangle, chosen so that the sine law offers two answers.

**Question.** In a triangle, side a is 7,877.8 m, side c is 4,517.9 m, and the angle C opposite side c is 32.6 degrees. Find angle A and side b. How many triangles fit these measurements, and which one matches the three Burnaby stations?

**Step 1, use the sine law for A.** sin A divided by a equals sin C divided by c, so sin A equals 7,877.8 times sin 32.6 degrees, divided by 4,517.9, which is about 0.9394. The calculator gives A = 69.96 degrees.

**Step 2, look for the second angle.** Sine is positive in the first and second quadrants, so 180 minus 69.96, or 110.04 degrees, has the same sine. Both are possible here, because 110.04 plus 32.6 is still less than 180. This is the ambiguous case the Pre-calculus 11 content names: two sides and an angle not between them can fit two different triangles.

**Step 3, finish both triangles.** If A is 69.96 degrees, B is 77.44 degrees and the sine law gives b = 8,184.9 m. If A is 110.04 degrees, B is 37.36 degrees and b = 5,088.4 m. Both are correct triangles.

**Step 4, let the data decide.** The real distance from Metrotown to Lougheed Town Centre, from the coordinates, is 8,184.9 m, so the stations form the first triangle. The algebra alone could not choose; extra information had to. Students who learn to ask what information would settle an ambiguity have learned something larger than a trigonometry rule.

**Try it yourself.** (1) Keep a and C the same and shrink c until only one triangle is possible; at what length does that happen? (2) Use the cosine law to find the distance from Patterson to Holdom from their coordinates, then check with the distance formula. (3) Convert the Gilmore angle from Metrotown, 99.2 degrees, to radians. We go through the answers in the free lesson.

## Will an AI solve a Burnaby student&rsquo;s triangle for them?

It will give an answer. Whether it gives both answers, or the right one, is another matter.

Type the ambiguous-case question into a chatbot and it may return 69.96 degrees and stop, never mentioning the second triangle. Ask it for the distance between two stations and it may give a straight line when the question meant track length. A student who understands the sine law and the map catches both slips; one who copies the output does not.

So every lesson asks the student to sketch, estimate and justify before computing. AI tools appear only for students old enough under our age policy, and then only as a second opinion on finished work. Work handed in at school stays the student&rsquo;s own.

The Pre-calculus 11 phrase &ldquo;including ambiguous cases&rdquo; is a small reminder that mathematics is about knowing when an answer is incomplete. The longer argument is in [why problem solving beats copying in the age of AI](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
