Best coding classes for kids in Charlotte

What makes the best coding class for a child in Charlotte?

The best coding classes for Charlotte kids are taught live by a teacher who can see the child’s screen, are matched to the child’s level, and end each week with something the child built. Modern Age Coders runs exactly that online for ages 6 to 12, from Scratch and block games to first Python, in a batch of 15 to 20 or one to one on Eastern Time.

A seven-year-old does not need a programming language so much as a reason to keep going: a cat that moves when told, a game a sibling can lose at, a question with an answer the child worked out. That is what separates a good class from a video playlist. This page sets out what we think the best coding classes for Charlotte children look like and how ours meet that bar, then shows a first data project built on the City of Charlotte’s own bus stop inventory. It is small enough for a fourth grader, and it teaches one of the most useful ideas in all of computing: before you trust a percentage, ask what it is a percentage of.

Kid-sized data project

How many Charlotte bus stops have a shelter? Percent of what?

The City of Charlotte publishes every active CATS bus stop on its open data portal, with a column for shelters, benches and more. We asked the server to count them on 18 October 2026 and turned the answers into a lesson about denominators.

CATS bus stops with a shelter or a bench (our counts and percentages from the City of Charlotte Bus Stops layer, 18 October 2026)
Group of stopsHow many stopsWith a shelterWith a bench or a shelter
All active stops2,849299, or 10.5%637, or 22.4%
Stops marked as a transfer point433103, or 23.8%180, or 41.6%

1. Count the whole

First question: how many stops are there? The server answered 2,849. That number is the denominator, the bottom of every fraction that follows, and children write it down before anything else.

2. Filter a group

Next, keep only the stops whose Shelter column says Shelter. There are 299. Divide by 2,849 and you get about 10.5%, roughly one stop in ten.

3. Change the bottom

Now keep only transfer points, where riders change buses: 433 stops, 103 of them sheltered. Same city, same shelters, yet the share jumps to 23.8% because the bottom of the fraction changed.

The sentence we want every child to be able to say

Saying "23.8% of stops have shelters" would be wrong; the true sentence is "23.8% of transfer-point stops have shelters". Children who learn to name the group behind a percentage at ten are hard to fool with a chart at fifteen.

Younger children do the same lesson with paper slips: one slip per stop in a small sample, sorted into piles, counted, then written as a fraction. Older ones write it in Python with a list of dictionaries and a filter, and then try a bonus question: 14 stops have both a bench and a shelter, so why does "bench or shelter" come to 637 and not 299 plus 352? Working out that the 14 would otherwise be counted twice is a real piece of logic.

All of these numbers are ours, worked out from the city’s layer on 18 October 2026; the city notes that the stop file is updated over time, so a later count may differ. The City of Charlotte publishes the layer under a Creative Commons Attribution NonCommercial ShareAlike licence. We quote only a handful of totals with credit to the city, we do not copy or resell the data, and students download it themselves from the city’s portal.

Coding and AI

Should a Charlotte kid learn to code when AI can write code?

Yes, for the same reason children still learn arithmetic in the age of calculators.

An AI chatbot could tell a child that 23.8% of stops have shelters, and the child would have no way to see the missing words "of transfer points". Children who have filtered the list themselves know exactly which group the number describes, because they chose it.

Our youngest students do not use adult AI tools at all. Older children learn, in a supervised class, what AI is, how it makes mistakes and why a person must check its answers, with tools whose own terms allow their age.

Coding gives a child the power to ask a precise question and check the answer; that is the skill AI makes more valuable, not less. The longer argument is in is coding still worth learning in 2026.

FAQ

Charlotte parents ask

Common questions before a first coding class.

What age can my Charlotte child start coding?

From age 6, with block coding. Scratch usually suits ages 7 to 11, and typed Python often starts around 9 or 10, depending on reading and patience.

Are the classes live or recorded?

Live. A teacher teaches every lesson in real time and can see the child's screen, which is how small mistakes get fixed before they spoil the fun.

What is the Charlotte bus stop project?

Children count City of Charlotte bus stops with shelters, turn the counts into percentages, and learn that 23.8% of transfer points is a different claim from 10.5% of all stops.

Why does the denominator matter?

Because a percentage only means something once you know the group. Of 2,849 stops, 299 have shelters; of 433 transfer points, 103 do. Same shelters, different question.

What time are classes for Charlotte kids?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Times are confirmed in Eastern Time.

Will class times change when the clocks change?

Only on the India side. Your Eastern Time slot is checked again before November 1, 2026 and March 14, 2027, since India keeps one clock all year.

Does my child need a special computer?

A laptop or desktop with a keyboard, a mouse if possible, sound and a reliable connection. Tablets are hard for typed code, so we suggest a computer.

Do young children use AI tools in class?

Not adult ones. Children learn what AI is and how it can be wrong, and any tool used is one whose own terms allow the child's age.

Do you have a center in Charlotte?

No. All lessons are live online from India, so there is nothing to drive to and no building to find.
