### The fine print students miss

The general notes in the same document carry rules that cost marks when they are ignored. One is about order: &ldquo;Stretches and reflections are performed prior to translations unless otherwise stated.&rdquo; A student who translates first gets the right shape in the wrong place.

Another is about technology. For the transformation, inverse and logarithm outcomes, and for factoring polynomials, Alberta says that &ldquo;students are expected to meet these outcomes without the use of technology&rdquo;, even though they may use it to explore. In practice that means the graphing calculator can confirm an answer but cannot be the method.

### Seven processes, not just topics

Alberta lists seven mathematical processes that are meant to run through the whole course: Communication [C], Connections [CN], Mental Mathematics and Estimation [ME], Problem Solving [PS], Reasoning [R], Technology [T] and Visualization [V]. Every specific outcome is tagged with some of them.

Communication is the one families underestimate. The bulletin says students are expected to &ldquo;communicate solutions clearly and effectively when solving both routine and non-routine problems.&rdquo; On the diploma exam that is not a slogan: it is a quarter of the mark, the written-response part.

## The Math 30-1 skills Alberta&rsquo;s markers flag

Each year&rsquo;s bulletin carries a commentary on how students did on past diploma exams. It is the most useful page a Math 30-1 student never reads. We use it as a teaching map: the left column is Alberta&rsquo;s observation, the right is what we do about it in class.

**Areas for improvement named in the 2026 to 2027 Math 30-1 bulletin commentary, and our response (left column quoted; right column ours)**

| Alberta&rsquo;s commentary says students... | How we teach it |
|---|---|
| have &ldquo;difficulty using this notation to represent stretches&rdquo; (mapping notation) | Every transformation is written as a mapping rule first, (x, y) to (new x, new y), before any graph is drawn |
| have &ldquo;difficulty determining the number of invariant points associated with other transformations&rdquo; | Students solve for the points a rule leaves unchanged instead of guessing from a sketch |
| have difficulty &ldquo;combining multiple logarithm laws to simplify an expression into a single logarithm or to solve a logarithmic equation&rdquo; | Laws are applied one per line, with the power law always first, and every solution checked in the original equation |
| have &ldquo;difficulty determining the domain and range of a rational function that contains a point of discontinuity&rdquo; | Factor first, cancel second, and record what the cancelled factor removed from the domain and the range: worked problem 1 below |
| have &ldquo;difficulty solving equations when an identity substitution is required&rdquo; | Students learn to spot a mixed equation, choose the identity that leaves one function, then factor: worked problem 2 below |
| struggle with identities &ldquo;that involve conjugates, double angle identities, and the use of rational operations&rdquo; | Proofs written in two columns, one side at a time, never moving terms across an equals sign |
| struggle with permutation problems with &ldquo;three or more constraints or multiple cases&rdquo; | Cases are listed in a table before any factorial is written, then added |
| have &ldquo;difficulty determining specific terms in the expansion of a binomial with non-linear terms&rdquo; | The general term is written out with its exponent as an expression in k, and k is solved for |

### What markers say about written responses

The written-response observations are blunter. Markers saw many students who do not grasp &ldquo;the difference between simplifying or factoring an expression and solving an equation&rdquo;, or &ldquo;the difference between verifying and proving a trigonometric identity&rdquo;. Those are not slips; they are misunderstandings of what the question asked.

Smaller habits cost marks too: students &ldquo;forget to include the angle argument&rdquo; (writing sin instead of sin x), drop brackets, and &ldquo;write expressions in place of equations&rdquo;. Alberta also warns that &ldquo;Using abbreviations in written explanations is inappropriate and not acceptable.&rdquo;

### What they say students do well

The commentary is not all bad news. Alberta reports that students &ldquo;continue to perform well&rdquo; on coterminal angles, arc length and angles in standard position, that they can &ldquo;solve exponential equations that can be simplified to a common base&rdquo;, and that they relate the parameters of a sinusoidal equation to its graph.

That shapes how we spend time. A student already strong on sinusoidal graphs does not need another week of them; the hour goes to the identity substitution or the multi-case counting problem instead.

## Two Math 30-1 problems, solved the way markers want to see

Both problems are our own, written for this page, and both sit on a line of Alberta&rsquo;s commentary above. They are not Alberta Education questions. Each one is laid out as a full written response: every step shown, every restriction stated, every answer checked.

Problem 1: a rational function with a hole. For f(x) = (x2 &minus; 4) / (x2 &minus; x &minus; 2), state the non-permissible values, the point of discontinuity, the asymptotes, and the domain and range.

1. Factor numerator and denominator. x2 &minus; 4 = (x &minus; 2)(x + 2) and x2 &minus; x &minus; 2 = (x &minus; 2)(x + 1). So f(x) = (x &minus; 2)(x + 2) / ((x &minus; 2)(x + 1)).
2. Record the non-permissible values before cancelling. The original denominator is zero at x = 2 and x = &minus;1. Both are excluded from the domain, whatever happens next.
3. Cancel, and name what the cancelled factor leaves behind. The factor (x &minus; 2) cancels, leaving y = (x + 2) / (x + 1) with x &ne; 2. A factor that cancels gives a hole, not an asymptote.
4. Find the hole&rsquo;s coordinates. Substitute x = 2 into the simplified form: (2 + 2) / (2 + 1) = 4/3. The point of discontinuity is (2, 4/3).
5. Find the asymptotes. The factor (x + 1) remains in the denominator, so x = &minus;1 is a vertical asymptote. Numerator and denominator have equal degree with leading coefficients 1 and 1, so y = 1 is the horizontal asymptote.
6. Write the domain. {x | x &ne; &minus;1, x &ne; 2, x &isin; R}.
7. Write the range, and check the hole. The simplified form is 1 + 1/(x + 1), which never equals 1. It equals 4/3 only when x + 1 = 3, that is x = 2, which is excluded. So the range is {y | y &ne; 1, y &ne; 4/3, y &isin; R}. Forgetting the second restriction is exactly the slip the commentary describes.

Problem 2: an equation that needs an identity substitution. Solve 2cos2x &minus; sin x &minus; 1 = 0 algebraically for 0 &le; x < 2&pi;, giving exact values.

1. Notice the problem. The equation mixes cos2x and sin x, so it cannot be factored as it stands. One function has to go.
2. Choose the identity that leaves one function. From sin2x + cos2x = 1, replace cos2x with 1 &minus; sin2x: 2(1 &minus; sin2x) &minus; sin x &minus; 1 = 0.
3. Simplify to a quadratic in sin x. 2 &minus; 2sin2x &minus; sin x &minus; 1 = 0 becomes 2sin2x + sin x &minus; 1 = 0 after multiplying by &minus;1.
4. Factor and set each factor to zero. (2sin x &minus; 1)(sin x + 1) = 0, so sin x = 1/2 or sin x = &minus;1. Markers specifically remind students to set factors equal to zero rather than stopping at the factored form.
5. Solve each case on the unit circle. sin x = 1/2 at x = &pi;/6 and x = 5&pi;/6 (quadrants I and II). sin x = &minus;1 at x = 3&pi;/2.
6. State the solution set and check it. x = &pi;/6, 5&pi;/6 or 3&pi;/2. Substituting x = 3&pi;/2: 2(0)2 &minus; (&minus;1) &minus; 1 = 0. The other two check the same way, and a graph of the left side crosses zero at exactly these three points in the domain.

### Why the steps are numbered

Alberta&rsquo;s directing word algebraically means &ldquo;Using mathematical procedures that involve variables or symbols to represent values&rdquo;. A graphing calculator would find the three solutions of problem 2 in seconds, but a response that only reports them has not answered a question that says algebraically.

Writing one step per line also makes the response markable. Alberta scores each written-response part against a general scoring guide, and partial credit is available, so a student who sets up the substitution correctly and then slips still earns something.

### What the two problems have in common

In both, the trap is a restriction. In problem 1 it is a value the cancelled factor removed; in problem 2 it is the solutions hiding in a second quadrant. Neither is hard mathematics. Both are skipped by students in a hurry.

So our students finish every solution with the same question: what did I exclude, and did I find everything in the domain? It takes ten seconds and it is the habit we most want them to carry into the exam room.

## Ten Math 30-1 practice questions

We wrote these ten for this page; none is an Alberta Education question and none comes from a released exam. They follow the course from functions to combinatorics. Work them without a calculator unless a question says otherwise, then check the answers in the next section.

1. Composition. Given f(x) = 2x &minus; 3 and g(x) = x2, evaluate (f &#8728; g)(&minus;2) and (g &#8728; f)(&minus;2). Are they equal?
2. Transformations. The point (6, &minus;4) is on the graph of y = f(x). Find its image on the graph of y = &minus;2f(3(x &minus; 1)) + 5.
3. Invariant points. The graph of y = x2 &minus; 9 is reflected in the x-axis. How many invariant points are there, and what are they?
4. Logarithm laws. Write 2log5x &minus; log5(x + 1) + log53 as a single logarithm.
5. Common base. Solve 4x + 1 = 8x &minus; 1 algebraically.
6. No common base (calculator allowed). Solve 3x = 20, to the nearest hundredth.
7. Logarithmic equation. Solve log3(x + 6) + log3x = 3 algebraically, and justify any value you reject.
8. Polynomials. Factor P(x) = x3 &minus; 2x2 &minus; 5x + 6 and state its zeros and y-intercept.
9. Radians. A circle has radius 12 cm. Find the arc subtended by a central angle of 2&pi;/3, exactly and to the nearest hundredth. Then give the negative angle coterminal with 5&pi;/3 that lies between &minus;2&pi; and 0.
10. Counting. How many different arrangements of the letters in CANADA are there? How many begin and end with A?

Questions 2, 3, 4 and 7 come straight from the commentary&rsquo;s list of weak spots, so a student who gets them right has fixed something markers see often. A question you cannot start is information, not failure: note it, and bring it to a class.

## Answers to the ten questions, with the reasoning

Each answer was checked by substitution and in Python. The reasoning matters more than the number, because on the written-response part it is the reasoning that earns the marks.

1. 5 and 49; not equal. (f &#8728; g)(&minus;2) = f(g(&minus;2)) = f(4) = 5. (g &#8728; f)(&minus;2) = g(f(&minus;2)) = g(&minus;7) = 49. Composition is not commutative, which is the point of the question.
2. (3, 13). The mapping rule is (x, y) to (x/3 + 1, &minus;2y + 5): the horizontal stretch by 1/3 and the reflection and vertical stretch come before the translations. x: 6/3 + 1 = 3. y: &minus;2(&minus;4) + 5 = 13.
3. Two: (&minus;3, 0) and (3, 0). A reflection in the x-axis leaves a point unchanged only where y = 0, so the invariant points are the x-intercepts. Solve x2 &minus; 9 = 0.
4. log5(3x2 / (x + 1)). Power law first: 2log5x = log5x2. Then the product law joins x2 and 3, and the quotient law puts x + 1 underneath.
5. x = 5. Write both sides as powers of 2: 22x + 2 = 23x &minus; 3. Equate exponents: 2x + 2 = 3x &minus; 3, so x = 5. Check: 46 = 84 = 4096.
6. x &asymp; 2.73. Take logarithms of both sides: x = log 20 / log 3 = 2.7268..., which rounds to 2.73. Keep every decimal until the final step, as the bulletin instructs.
7. x = 3. The product law gives log3(x(x + 6)) = 3, so x2 + 6x = 27 and (x + 9)(x &minus; 3) = 0. Reject x = &minus;9: log3(&minus;9) is undefined, since a logarithm takes positive arguments only. Check x = 3: log39 + log33 = 2 + 1 = 3.
8. (x &minus; 1)(x + 2)(x &minus; 3); zeros &minus;2, 1 and 3; y-intercept 6. P(1) = 1 &minus; 2 &minus; 5 + 6 = 0, so x &minus; 1 is a factor by the factor theorem. Dividing leaves x2 &minus; x &minus; 6 = (x + 2)(x &minus; 3).
9. 8&pi; cm, about 25.13 cm; and &minus;&pi;/3. Arc length is radius times angle in radians: 12 &times; 2&pi;/3 = 8&pi;. For the coterminal angle, subtract one full turn: 5&pi;/3 &minus; 2&pi; = &minus;&pi;/3.
10. 120, then 24. CANADA has six letters with A repeated three times: 6! / 3! = 720 / 6 = 120. Fixing an A at each end leaves C, N, D and A in the middle, all different, so 4! = 24.

## How we pace Math 30-1 across a school term

Schools set their own unit order, so we start from the student&rsquo;s course outline. When a family asks us for a default, this is the order we use and the reason for each step. It is our plan, not Alberta&rsquo;s.

**A default unit order for Math 30-1 tutoring (ours)**

| Stage | Units | Finished when the student can |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Transformations, inverses, function operations and composition | Write any transformation as a mapping rule and find invariant points by solving, not sketching |
| 2 | Exponential and logarithmic functions and equations | Combine three log laws in one line and reject an extraneous root with a reason |
| 3 | Polynomial, radical and rational functions | Find zeros with multiplicity, and the domain and range of a rational function with a hole |
| 4 | Angles, the unit circle, the six ratios and trigonometric graphs | Give exact values from memory and read amplitude, period and phase shift from an equation |
| 5 | Trigonometric equations and identities | Solve with an identity substitution, and prove an identity working one side |
| 6 | Permutations, combinations and the binomial theorem | Split a counting problem into cases and find one term of an expansion with a non-linear term |
| 7 | Mixed review and timed practice | Finish a full-length mixed set in three hours with the written responses fully laid out |

### Why functions come first

Relations and Functions is more than half of the exam blueprint, and its ideas return everywhere: a sinusoidal graph is a transformed sine function, and an exponential model is a function to be inverted. Students who master mapping notation early find the trigonometry unit shorter.

Logarithms come early for a different reason. They take repetition to become automatic, and a student who meets them in October has months of spaced practice before the exam. A student who meets them in May has days.

### What a weekly class looks like

A class starts with five minutes of mixed retrieval from earlier units, then the current school topic taught from the student&rsquo;s own homework, then one written response laid out in full. The student does the writing; the teacher asks questions.

Every few weeks we set a short mixed set in the diploma style: machine-scored questions first, then one written response. Its mistakes go into a log the student keeps until exam week.

For the exam itself, Alberta&rsquo;s schedule for 2026 to 2027 sets Mathematics 30-1 at 9 AM on Tuesday 3 November 2026, and on Wednesday 20 January, Thursday 8 April, Thursday 17 June and Thursday 5 August 2027. The exam earns a separate plan for its final weeks, built around the written-response rules, and we start that plan about eight weeks before the student&rsquo;s sitting. ## Math 30-1: common questions

### What is Math 30-1 in Alberta?

Mathematics 30-1 is the Grade 12 Alberta math course for students heading to post-secondary programs that require calculus. Its three strands are Relations and Functions, Trigonometry, and Permutations, Combinations and Binomial Theorem, and it ends with a provincial diploma exam.

### Do you grant the Math 30-1 credit?

No. We teach alongside your teen's Math 30-1 class and do not grant provincial credits. The credit and the school-awarded mark come from their own school, and the diploma exam portion is set and marked by Alberta Education and Childcare.

### How much is the Math 30-1 diploma exam worth?

Alberta says the weighting of diploma exams returned to 30 percent on 1 September 2023: 70 percent of the final mark comes from course work and 30 percent from the diploma exam.

### What is on the Math 30-1 diploma exam?

For 2026 to 2027 the bulletin lists 32 machine-scored questions worth 75 percent and 3 written-response questions worth 25 percent. Relations and Functions carries 53 to 58 percent, Trigonometry 27 to 33 percent, and the counting strand 14 to 18 percent.

### When is the Math 30-1 diploma exam in 2026 to 2027?

Alberta schedules it at 9 AM on Tuesday 3 November 2026, Wednesday 20 January 2027, Thursday 8 April 2027, Thursday 17 June 2027 and Thursday 5 August 2027. Schools register first-time students.

### Which units of Math 30-1 are hardest?

Alberta's own commentary points to logarithm equations that combine several laws, rational functions with a hole, trigonometric equations that need an identity substitution, identity proofs, and counting problems with several cases. Those are the units we spend most time on.

### Can I use a calculator in Math 30-1?

Yes, an approved graphing calculator is required on the diploma exam, cleared and configured before and after. But Alberta expects several outcomes, including transformations and logarithm laws, to be met without technology, so steps still have to be shown.

### What comes before and after Math 30-1?

Alberta's Guide to Education shows Math 10C, then Math 20-1, then Math 30-1 as the typical route, and says Math 30-1 is a prerequisite or corequisite for Mathematics 31.
