# Coding Classes in British Columbia

> British Columbia delivers Applied Design, Skills and Technologies content in the middle years as short modules that the Ministry says may be offered in a variety of ways, in rotation being its own example, with Grade 8 and 9 options differing again. So what a BC learner has actually met varies by school and by year. Modern Age Coders teaches live online coding and math to ages 6 to 67 in BC, from USD 100 a month, and the first class is free.

## How does ADST actually reach a BC student?

Applied Design, Skills and Technologies is the BC learning area containing design thinking, making, and the digital material a family would recognise as coding. How it reaches a student changes with the grade band.

| Grades | What the Ministry states | What it means for a parent |
|---|---|---|
| K to 5 | Big Ideas and Curricular Competencies are provided for use with grade-level content from other learning areas, through exploratory and purposeful play | Cross-curricular by design. No separate subject to inspect, so provision is hard to see from outside |
| 6 and 7 | A common set of Content options is structured as short modules that may be offered in a variety of ways, for example in rotation | Which options a school runs, and which a student sits in, varies. Two children in one grade can meet different material |
| 8 and 9 | The Content options differ from those in Grades 6 and 7, and may also be offered as modular rotations of varying length or as full-year courses | Different options again, and the length varies from a short rotation to a full year |

Source: [BC Ministry of Education, Applied Design, Skills and Technologies introduction](https://curriculum.gov.bc.ca/content/applied-design-skills-and-technologiesintroduction). Verified 17 August 2026.

**We have deliberately not repeated** widely circulated claims that ADST is mandatory for specific grades, or that students take a minimum number of modules, because neither could be confirmed at a Ministry source.

## Is that a problem with the curriculum?

No, and it is worth saying so plainly.

Rotations exist for good reasons. They let a school use the specialist teachers and rooms it actually has, expose a student to several fields rather than one, and adapt year to year. **The cost is predictability.** A curriculum built to vary cannot also tell you what any individual child covered, and no policy document can close that gap. Only the school can.

What it changes for us is concrete. In most markets a grade level is a reasonable first guess at a starting point. In British Columbia it is close to useless, which is why every learner here starts with a free session where a teacher watches them work rather than asking what they have done.

## What should I ask my child's school?

**Ask which modules, not whether ADST happened.**

"Does the school do ADST?" is answered yes everywhere, truthfully, and tells you nothing. "Which ADST modules did you offer this year, and which was my child in?" takes a teacher thirty seconds and is the only way to find out whether your child has met anything resembling programming.

The answer usually falls into one of three shapes:

- **They did a digital rotation.** Good. Ask what they built. If they can describe it and explain how it worked, they are ready for typed code
- **They did other rotations.** Also fine, and common. They may have had an excellent year of making things without touching code. Start from the beginning without treating it as remedial
- **Nobody is quite sure.** The most frequent answer in the middle years, and the reason the free session exists

**What we will not claim:** nothing here appears on a BC transcript, earns credit, or satisfies any graduation requirement.

## The British Columbia student data project

Every market page here works through one analytical trap. BC's comes straight out of the rotation structure: **counting arrangements when you meant to count sets.**

The learner starts from a real question with a countable answer. If a school runs a set of modules and each student sits in some of them, how many genuinely different ADST years can exist in one grade? The learner picks the numbers, because the Ministry publishes the structure rather than a roster: say eight modules offered, three taken.

Almost everybody computes 8 x 7 x 6 and gets **336**.

But 336 counts *ordered* selections. It treats a student who did woodwork, then digital, then food as different from one who did digital, then food, then woodwork. For the question actually asked, which modules did you cover, those are the same year. Every distinct set of three has been counted six times, because three things can be arranged in six orders.

The real answer is 336 divided by 6 = **56**. Still a lot, and that is the point: 56 meaningfully different years inside one grade in one school is exactly why nobody can tell you what a BC child covered from their grade alone. But the learner only earns that number by noticing that order was never part of the question.

Then it generalises. Choosing k things from n unordered means dividing by k factorial. The learner writes the function, then tests it against a brute-force list of every possibility for small n to prove the formula agrees. **Checking a clever answer against a slow one you trust is the most transferable habit in the whole curriculum.**

Every number in the exercise is chosen by the learner. The Ministry publishes the rotation structure, quoted above; it does not publish module counts per school, and we have not invented any.

## Why teach coding at all when AI can write it?

BC's own curriculum answers this better than an advertisement could, because the learning area is not called programming. It is called applied design, skills and technologies, and it puts deciding what to make ahead of any particular way of making it.

Ask an AI how many different three-module years are possible from eight modules and you may well get 336, stated confidently, with the multiplication shown. You may also get 56. Which one you get depends on how the question was worded, and the answer arrives with the same tone either way. Nothing in the output tells you that a decision about order was made on your behalf.

The durable skill is noticing the question contained an assumption, then checking a clever answer against a slow one you trust. That is also, incidentally, how you supervise a machine that writes code faster than you can read it.
