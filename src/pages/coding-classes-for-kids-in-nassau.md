# At six it should be a game. At twelve it should be a language.

> Modern Age Coders teaches children in Nassau aged six to twelve in live online weekly classes, in batches of five to eight or one to one. Younger children start with block-based work and small amounts of typed Python alongside it; by about ten the emphasis moves to typed code, reading error messages and building something that runs. Placement is decided by a task in the free first class rather than by age. Sessions are ninety minutes on weekend mornings in Bahamian time. Group teaching is USD 100 a month, private USD 150, and the first class is free.

## What a six year old should actually be doing

Not Python. A six year old should be finding out that a machine will do exactly what it is told and nothing else, which is a genuinely surprising discovery and the foundation of everything that follows.

That is blocks, sequence, repetition and the first experience of something not working and being fixed. It is about ninety minutes a week and it should be enjoyable, because a child who concludes at six that this is unpleasant has been given a conclusion that lasts.

## The transition that most programmes get wrong

Somewhere around nine, typed code has to start. Not instead of blocks, alongside them, and in small amounts.

The reason is specific. Blocks cannot be spelled wrongly. A child who has only ever assembled correct-by-construction programs has never met a syntax error, has never read a traceback, and arrives at twelve with real logical ability and a complete inability to sit with a broken file. Parents see this and think their child has lost interest. What has happened is that the child has hit the first genuinely uncomfortable step, eight years later than necessary.

We start typed Python from around nine, deliberately, in the same session as blocks, and the first goal is not fluency. It is that a red error message is boring rather than alarming.

## What you would see at home, by age

| Age | What you would notice | What you should not expect yet |
|---|---|---|
| 6 to 8 | Explaining to you, at length, what their program does | Typed code, or anything resembling AI |
| 9 to 10 | A blank file becoming something that works | Data, or models |
| 11 to 12 | Reading an error instead of asking for help | Machine learning with a measured result |
| 12 and up | Complaining about how bad a data file was | A finished portfolio, yet |

The third row is the one we watch for, because it is the reliable signal. A child who reads their own error message before calling an adult has crossed the line that matters, and everything after it is a question of time.

## Group size, and why the number is small

Five to eight students, on a call where screens are shared both ways. That number is not arbitrary: it is the largest group in which a teacher can watch code being typed and interrupt while a misconception is forming rather than after it has been built on.

A room of twenty children with one instructor circulating is a different product, whatever the label says. When you compare providers, ask for the number rather than the adjective.

## The one thing we will not do

Promise that a child of eight will be building AI. They will not, and any provider telling you otherwise is showing your child a pre-built model and calling it their work. Data handling and measured modelling take months and cannot be compressed into a term, let alone a week. What an eight year old can get is the foundation those things stand on, which is worth considerably more and takes longer to say.
