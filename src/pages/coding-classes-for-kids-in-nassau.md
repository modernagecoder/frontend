The question behind the search

## What should "coding for kids" actually mean at six, at nine and at twelve?

These three ages want completely different things, and a programme that treats them as one audience is either boring the older children or losing the younger ones.

| Age | What the class should be | What they take away | The ceiling of this stage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6 to 8 | A game with rules they can change | Sequence, repetition, cause and effect, and the confidence that they can make a computer do something | A block cannot be misspelled, so they never meet precision or a readable error |
| 9 to 11 | Blocks plus small amounts of typed Python | The first real syntax, the first traceback read rather than feared, and something they built on purpose | Programs stay small, so they never meet the problem of organising code |
| 12 | Typed Python as the normal medium | Functions, files, data, and a project that runs for somebody else | Without data and measurement it stops short of anything you could call AI |

The row that parents find most useful is the last column. Every stage has a ceiling, and the ceiling is not a failure of the stage: it is the point at which continuing to do the same thing stops adding anything. A nine year old who has been dragging blocks for three years is not further ahead than one who started last term, and that is the single most common thing we see in first classes.

The moment that matters

## When blocks have to stop, and what makes typing worth the friction

Somewhere between about eight and ten there is a transition that decides whether a child keeps going with this subject or quietly drifts away from it.

Block environments were a genuinely great invention. They removed spelling from programming and let a six year old build a working thing on the first afternoon, which is exactly what a six year old needs. Nobody sensible argues against them at that age.

The problem is that they also remove the two experiences a programmer is actually made of. A block cannot be misspelled, so a child never learns that a computer is unforgiving in a precise and knowable way. And a block program rarely produces a readable error, so a child never learns that an error message is a sentence written to help them.

Typed code restores both, and it costs something: the first weeks are slower and more frustrating than dragging. That trade is worth making somewhere around nine, and it is much harder to make at fourteen, because by then a child has three or four years of evidence that programming means dragging and feels betrayed by the change.

So our answer is small amounts of typing early, alongside the blocks rather than instead of them. A child of eight writing three lines of Python that draw a square has met the whole relationship, and will not experience the transition as a demotion when it comes.

For Nassau parents specifically

## Four practical things worth knowing before you book anything

### Weekend mornings, not after school

Our teachers are in India, nine and a half to ten and a half hours ahead, so a four o'clock Tuesday in Nassau is half past two in the morning for them. We do not offer it. Saturday at eight works beautifully at both ends.

### Ninety minutes is right, once a week

Shorter than that and a child spends the session getting started; longer and the last part is wasted. Once a week with something to build in between beats three short sessions with nothing between them.

### The group matters more than you would think

Five to eight children at a similar level, hearing each other get the same thing wrong in different ways, learn faster than one child alone. That is why one to one is not automatically the premium option at this age.

### Nothing gets published without you

Older students keep a public GitHub repository. For a child, no personal information, no photographs and no real names of other people go anywhere near it, and a parent knows the account exists.

### You will hear from us monthly

A few hundred words in plain English: what was covered, what your child found hard, what they built and what would help next. Not a dashboard and not a score out of ten.

### It is a year, not a week

The whole design assumes twelve months with the same teacher. A holiday camp is a good purchase for discovery and a poor one for accumulation, which is argued properly on its own page.

## Five questions to ask anybody selling children's coding, including us

This market is noisy and a parent has no easy way to see behind a course title. These five questions separate a programme from an activity, and we are happy to be held to our own answers.

### Will my child type code, and from when?

If the answer is blocks indefinitely, the ceiling arrives around ten. Ours: small amounts of typed Python from about eight or nine, alongside blocks rather than replacing them.

### Is it the same teacher every week?

Rotating instructors mean nobody remembers what happened last month, which is where most of the value in a long programme lives. Ours: the same person for the twelve months.

### What will I receive in writing?

A certificate at the end is not progress reporting. Ours: a few hundred words in plain English every month on what was covered, what was hard and what would help next.

### What does my child have at the end?

If the answer is a folder or a certificate, ask what a stranger could open. Ours: something running at a public address, with the code readable and a year of dated commits behind it.

### What time is my child's class at your end?

The question nobody asks and the one that predicts whether a schedule survives. For an overseas provider a four o'clock local slot may be the middle of their night. Ours: a Nassau Saturday morning is early evening for the teacher, which is why we offer it and not weekday afternoons.

None of those five is about how advanced the material is. They are all about whether the thing is a continuous programme or a series of pleasant afternoons, and both exist in this market.

Questions from parents of younger children

## What Nassau parents of six to twelve year olds ask

### How young is too young?

Six is our floor and it is a real floor rather than a marketing one. Below that, most children do not yet have the reading and mouse control to get value from a ninety-minute session, and the honest advice is to wait a year. At six to eight the work is a game with rules the child can change, and the aim is that making a computer do something feels normal to them rather than remarkable.

### My child already does blocks at school. Is this the same thing?

It starts in the same place and it does not stay there. Blocks are an excellent on-ramp and a poor destination, because a block cannot be misspelled and rarely produces a readable error, so a child never meets the two experiences programming actually consists of. We introduce small amounts of typed Python from around eight or nine, alongside the blocks rather than instead of them, so the transition is not experienced as a demotion later.

### Can you teach after school?

No, and the reason is arithmetic rather than preference. Our teachers are in India, nine and a half to ten and a half hours ahead of the Bahamas depending on the season, so four in the afternoon in Nassau is half past two in the morning at their end. We will not staff that. Weekend mornings are the main offer and they suit most families here better anyway.

### How much does it cost?

A group batch of five to eight students is USD 100 a month and one to one teaching is USD 150 a month, billed in US dollars, the same flat figures everywhere outside India. There is no enrolment fee, no annual contract, and the first class is free without a card.

### Would one to one be better for a young child?

Usually not, which is the opposite of what most families expect. At this age a good deal of the learning comes from hearing five other children attack the same problem badly before somebody gets it right, and a child alone with a teacher misses all of that. One to one is the right answer for a specific reason, such as a weekday morning slot or a learning need, and those reasons are set out on their own page.

### What if my child loses interest?

Then we would rather you stopped than kept paying. There is no minimum term and no enrolment fee, so stopping costs you the month you are in. What we do first is change the project rather than the child: nearly every case of a bored nine year old we have seen was a child working on somebody else's idea, and letting them choose something they can check the answer to fixes it more often than not.

### Do the classes involve any screen-free work?

Some, deliberately. A good deal of early computational thinking is arguing about the order of steps, predicting what a program will do before running it, and explaining out loud why something failed. None of that needs a screen and all of it transfers. For younger children it is also a useful break in the middle of ninety minutes.

### Will my child put anything online?

Not at this age without you knowing. Older students keep a public GitHub repository because that is the asset the whole programme builds toward, and for anybody under eighteen the rules are firm: no personal information, no photographs, no real names of other people, and a parent aware of the account. For a six or eight year old the work usually stays private entirely.

### How do we know if it is working?

A written note each month in plain English, and the thing itself. By the end of the first quarter a child should be able to show you something they made and tell you what each part does, which is a far better test than any grade we could invent. If they can show it and not explain it, we would treat that as our problem to fix.

### What happens after we send the form?

A mentor telephones you at a Bahamian hour to arrange the free session. Nothing is charged and no card is requested. Sending the form starts a conversation rather than an enrolment and reserves no place. Which entry point suits your child, and which weekend slot, are settled on that call before money comes up.
