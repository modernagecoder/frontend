## Fitting a sine curve to a Calgary rooftop&rsquo;s solar output

The City of Calgary publishes how much electricity each of its solar installations produces every month. Math 30-1 students take one site, find its amplitude, midline, period and phase shift, and test how well a sinusoidal function describes a real year. Every figure below is our own calculation.

The dataset is Monthly Solar Energy Production (KWh), which the City describes as the monthly output at each of The City of Calgary&rsquo;s solar photovoltaic projects. Our download on 23 October 2026 held 2,323 rows across 25 facility names, from December 2010 to December 2025. We chose Southland Leisure Centre: 123 monthly readings from September 2015, no month recorded twice, and nine complete calendar years from 2016 to 2024.

| February | 4,278.6 | 2,556.7 | 5,146.9 |
| March | 11,115.2 | 6,769.4 | 10,591.6 |
| April | 18,042.1 | 12,524.0 | 16,680.5 |
| May | 21,350.8 | 18,278.6 | 21,781.8 |

The hand model is what a Math 30-1 student builds with a pencil. The highest mean, 24,033.2 kWh in July, and the lowest, 1,014.8 in December, give an amplitude of half their difference, 11,509.2, and a midline halfway between them, 12,524.0. A year is 12 months, so b is 2&pi;/12, or &pi;/6, and putting the peak at month 7 gives y = 11,509.2 cos(&pi;/6 (x &minus; 7)) + 12,524.0. It explains 89 percent of the month-to-month variation, but it runs late: spring is too low and autumn too high.

The least-squares model, fitted with a little more mathematics, moves the peak back to month 6.39, between June and July, with amplitude 11,781.4 and midline 12,995.2. It explains 99.1 percent of the variation. A peak near the end of June makes physical sense, since days are longest then. Here is the Python for the hand model, which students write first:

```
import csv, math, statistics

with open("monthly-solar.csv", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    site = {r["Period"]: float(r["Solar PV Production (kWh)"])
            for r in csv.DictReader(f)
            if r["Facility Name"] == "SOUTHLAND LEISURE CENTRE"}

years = range(2016, 2025)  # the complete years
means = [statistics.mean(site[f"{y}-{m:02d}"] for y in years) for m in range(1, 13)]
top, bottom = max(means), min(means)
a = (top - bottom) / 2      # amplitude
d = (top + bottom) / 2      # midline
c = means.index(top) + 1    # month of the peak
for m in range(1, 13):
    model = a * math.cos(math.pi / 6 * (m - c)) + d
    print(m, round(means[m - 1]), round(model))
```

### Amplitude and midline

Amplitude is half the swing between the sunniest and darkest month; the midline is the level the output swings around. Students should say both in kWh, not just as numbers, and explain why neither is an average of the twelve months in the hand method.

### Phase shift

The horizontal shift c places the peak. Our first guess, month 7, came from the largest monthly mean; the better fit says 6.39. The difference is a lesson in itself: a model built from two points is only as good as those two points.

### Period

The period is fixed by the calendar at 12 months, which is why b is &pi;/6. In a contrasting exercise, students try a period of 11 or 13 and watch the fit fall apart within two years.

### A year that does not fit

June output at this site fell from 25,613 kWh in 2016 to 18,379 in 2024, and 2025 readings are roughly half the usual level, with October 2025 missing altogether. The file does not say why: equipment, shading, maintenance or a recording change are all possible. That is why we fitted only the complete years from 2016 to 2024, and why a student should flag 2025 rather than average it in.

## Worked problem: when does output stay above 18,000 kWh?

Written by us at Math 30-1 level. It turns the hand model into a trigonometric inequality and then checks it against the data.

**Question.** Using y = 11,509.2 cos(&pi;/6 (x &minus; 7)) + 12,524.0, where x is the month number, find the part of the year when the model predicts more than 18,000 kWh a month. Then compare with the monthly means in the table.

**Step 1, isolate the cosine.** Subtract the midline and divide by the amplitude: cos(&pi;/6 (x &minus; 7)) &gt; (18,000 &minus; 12,524.0) / 11,509.2, which is about 0.4758.

**Step 2, find the reference angle.** The inverse cosine of 0.4758 is about 1.0749 radians. Cosine is greater than 0.4758 when the angle lies between &minus;1.0749 and 1.0749 within one cycle, so &pi;/6 (x &minus; 7) must lie in that interval.

**Step 3, solve for x.** Multiply through by 6/&pi;: x &minus; 7 lies between &minus;2.05 and 2.05, so x lies between about 4.95 and 9.05. The model says output stays above 18,000 kWh from the very start of May to the very start of September.

**Step 4, check against reality.** The table says April already averaged 18,042.1 kWh and September only 15,210.1. The real window is roughly April to August, a month earlier than the hand model predicts. Repeating the steps with the least-squares model, peak 6.39, gives x between about 4.23 and 8.55, much closer to the data. The algebra was right; the phase shift was wrong.

**Try it yourself.** (1) Write the hand model as a sine function instead of a cosine. (2) Find the months when the least-squares model predicts less than 3,000 kWh. (3) State the domain and range of the hand model for one year, and explain why the range should not include negative values. Bring your answers to the free lesson and the teacher will mark them with you.

## Why a Calgary student should not let an AI fit the curve

A tool can fit a sine curve in a second. Knowing whether to trust it takes understanding.

Give an AI tool the Southland file and ask for a model, and it may fit all eleven years at once, 2025 included, and present the result with confidence. It will not stop to ask why 2025 is half the size of 2016, or why the peak should be near the end of June. A student who has built the model by hand can see both problems at a glance.

Our teachers ask Calgary students to sketch the graph, predict the parameters and only then compute. Older students use AI tools under the age rules we follow, and only to check finished work. Anything handed in for a mark at school remains the student&rsquo;s own.

The Math 30-1 bulletin notes that students handle sinusoidal parameters well; knowing when the data breaks the model is the next step. The longer argument is in [why thinking for yourself still matters with AI](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
