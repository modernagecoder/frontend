## Loops inside loops: the Ontario Grade 4 coding a North York child meets

North York public schools follow the Ontario curriculum. We read the Grade 4 math expectations from the ministry&rsquo;s curriculum content service on 22 October 2026. The left column quotes the ministry; the right column is how we teach it.

- **Grade 4, C3.1**: The coding expectation now covers &ldquo;code that involves sequential, concurrent, repeating, and nested events&rdquo;. Our answer: Nested is the new word. We begin with a child walking a square on the classroom floor, four sides, four turns, then ask what happens if the square itself is repeated six times around a point.
- **Grade 4, teacher notes**: The ministry&rsquo;s example program: &ldquo;Repeat 4 times: Move 100 steps. Turn 90 degrees.&rdquo; Our answer: Children type this in Scratch, predict the shape, and then change 90 to 60. The square becomes something else, and finding out why is the lesson.
- **Grade 4, teacher notes**: &ldquo;Loops make the code more readable and reduce the number of instructions that need to be written.&rdquo; And: &ldquo;Loops can exist within loops, referred to as &ldquo;nested loops&rdquo;.&rdquo; Our answer: We count blocks before and after. In one of our exercises a star drawn with no loop takes forty blocks; with two loops it takes six. Children love seeing their own long program shrink.
- **Grade 4, C3.2**: Children &ldquo;read and alter existing code&rdquo;, and the notes add: &ldquo;Code can be altered to be used for a new situation.&rdquo; Our answer: Our favourite move: hand over a working program for one job and ask the child to bend it to a different one, such as turning a square drawer into a hexagon drawer.

One line in the Grade 4 notes matters for the project further down. Among the contexts the ministry suggests for coding, it lists &ldquo;determining area, using whole numbers&rdquo;. Area is length times width, and a nine-year-old who can multiply already has everything needed to write a program that does it a thousand times without getting tired. The [Ontario coding guide for Grades 1 to 8](/ontario-coding-curriculum-grades-1-to-8) covers the years before and after; here we stay with Grade 4, because it is the year North York parents most often tell us the homework stopped making sense.

### EQAO two years on

### Blocks or text

### Away from the phone

## Which North York ice pad has the most room to skate? Measuring the City&rsquo;s rink files

Parks, Forestry & Recreation publishes two rink lists as open data: one for indoor ice and one for outdoor artificial rinks. Both have a column called Community Council Area, and North York is one of its values. Our older kids keep those rows, multiply length by width, and rank the results. Every figure below is our own calculation from files downloaded on 22 October 2026.

**Ice pad areas in the North York Community Council Area, our calculation from the City of Toronto Indoor Ice Rinks and Outdoor Artificial Ice Rinks files (lengths and widths in feet, as the files give them)**

| Pad size (feet) | Area in square feet | Area in square metres (rounded) | North York pads this size |
|---|---|---|---|
| 180 by 135 (outdoor) | 24,300 | 2,258 | 1 |
| 180 by 120 (outdoor) | 21,600 | 2,007 | 2 |
| 185 by 85 (14 indoor, 4 outdoor) | 15,725 | 1,461 | 18 |
| 180 by 80 (indoor) | 14,400 | 1,338 | 1 |
| 289 by 49 (outdoor) | 14,161 | 1,316 | 1 |
| 160 by 73 (indoor) | 11,680 | 1,085 | 1 |
| 160 by 70 (indoor) | 11,200 | 1,041 | 1 |
| 132 by 58 (outdoor) | 7,656 | 711 | 1 |

The indoor file lists 20 North York rows and the outdoor file 13, one of them a skate trail rather than a pad. Seven rows have no length or width at all, so the table covers 26 pads. The indoor file explains that sizes follow &ldquo;Hockey Canada Line Markings diagrams approved by Hockey Canada&rdquo;, which is why 185 by 85 turns up so often: the file labels it Regular(185X85), and three North York indoor pads carry the label Undersized(less than 185X85). Here is the program a 10- or 11-year-old writes, with the two downloads renamed indoor.csv and outdoor.csv:

```
import csv

pads = []
for name, lcol, wcol in [("indoor.csv", "Pad Length", "Pad Width"),
                         ("outdoor.csv", "Pad Length (ft.)", "Pad Width (ft.)")]:
    for row in csv.DictReader(open(name, encoding="utf-8")):
        if row["Community Council Area"] == "North York":
            try:
                pads.append(int(row[lcol]) * int(row[wcol]))
            except ValueError:
                print("no size given, skipped")

pads.sort(reverse=True)
print(len(pads), "pads with a size")
print("biggest:", pads[0], "square feet")
print("in square metres:", round(pads[0] * 0.3048 ** 2))
```

### Two files, one loop

The files name their size columns differently, Pad Length in one and Pad Length (ft.) in the other. Rather than copy the loop twice, the child lists each file with its own column names and loops over that list.

### Blank sizes

Some rows hold an empty cell or the word None. Turning that into a number fails, and Python raises ValueError. The try and except lines catch it, print a note and move on: seven notes appear, and 26 areas remain.

### Rank and convert

Sorting from largest down puts 24,300 square feet first. One foot is exactly 0.3048 metres, so a square foot is 0.3048 squared square metres, and the program reports about 2,258.

### The longest is not the largest

Ask a class which pad is biggest and someone points at the 289-foot one. It is the longest in North York, but at 49 feet wide its area is 14,161 square feet, smaller than a standard 185 by 85 pad. Children who see this have learned something about area that a worksheet rarely shows them.

A second puzzle sits in the location numbers. The indoor North York rows use 18 different location IDs and the outdoor rows use 10, yet together there are only 27, because one site appears in both files with indoor ice and an outdoor pad. Counting places, not rows, means removing that repeat, and a Python set does it in one line. Other facts in the files are worth a class discussion: all 13 outdoor rows are marked as lit; 15 of the 20 indoor rows are run by the City&rsquo;s recreation division and 5 by an arena board, and the file notes that &ldquo;Eight arenas in Toronto are operated by volunteer Arena Boards of Management&rdquo;. The North York rows fall in wards 6, 8, 15, 16, 17 and 18, which the City&rsquo;s ward file names York Centre, Eglinton-Lawrence, Don Valley West, Don Valley East, Don Valley North and Willowdale. We use the Community Council Area as the City labels it and make no claim about older municipal boundaries. ## Problem of the Week and Courseware: free CEMC material for North York children

Waterloo&rsquo;s Centre for Education in Mathematics and Computing, known as CEMC, runs the school contests many Canadian students write, but for a Grade 3 or Grade 4 child its free weekly problems are often the better first step. Details are CEMC&rsquo;s, read on 22 October 2026.

**Two free CEMC resources, as CEMC describes them (University of Waterloo, read 22 October 2026)**

| Resource | Who it is for | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| Problem of the Week | E-mailed to educators for use with students from Grades 3 to 12 | New problems are sent every Thursday; solutions follow one week later |
| Courseware | Covers topics from Grades 4 to 12 mathematics and computer science | No registration needed; no certificate or credential is awarded |

### A teacher&rsquo;s resource

CEMC sends the weekly problem to educators, so the natural route is a North York teacher who already shares it. Ask whether the class receives it; many families never hear that it exists.

### One page, one week

A weekly problem suits a nine-year-old: one idea, a week to think, then a worked solution. We encourage children to write down a first guess before reading the answer, then compare.

### Contests come later

CEMC designs its contests for Grades 5 to 12, and says all students must participate through their school. For now, puzzles and code build the habits those contests reward.

We never reproduce CEMC problems in our classes. We write our own puzzles, label them as ours, and send families to [CEMC&rsquo;s Problem of the Week page](https://cemc.uwaterloo.ca/resources/potw) for the originals. A sample of ours, sized for Grade 4: a rink is 20 squares long and 9 squares wide; how many squares of ice is that, and how many more would a 21 by 9 rink have? Children solve it on paper, then write a two-line loop that prints the area of every rink from 15 to 25 squares long. Our [guide to free CEMC resources](/cemc-free-resources-guide) lists the rest, and the [Beaver Computing Challenge guide](/beaver-computing-challenge-guide) covers the first contest most children can write. CEMC and the University of Waterloo have no link with Modern Age Coders.

## Will AI make coding pointless for a North York child?

No. It makes knowing what the code should do more valuable, not less.

Take the rink project. A tool could write the area script in seconds, and it would happily rank the 289-foot pad near the top if asked for the longest. A child who multiplied the numbers by hand knows that long and large are different questions, and knows to check which one was asked. That judgement is the skill.

Our under-13 students do not use adult chatbots. They learn what an AI model is, how it learns from examples and why it can be confidently wrong, through activities a teacher leads. The rest of the time they reason through problems themselves, one step at a time, and test the answer instead of trusting it.

Ontario asks a Grade 4 child to read code and predict what it will do, and that habit of predicting and checking is exactly what working alongside AI requires. The longer argument is in [why children should still learn to code](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).

### What does my child learn about coding in Grade 4?

Ontario Grade 4 math asks children to write and change code with repeating and nested events, which means loops and loops inside loops. It sits inside the math program rather than in a separate subject.

### My child finds nested loops confusing. Can you help?

Yes, it is one of the most common sticking points we see. We draw the pattern on paper, act it out, then build it in Scratch one loop at a time until the inner and outer loops make sense.
