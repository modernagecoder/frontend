## What happens in a live online coding lesson?

Every lesson has the same rhythm, so children know what comes next. This is our plan for an hour with a group of nine year olds working in Scratch.

**One hour of a live online lesson, minute by minute (our lesson plan)**

| Minutes | What happens | Who drives the screen |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 5 | Hello, cameras on, a quick look back at last time's game | Mentor |
| 5 to 15 | One new idea shown on a tiny example, such as a timer that counts down | Mentor |
| 15 to 40 | Children add the idea to their own game; the mentor visits each screen in turn | Children |
| 40 to 50 | A bug hunt: something is broken on purpose, and the group finds it | Children, one at a time |
| 50 to 55 | Two or three children show their game and explain one block | Children |
| 55 to 60 | What to try before next time; a short note goes to parents | Mentor |

Our own lesson plan for a group class. One-to-one lessons follow the same order, with more time for the child's own project and less for showing.

### The child drives

For more than half the lesson the child's screen is the one being shared. The mentor can point, ask and suggest, but the child's hands are on the keyboard, which is why online lessons work at all.

### Nobody hides

In a video lesson a quiet child cannot sit at the back. The mentor calls on each child by name, checks every screen, and notices within a minute when a child is stuck.

### Nothing pre-recorded

There is no video to watch alone and no worksheet to finish without help. Each lesson happens once, live, with the mentor who taught the last one.

Our mentors are in India and teach only in English. On video that matters less than parents fear: the mentor shows each step on screen before saying it, keeps sentences short, and repeats in other words when a face looks puzzled. A child who answers in German is understood, and gently handed the English word.

## What does a child need at home for online coding classes?

Less than for most hobbies. Here is the checklist we send to families before the first lesson.

1. **A laptop or desktop computer with a real keyboard.** Scratch works on a tablet, but typing Python and sharing a screen are far easier on a computer. Any machine from the last few years with a current browser is enough.
2. **A headset with a microphone.** Cheap is fine. It keeps the mentor's voice clear and the child's answers easy to hear, and stops a younger sibling joining in.
3. **A steady internet connection.** The same connection that runs a family video call will run a lesson. Sitting close to the router helps more than a faster plan.
4. **A quiet corner and a glass of water.** A table, not a sofa. The same place every week turns the lesson into a habit.
5. **For children under about 8, a parent nearby.** Not to help with the code, just to help with logging in and finding the mouse in the first two or three lessons.
6. **No special software.** Scratch runs in the browser, and so can Python at the start. The mentor helps install anything else, live, when the child is ready.

### What we never ask for

Children do not need their own email address or any social media account to join a lesson. The class link goes to the parent.

Mentors do not ask children for personal details, photos of themselves or their school's name, and children do not share contact details in class.

### If something goes wrong

Connections drop. If a child falls out of the call, they rejoin with the same link and the mentor recaps what was missed.

If a lesson is lost to a broken connection or illness, we arrange a catch-up with the same mentor rather than leaving a gap.

## What time are online classes in Germany, and what changes in March?

German law fixes the clock in two lines. The Einheitenzeitgesetz says legal time is Central European Time, "die koordinierte Weltzeit unter Hinzufügung einer Stunde": UTC plus one hour. In summer it is UTC plus two. India keeps UTC+5:30 all year, so the gap between a child's clock and a mentor's clock changes twice a year. Our older children turn that into a program.

**Four example class times, in Germany and on the mentor's side**

| A child's clock in Germany | The mentor's clock this winter | The mentor's clock from 28 March 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Weekday 14:00 | 18:30 | 17:30 |
| Weekday 16:45 | 21:15 | 20:15 |
| Saturday 11:30 | 16:00 | 15:00 |
| Sunday 09:00 | 13:30 | 12:30 |

Under the Sommerzeitverordnung, summer time begins on the last Sunday in March at 2 Uhr, when the clocks jump to 3 Uhr, and ends on the last Sunday in October at 3 Uhr, when they go back to 2 Uhr. It ended on 25 October 2026 and begins on 28 March 2027. India does not change its clocks. The rows are examples; no class is fixed to them.

### The puzzle

A Saturday class sits at 11:30 in Germany. A child writes a program to tell the mentor's time: take the hours and minutes and add four and a half hours. It prints 16:00, which is correct today.

In April the program still prints 16:00. The mentor, meanwhile, is starting the class at 15:00. Nothing in the code is broken. The rule it uses is only true for half the year.

### The fix

The better program never adds hours by hand. It records the moment of the class in Germany's own time zone, Europe/Berlin, and asks Python to show that same moment in Asia/Kolkata.

Python's zoneinfo module knows the German clock-change rules, so the answer moves by itself on 28 March 2027.

**Version 1: add four and a half hours** (Python)
```python
def india_time(hour, minute):
    total = hour * 60 + minute + 4 * 60 + 30
    return f"{total // 60:02d}:{total % 60:02d}"

print("Saturday 11:30 in Germany is", india_time(11, 30), "in India")
```
It prints: Saturday 11:30 in Germany is 16:00 in India. Right for this winter, wrong from April.

**Version 2: convert the moment, not the clock reading** (Python)
```python
from datetime import datetime
from zoneinfo import ZoneInfo

germany = ZoneInfo("Europe/Berlin")
india = ZoneInfo("Asia/Kolkata")

for day in ["2027-03-20", "2027-03-27", "2027-04-03"]:
    lesson = datetime.fromisoformat(day + " 11:30").replace(tzinfo=germany)
    print(day, "11:30 in Germany is", lesson.astimezone(india).strftime("%H:%M"), "in India")
```
It prints 2027-03-20 11:30 in Germany is 16:00 in India, then the same for 27 March, then 2027-04-03 11:30 in Germany is 15:00 in India. The class did not move; the gap between the clocks did.

Then comes the part children remember. The Sommerzeitverordnung says that when summer time ends, "Die Stunde von 2 Uhr bis 3 Uhr erscheint dabei zweimal": the hour from 2 to 3 happens twice, and the law even names the two copies 2A and 2B. Version 3 walks through the night of 25 October 2026 in half-hour steps of world time and prints what a German clock showed.

**Version 3: the hour that happened twice** (Python)
```python
from datetime import timedelta, timezone

night = datetime(2026, 10, 25, 0, 0, tzinfo=timezone.utc)
for step in range(4):
    moment = night + timedelta(minutes=30 * step)
    print(moment.strftime("%H:%M UTC"), "is", moment.astimezone(germany).strftime("%H:%M %Z"), "in Germany")
```
After Version 2, it prints: 00:00 UTC is 02:00 CEST in Germany, 00:30 UTC is 02:30 CEST in Germany, 01:00 UTC is 02:00 CET in Germany and 01:30 UTC is 02:30 CET in Germany.

1. **Ages 7 to 9, on paper.** Draw two clocks, one for Germany and one for India. Move the German hand to 16:00 and work out the Indian clock. Now do it again for a day in May.
2. **Ages 10 to 12, in Python.** Run Version 1 for a date in April and explain why it is wrong before reading Version 2.
3. **Going further.** In March the hour from 2 to 3 never happens in Germany. Change Version 3 to walk through the night of 28 March 2027 and find the missing hour yourself.

## How a child settles into online lessons

Our own description of the first weeks, based on how most children respond. Some move faster, a few need longer, and that is fine.

**The first six weeks online (ours)**

| Week | What usually happens | What the mentor looks for |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Finding the mute button, learning names, a first moving sprite | Can the child hear clearly and share a screen? |
| 2 | Following along on their own screen without waiting to be told | Does the child try before asking? |
| 3 | Answering a question out loud in English, in a short sentence | Is the child relaxed on camera? |
| 4 | Fixing a small bug while the group watches | Does the child explain what they changed? |
| 5 to 6 | Showing a finished game and taking a question about it | Is the child proud of something they made? |

### For parents

The best sign is not a perfect game. It is the week your child stops asking you to sit with them.
If week 3 feels slow because of English, tell us. The mentor can slow down, or a one-to-one plan can take the pressure off.

### For children

If you cannot hear, say so straight away. Everyone has had a crackly microphone.
When you share your screen, the mentor sees your mistakes. That is the point: they can only help with what they can see.

## Online coding classes for kids in Germany: questions

### Are the classes in German?

No. Every lesson is live and in English; we do not offer German-language teaching. Mentors keep their English simple, show each step on screen, and adapt to each child's pace and accent, so a child with some school English can follow.

### Is an online class as good as one in a room?

For coding, often better. The child's own screen is shared, so the mentor sees exactly what they typed, and nobody can drift at the back. What matters is a live mentor and a small enough group, not the building.

### What does my child need at home?

A laptop or desktop with a keyboard, a headset with a microphone, a steady internet connection and a quiet table. No special software is needed at the start, and no account of the child's own.

### Can a six year old learn coding online?

Yes, with short lessons and a parent nearby for the first few weeks to help with logging in. Six year olds start in Scratch, where blocks are dragged rather than typed, which works well over video.

### When are the lessons in German time?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. A Saturday class at 11:30 in Germany is 16:00 for the mentor until 28 March 2027 and 15:00 after it.

### What happens when the clocks change in March?

Your class stays at the same German time. Germany moves to summer time on 28 March 2027 and India does not change, so the mentor starts an hour earlier in their own day. You do not need to do anything.

### Do classes stop in the school holidays?

No. Every Land has its own autumn, Christmas and Easter dates, so classes run all year. If you travel, tell us and we pause your place or arrange catch-ups.

### Are lessons recorded?

Lessons are live and happen once. Your child is not asked to learn from a recording; if a lesson is missed, the mentor arranges a catch-up instead.

### How is the fee shown and charged?

We show the monthly fee in euro, worked out with the European Central Bank reference rate. The checkout charges US dollars and your card provider converts them, so the statement can differ by a little. The first lesson is free.

### What if our internet drops in the middle of a class?

Your child rejoins with the same link and the mentor fills them in. If a whole lesson is lost, we arrange a catch-up with the same mentor.
