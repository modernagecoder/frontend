## Can a computer tell from a description, in English or French, whether a Fredericton amenity is open all year?

The City of Fredericton lists its recreation amenities, from playgrounds and ball diamonds to dog parks, with a description in English, a description in French and a season. Our students hide the season and train a naive Bayes classifier on the words. All accuracy figures here are our own results; the descriptions and seasons are the City&rsquo;s.

**Our naive Bayes results on City of Fredericton Recreation Amenities (283 amenities tagged Summer or Year-Round, fetched 22 October 2026; each one tested while the other 282 are used for training)**

| Model | What it reads | Share of amenities whose season it guesses right |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | Nothing: always says Year-Round, the commoner season | 54.1% |
| Naive Bayes, English | The words of the English description | 88.7% |
| Naive Bayes, French | The words of the French description | 85.2% |

This is the complete program, using only Python&rsquo;s standard library. It downloads the amenity list, turns each description into a set of lower-case words, and for every amenity in turn trains on all the others and guesses the left-out one, a method called leave-one-out that suits a small dataset. The add-one in the formula keeps a word the model has never seen with a season from zeroing out the whole calculation. Its output on 22 October 2026 is the three-row table above:

```
import json, math, re, urllib.request
from collections import Counter

URL = ("https://services2.arcgis.com/iLWAxhpxafhOza2U/arcgis/rest/services/"
       "Recreation_Amenities_view/FeatureServer/0/query?where=1%3D1"
       "&outFields=Description,DescriptionFR,Season&returnGeometry=false&f=json")
rows = [f["attributes"] for f in json.load(urllib.request.urlopen(URL))["features"]]
rows = [r for r in rows if r["Season"] in ("Summer", "Year-Round")]

def words(text):
    return set(re.findall(r"\w+", (text or "").lower()))

def train_and_guess(train, sample):
    seasons = Counter(season for _, season in train)
    counts = {s: Counter() for s in seasons}
    for bag, season in train:
        counts[season].update(bag)
    vocab = set().union(*(bag for bag, _ in train))
    def score(s):
        total = sum(counts[s].values())
        return math.log(seasons[s] / len(train)) + sum(
            math.log((counts[s][w] + 1) / (total + len(vocab))) for w in sample if w in vocab)
    return max(seasons, key=score)

def leave_one_out(field):
    data = [(words(r[field]), r["Season"]) for r in rows]
    right = sum(train_and_guess(data[:i] + data[i + 1:], bag) == season
                for i, (bag, season) in enumerate(data))
    return round(100 * right / len(data), 1)

top = Counter(r["Season"] for r in rows).most_common(1)[0]
print(len(rows), "amenities; always", repr(top[0]), "is right",
      round(100 * top[1] / len(rows), 1), "% of the time")
print("naive Bayes on English descriptions:", leave_one_out("Description"), "%")
print("naive Bayes on French descriptions:", leave_one_out("DescriptionFR"), "%")
```

### 1. Words as evidence

Naive Bayes treats each word as a small piece of evidence. In the English descriptions, &ldquo;diamond&rdquo; and &ldquo;ball&rdquo; appear 25 times each, always on summer amenities, while &ldquo;playground&rdquo; appears 57 times on year-round amenities and only twice on summer ones.

### 2. Surprising clues

The word &ldquo;drop&rdquo;, from &ldquo;drop-in&rdquo;, appears 31 times, every time on a summer amenity. The model has no idea what drop-in means; it only learned that the word travels with summer. That is the strength and the weakness of statistical learning.

### 3. English or French?

The French descriptions give 85.2 per cent against 88.7 in English. Students investigate why: French joins words with apostrophes, uses more forms of each word, and spreads the same evidence over more spellings. Handling that is real natural language processing.

### Where the model fails, and why that is useful

The English model makes 32 mistakes. Seven are summer parking areas called year-round and four are year-round parking areas called summer: a parking description says little about the season, and the City lists 28 parking rows as year-round and 11 as summer. Six more are community school gyms. When a model fails on one category again and again, the honest conclusion is that the text does not contain the answer, not that a cleverer model is needed.

A few notes on the data. Of the 295 amenities in the layer, 11 are tagged Winter and one has no season, so the model compares only Summer (130) and Year-Round (153). The descriptions were written by people, not generated, which is why identical amenities often share a template sentence and why the model does well on them. The dataset sits in the City&rsquo;s Fredericton Open Data catalogue, published under the Open Government License - City of Fredericton, version 1.0, which the City says is based on the Open Government Licence - Canada and whose grant extends to use &ldquo;including for commercial purposes&rdquo;. Source: City of Fredericton, Recreation Amenities, [City of Fredericton open data](https://data-fredericton.opendata.arcgis.com/). Nothing here carries the City&rsquo;s endorsement.

## Why a Fredericton teen should learn to debug alone before using AI to debug

Because New Brunswick allows the tool in class, the CCC forbids it, and only a student who can do both is ready for either.

An AI debugger is useful in the hands of someone who knows what a correct program should do. In the hands of someone who does not, it just moves errors around. A teen who has found a dozen bugs by reading their own output knows what to ask a tool, and knows when its answer is wrong.

So we keep a fixed order. Understand the problem, write the steps, predict the output, run it, find the mistakes, and only then, within the age rules, try a tool and compare its suggestion with your own. Students taught this way get more from AI, not less.

In the CCC the order is enforced for everyone: no AI in the editor, so every fix has to come from the student. The longer argument is in [a fuller case for coding in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
