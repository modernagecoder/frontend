Math tutor in Toronto, live and online

Where can a Toronto student get a real math tutor?

Modern Age Coders gives Toronto students live online math tutoring from Grade 1 to Grade 12, one to one or in a class of fifteen to twenty at one level. Teens usually join Ontario Grade 12 University Math (MHF4U and MCV4U) or Waterloo Math Contests: Gauss to Euclid; younger children join a maths course for their age. The first class is free.

A drop in a math mark usually has a cause older than the test that revealed it. A Grade 11 student stuck on transformations is often missing a Grade 9 idea about slope; a Grade 4 child who freezes on word problems may simply never have been asked to explain a step aloud. This page walks through Ontario math the way a Toronto student meets it, sends you to our detailed page for each course code, and then does something a tutoring advert rarely does: it teaches a full lesson. We take a City of Toronto file of ferry ticket counts and use it to show why the average of a lopsided set of numbers can mislead, which is the heart of Grade 12 data management and a skill every adult needs.

Two rules in the course records shape a Toronto timetable more than any tutor can. MCR3U lists the Grade 10 academic course as its prerequisite, and the ministry says MHF4U must be taken before or at the same time as MCV4U. A student who wants calculus in Grade 12 therefore needs functions secured in Grade 11, and a weak Grade 11 term shows up again a year later. That is why our first question to a Toronto family is about the course before the one the student is in now.

MDM4U, the data management course, is easy to overlook, and it is the one this page leans on most. It is where students meet distributions, measures of centre and spread, and the habit of asking where a number came from. The ferry lesson below is a taste of that course, taught at a level a strong Grade 9 student can follow.

Toronto data lesson

Why the average day on the Toronto Island ferry is not a typical day

The City of Toronto publishes ticket counts for the Island ferries as open data. We took every 2025 row, added up each day ourselves, and found a mean far above the median. The figures are our calculations from the City's file, not numbers the City publishes.

The dataset is called Toronto Island Ferry Ticket Counts, kept by the City's Fleet Services division. Its description says the counts are provided in 15 minute intervals and are updated hourly, and that the ferries operate year-round out of Jack Layton Ferry Terminal. Our download on 22 October 2026 held 284,387 rows, starting on 1 May 2015, each with a timestamp, a redemption count and a sales count. We used only redemptions, which are tickets used to board, and only calendar year 2025.

Look at the bottom row. The mean day in 2025 saw 4,191.9 redemptions, yet the median day saw only 1,690. Only 121 of the 364 days were above the mean at all, while 139 days had fewer than 1,000 redemptions. A short run of enormous summer days, 47 of them above 10,000, drags the mean upward. The median ignores how large those days were and simply finds the middle of the sorted list, so it describes an ordinary Toronto day far better.

The short program below reproduces our yearly figures. It reads the file line by line, keeps 2025, adds the 15-minute counts into one total per date, and prints the count of days, the mean, the median and the busiest date. It needs only Python 3 and the CSV in the same folder:import statistics from collections import defaultdict day_total = defaultdict(int) with open("toronto-island-ferry-ticket-counts.csv", encoding="utf-8") as file: next(file) # the first line holds the column names for line in file: _id, stamp, redeemed, sold = line.strip().split(",") if stamp.startswith("2025-"): day_total[stamp[:10]] += int(redeemed) values = sorted(day_total.values()) print("days:", len(values)) print("mean:", round(statistics.mean(values), 1)) print("median:", statistics.median(values)) print("busiest:", max(day_total, key=day_total.get), values[-1])

Quartiles tell the shape

Our lower quartile was 712.25 and our upper quartile 5,885.5. The gap from the median up to the upper quartile is more than four times the gap down to the lower quartile, the numerical signature of a right-skewed distribution.

Weekends in summer

In July 2025 the 23 weekdays averaged 9,881.9 redemptions and the 8 weekend days averaged 17,252.5. A student who reports one July average for the whole month has blended two different populations into one number.

A gap in the record

The file has rows for 364 dates in 2025, not 365: nothing is dated 26 January. Before computing anything, a careful student decides whether that day was quiet, closed or simply missing, and says which assumption the answer depends on.

A worked problem, ours

One busy Saturday, worked through as a Grade 12 data question

This is the kind of question we set a Toronto student after the ferry lesson. The data come from our sums for Saturday 12 July 2025, the busiest day in the 2025 file; the question and the solution are ours.

Question. What share of the day's redemptions fell between 10:00 and 13:59? Is it fair to say that the ferry was busiest at lunchtime?

Step 1, add the four hours. 2,030 + 2,657 + 3,158 + 3,469 = 11,314 redemptions. These are four of our own hourly sums, so adding them is legitimate; we are not adding someone else's rounded figures to manufacture a total.

Step 2, divide by the day. 11,314 divided by 24,341 is about 0.465, so roughly 46.5 percent of the day's boarding happened in four of the twenty-four hours.

Step 3, test the claim. The single busiest hour began at 13:00, not at noon, and the 15:00 hour was busier than the 14:00 hour, so the pattern is not a smooth peak. A student who writes “busiest around midday, with a second rise mid-afternoon” has read the table; one who writes “busiest at lunch” has guessed. In MDM4U terms, the conclusion has to match the evidence, not the expectation.

Try it yourself. Using the table, find the mean redemptions per hour from 9:00 to 16:59 and compare it with the median of those eight hours. Which is larger, and why is the difference small here when it was huge across the whole year?

Waterloo contests for Toronto students

Pascal, Cayley and Fermat: the February contests for Grades 9 to 11

The Centre for Education in Mathematics and Computing at the University of Waterloo runs a contest for each grade. For a Toronto high school student the first of the 2027 math contests is the Pascal, Cayley and Fermat sitting.

Twenty-five questions in an hour leaves under two and a half minutes each, and the later questions are written so that a direct calculation is too slow. What these contests reward is choosing a method before computing, which is the same judgement the ferry question asks for. CEMC puts it plainly on its contest pages: the contests are not tests or exams, and there is no passing or failing score.

Entry is through a school

No Toronto student can enter alone: CEMC takes entries only from schools. Where a student’s own school is not taking part, CEMC’s advice is to find another local school willing to let the student join.

Practise on the real archive

CEMC keeps the last ten years of past contests free, with no registration. Real past papers come from CEMC’s own archive; our lessons use problems of our own.

More on each contest

Our pages on the Pascal, Cayley and Fermat go deeper, and the full ladder from Gauss to Euclid sits on our Waterloo contests page.

Not connected with CEMC

CEMC’s rule is that “all students must participate through their school”. We are not connected with CEMC or the University of Waterloo. We never reproduce their past problems or solutions; our contest classes use original problems, labelled as ours.

Math and AI

Why a Toronto teen still needs to do the math when AI can

Because the answer an AI gives is only as good as the question, and only a person who understands the math knows whether the question was right.

Give a chatbot the ferry file and ask for the average day, and it will happily report the mean. It will not stop to ask whether the mean is the right summary for a set of numbers that is mostly quiet winter days with a few huge summer ones. That decision is the mathematics, and it belongs to the student.

Our teachers ask students to predict before they calculate, show the step that matters, and explain the result in a sentence. Older students may check work with tools, but marked school work stays the student's own, and the thinking is never handed over.

Ontario math asks students to reason, model and communicate; those are exactly the parts a tool cannot do for them. The longer argument is in our essay on why thinking skills outlast any tool.
