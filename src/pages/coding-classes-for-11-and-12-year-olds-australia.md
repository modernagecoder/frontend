# Coding classes for 11 and 12 year olds

Eleven and twelve are the bridge years. Most Australian children of this age are finishing primary school or starting Year 7, and their coding should be crossing a bridge too: from block programs to typed code, from making games for fun to understanding how computers actually work. Children of this age can think abstractly enough to enjoy ideas such as binary and data, and they are old enough to care about privacy and what they leave behind online. This page explains what eleven and twelve year olds can realistically achieve, works through one idea from the curriculum (how a computer turns a letter into ones and zeros), shows how it lines up with Years 5 to 8, and sets out the paths our students take from here.

## What eleven and twelve year olds can achieve

With a little experience, children of this age write real programs from a blank file, organise them into parts and explain how they work. Without experience, they learn faster than younger beginners because they can handle abstract ideas sooner.

## From letters to numbers to binary

Computers only store numbers, and underneath those, only ones and zeros. This lesson shows eleven and twelve year olds exactly how a word gets there, in two steps they can do with a pencil.

**Step one: letters to numbers.** Give each letter its position in the alphabet: A is 1, B is 2, and so on up to Z, which is 26. Puzzle fans call this the **A1Z26** code. The word CODE becomes 3, 15, 4, 5. It is exactly the activity ACARA suggests for Years 5 and 6: "converting letters in a message to numbers using their position in the alphabet".

**Step two: numbers to binary.** In binary, each digit is worth double the one to its right: 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. To write 15, children find the digits that add to it: 8 + 4 + 2 + 1, so 15 is 01111. Five digits can make every number from 0 up to 31, and 26 letters fit comfortably.

**The word CODE in A1Z26 and in five-digit binary (our worked example, checked in Python)**

| Letter | Position (A1Z26) | Binary, five digits | Made from |
|---|---|---|---|
| C | 3 | 00011 | 2 + 1 |
| O | 15 | 01111 | 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 |
| D | 4 | 00100 | 4 |
| E | 5 | 00101 | 4 + 1 |

### Why five digits, not four?

Four binary digits can only make 16 different values, 0 to 15, which is not enough for 26 letters: P to Z would have nowhere to go. Five digits make 32 values, which is enough with six to spare.

Children work this out by trying, and it is a lovely moment: they have just reasoned about storage the way a computer designer does.

### How real computers differ

Real computers use a longer code that covers capitals, small letters, digits, punctuation and symbols from every language, so they need many more digits per character.

In Year 7 and 8 the curriculum takes the next step, with students "counting in binary from 0 to 31" and learning that a byte of 8 bits can represent 0 to 255. Our five-digit code is that same 0 to 31.

### Write it in Python

Children then write a short Python function that turns any word into A1Z26 numbers, and another that turns numbers into five-digit binary.

### Send secret messages

Classmates swap binary messages and decode each other's, checking the program against their pencil work.

### Ask a better question

Is this a secret code? Not really: anyone who knows the rule can read it. That opens a good discussion about real encryption.

## How this matches Years 5 to 8 at school

Eleven and twelve year olds sit at the end of the Years 5 and 6 band and the start of the Years 7 and 8 band. Data and binary run through both.

**Data, binary and privacy in Digital Technologies, Australian Curriculum v9 (quoted)**

| Code | Students learn to |
|---|---|
| AC9TDI6K03 | "explain how digital systems represent all data using numbers" |
| AC9TDI6K04 | "explore how data can be represented by off and on states (zeros and ones in binary)" |
| AC9TDI6P10 | "explain the creation and permanence of their digital footprint and consider privacy when collecting user data" |
| AC9TDI8K04 | "explain how and why digital systems represent integers in binary" |

### Thinking more abstractly

The Year 6 band description says students "think in a more abstract way, exploring how on and off states and whole numbers can be used to represent data". That shift is exactly what makes binary fun at eleven instead of baffling.

It is also why Python suits this age: a typed program is itself an abstraction, a description of what should happen rather than a picture of it.

### Footprints and passphrases

The same band asks children to understand that what they post online can last. By the end of Year 6, the achievement standard says, students "identify their digital footprint and recognise its permanence".

Children are also expected to use different passphrases for different accounts (AC9TDI6P09). We reinforce both habits in every course, because this is the age when many children start using more apps.

## What parents of 11 and 12 year olds ask

### What coding should an 11 year old learn?

Most eleven year olds are ready for a text language such as Python, often after a short spell of Scratch if they are new to coding. Web pages and app making are good parallel projects.

### Is 12 too old to start coding?

Not at all. Twelve year olds usually learn quickly because they read, type and reason fluently, and many cover in a term what takes younger children a year.

### What is the A1Z26 code?

A1Z26 replaces each letter with its position in the alphabet, so A is 1 and Z is 26. It is a simple way to show how computers turn letters into numbers before storing them.

### What is binary?

Binary is a way of writing numbers using only 0 and 1, where each digit is worth double the one to its right. Computers store all data in binary because their circuits are either off or on.

### How many binary digits does a letter need?

For just the 26 capital letters, five binary digits are enough, because five digits make 32 values. Real computers use longer codes to cover small letters, digits, punctuation and other languages.

### Will coding help my child in Year 7?

Yes. Year 7 Digital Technologies expects programming in a general-purpose language, so a child who already writes Python arrives confident instead of starting from nothing.

### Should my child use AI tools to code?

Used carefully, they can explain errors and ideas. We teach children to understand every line they use, and we follow each tool's minimum-age terms in lessons.

### What does the curriculum expect by the end of Year 6?

Among other things, that students show how digital systems represent data, design algorithms with complex branching and iteration, implement them as visual programs with variables, and recognise the permanence of their digital footprint.

### How often should an 11 or 12 year old have lessons?

Weekly lessons suit most families. Twice a week helps a child preparing quickly for Year 7 or a particular project.
