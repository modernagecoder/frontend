## Who is maths olympiad coaching for in Peru?

For students who already like mathematics and want more than school gives them. It is not catch-up tutoring: the problems are harder, slower and stranger than classwork, and that is the point.

### Curious younger students

Children in upper primaria who finish class exercises fast and enjoy puzzles. They meet contest problems that need an idea rather than a formula, and learn to try small cases before guessing.

### ONEM and Canguro regulars

Secundaria students who already compete through their school and want to go further than the first round. They need proof, not just answers, because the later ONEM stages ask for full solutions.

### Selection-exam candidates

Strong students considering the national selection exams for international teams. Those exams give a few hard problems and hours to solve them, which calls for stamina and clear writing as much as knowledge.

Coaching is in English. For a student in an English-medium or IB school that is natural; for a student whose contests are in Spanish it adds practice in a second language, while the ideas stay the same. We are honest that national contests in Peru are set in Spanish, so we also make sure students can read a statement in Spanish precisely. ### The school route: ONEM and Canguro

ONEM is the largest. El Peruano reported that ONEM 2026 drew more than 1.7 million secundaria students from the first stage, and that 237 reached the national stage at the Huampaní centre on 11 October 2026. It runs from the school through UGEL and regional stages to the national final, at three levels by grade.

The ONEM bases add a detail coaches should notice: from the regional stage onwards, the papers move step by step towards the standards of international competitions. The first round rewards accuracy; the later rounds reward proof.

### The open route: selection exams

The national teams for the Ibero-American olympiad and the girls' Pan-American olympiad, PAGMO, are chosen by the Comisión de Olimpiadas of the Sociedad Matemática Peruana, which the IMO lists as Peru's official contact. Its rules let a school tutor, or a parent where the school does not, register a student.

Canguro, by contrast, is school-only: its Peru site says the first stage runs inside each school, so individual entry is not possible. If your child's school does not take part in a contest, ask the school first.

### What four hours for three problems means

By our division that is eighty minutes a problem, and a single complete solution can be worth more than three half-ideas. Students who have only done timed multiple choice tend to rush, find a pattern, and write the pattern down as if it were a proof.

Coaching for this format is mostly about patience: trying small cases on paper, naming what stays the same, writing the argument so a stranger could check it, and knowing when to switch problems.

## What is an invariant? A lesson with lamps, a proof and Python

An invariant is something a move can never change. It is one of the first ideas we teach olympiad students, because it turns "I tried for an hour and could not do it" into "it cannot be done, and here is why". The problem below is ours.

### The problem

Seven lamps stand in a row, all switched on. A move consists of choosing exactly two lamps and flipping both: on becomes off, off becomes on. Can you reach a position where all seven lamps are off?

Students start by trying, which is right. After a few minutes they notice that however they play, some lamp is always left on. Noticing is not proving, though.

### The proof

Look at the number of lamps that are on. A move changes it by +2, 0 or &minus;2, so its parity never changes. It starts at 7, an odd number, so it is odd after every move. All lamps off would mean 0 lamps on, an even number, so **no sequence of moves can reach it**.

Three sentences, and they work for seven lamps or seven thousand. That is the strength of an invariant: it does not care how many moves you try.

**Version 1: search every pattern reachable by flipping two** (Python)
```python
from itertools import combinations

N = 7                       # seven lamps, all switched on
start = (1,) * N

def reachable(k):
    # every pattern we can reach if each move switches exactly k lamps
    seen = {start}
    todo = [start]
    while todo:
        lamps = todo.pop()
        for group in combinations(range(N), k):
            new = list(lamps)
            for i in group:
                new[i] = 1 - new[i]
            new = tuple(new)
            if new not in seen:
                seen.add(new)
                todo.append(new)
    return seen

two = reachable(2)
print("Patterns reachable, switching 2:", len(two))
print("All off reachable?", (0,) * N in two)
print("Lamps on, in every reachable pattern:", sorted({sum(p) for p in two}))
```
It prints Patterns reachable, switching 2: 64, All off reachable? False and Lamps on, in every reachable pattern: [1, 3, 5, 7]. Exactly half of the 128 possible patterns, and only those with an odd number of lamps on.

**Version 2: change the rule to flipping three** (Python)
```python
three = reachable(3)
print("Patterns reachable, switching 3:", len(three))
print("All off reachable?", (0,) * N in three)
```
Run after Version 1, it prints Patterns reachable, switching 3: 128 and All off reachable? True. A move of three changes the count by an odd number, so parity is no longer kept, and every pattern can be reached.

We use the program to check the proof, never to replace it. The search visits all 128 patterns of seven lamps in a blink, but for a thousand lamps there are more patterns than atoms in the observable universe and no computer could list them. The parity argument covers every size at once. In an olympiad paper, **the search would earn nothing and the proof would earn everything**; in a classroom, the search is how a student learns to trust the proof.

Version 2 teaches the second half of the idea: an invariant belongs to a rule, not to a puzzle. Change the move and the invariant may vanish. Good olympiad students test their invariant against the exact wording of the problem before they rely on it.

## Three invariant problems to try (ours, with answers)

All three problems were written by us for this page; none is taken from ONEM, Canguro or any other contest. Each answer was checked by an exhaustive search in Python, and each has a short proof that does not need a computer.

1. **Differences on a board.** The numbers 1 to 10 are written on a board. A move erases two numbers and writes their difference (larger minus smaller). After nine moves one number is left. Can it be 0?
2. **Odd handshakes.** At a meeting of five students, could every student shake hands with an odd number of the others? (Two people shake hands at most once.)
3. **Beetles on a grid.** A 3 by 3 grid has a beetle on each of its nine squares. At a whistle every beetle moves to a square sharing a side with its own. Must some square end up empty?

### Answers

1. No. The sum 1 + 2 + ... + 10 = 55 is odd, and replacing a and b with their difference keeps the parity of the total, because a + b and a &minus; b are both even or both odd. The last number is odd. Our search printed P1 possible last numbers: [1, 3, 5, 7, 9] zero? False.

2. No. Each handshake adds 2 to the total of everyone's counts, so that total is even; five odd numbers add up to an odd number. Our search over all 1,024 ways to shake hands printed P2 graphs with every degree odd: 0.

### Answer 3, and why colouring helps

3. Yes. Colour the grid like a chessboard: five squares of one colour, four of the other. Every beetle changes colour when it moves, so the five beetles on the first colour all land on the four squares of the second. Two must share, and a square is left empty.

Our search over every possible set of moves printed P3 ways to leave no square empty: 0. Colouring is an invariant in disguise: it names what a move always does, here "change colour", and counts what that forces.

## Will coaching teach my child to think, or just to recognise tricks?

To think. Tricks run out on the first unfamiliar problem; reasoning does not. We teach students to explore, conjecture, prove and write, in that order.

### Small cases first

Before anything clever, try two lamps, three lamps, four. Patterns found by hand are the raw material of every proof, and they are found faster than students expect.

### Written proof

Every solution is written in full sentences that another student could check. In English, which is good practice for the many students heading to universities where proofs are written that way.

### No AI answers

Olympiads test the student, so coaching does too. Peru's own computing olympiad, OPI, run by UTEC and free for all secundaria students, bans generative AI for solving problems in its 2025 rules, and we work the same way.

Children under 18 do not use adult AI chatbot accounts in our classes; tools such as Claude are for adults only, and ChatGPT needs a parent's permission under 18. Our problems come from our mentors, and organisers' past papers stay on the organisers' own sites, where students can read them as the organisers intend.

## Maths olympiad coaching in Peru: questions and answers

### Are the classes in Spanish?

No. All coaching is live, online and in English. Mentors speak clearly and at the student's pace, and they write every key term on screen. Because national contests in Peru are in Spanish, we also practise reading problems in Spanish carefully, but the teaching itself is in English.

### Can my child enter ONEM or the Canguro through you?

No. ONEM and the Canguro Matemático are entered through the school; the Canguro site says individual entry is not possible. We are not connected with MINEDU, Aceros Arequipa or Binaria Matemática. We prepare students for the mathematics; the school handles entry.

Through selection exams run by the Comisión de Olimpiadas of the Sociedad Matemática Peruana. In 2026 the Ibero team was chosen from two exams of three problems in four hours each, and a school tutor or a parent could register a student. Check the commission's site for each year's rules.

### Do you use past ONEM or Canguro problems?

No. Every problem we teach with is written by our mentors. Organisers publish their own past papers on their own sites, and students are welcome to read them there; we do not copy or reproduce them.

### What age should my child start?

Around 8 to 10 for puzzle-style contests such as the early Canguro levels, which start in 3° de primaria. Proof-based coaching suits most students from about 12 or 13. A strong student can start at any point; the stages table shows where they would begin.

### When would classes be in Peru time?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. Peru is on UTC minus 5 all year and India on UTC plus 5:30, so 09:00 in Lima is 19:30 in India.

No, and nobody honestly can. Results depend on the student, the year's problems and the other competitors. Peru's IMO team points ranged from 122 to 173 between 2022 and 2026. What we promise is careful teaching of the ideas that olympiads reward.

### Does coding help with olympiad maths?

It helps with checking, as the lamps lesson shows, and it opens the computing olympiad, OPI, which UTEC runs free for secundaria students. It never replaces proof. Students who enjoy both often take our problem-solving and algorithms course alongside olympiad maths.

No. We are an independent online school with mentors in India, not connected with MINEDU, UNI, PUCP, UTEC, the Sociedad Matemática Peruana or the IMO. Students who finish a course receive our own certificate of completion, not a contest award.
