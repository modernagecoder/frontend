## Darsait has two distinct Indian schools

Indian School Muscat says a land grant in Darsait enabled its establishment. The school began with 135 children in 1975 and reports more than 9,200 students. It lists Science, Commerce and Humanities. Its official history makes it a strong local reference, but school size does not identify an individual learner's coding level.

Indian School Darsait is not another name for Indian School Muscat. The Indian Schools Oman profile says it began in 1992 with 127 students as a sister concern, became independent in 1994 and moved to its present location on 6 July 1998. The profile lists CBSE affiliation, primary and secondary blocks, and Science and Commerce streams.

Muscat Municipality also names Al Waleed Basic School for grades 5 to 9 and Faisal bin Turki School for grades 5 to 10 in a Muttrah environmental-health programme. Another Greater Muttrah account lists Darsait School for Girls, Al Tawfik School, Hassan bin Thabit School and Rashid bin Al Nadar School among visited schools. The sources confirm the institutions without inviting guesses about unreported enrolment or computing provision.

## The Muttrah response-gap project

The NCSI Household Expenditure and Income Survey methodology publishes a real response table for Muscat. The historical 2010 report separates the selected sample, locked units, net sample, responding households and multiple kinds of non-response. A learner imports those categories and reproduces the published response calculation with the survey year clearly labelled.

The local extension uses the eight Muttrah districts named by the Municipal Council. Students design a privacy-safe questionnaire about a modest question, such as which recurring lesson window a household could protect. Invitations are allocated across all eight districts, and the response log keeps invited, contacted, completed, declined and unreachable as different states. No names, addresses or personal identities enter the analysis.

The confident wrong answer summarises only the completed responses and calls them Muttrah's preference. Yet households available during the contact window, comfortable replying online or already interested in education may complete at different rates. The people who answered are not equivalent to everyone asked.

This is non-response bias, not sampling bias. The invitation frame can contain all intended districts and still yield an unrepresentative response set. A high response percentage may reduce the concern, but it cannot prove that missing households share the same preference.

| Step | Student action | Reason |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Load the NCSI response categories and retain the survey year | A historic table stays historically labelled |
| 2 | Allocate invitations across eight official districts | The initial frame covers the intended place |
| 3 | Record every contact outcome separately | Silence is never coded as agreement |
| 4 | Compare response rates by district, time and contact mode | Missing replies become visible |
| 5 | Test best-case and worst-case non-response assumptions | The conclusion receives honest bounds |
| 6 | Publish method and aggregate results only | The work stays reproducible and privacy-safe |
