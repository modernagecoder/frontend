Digital Technologies curriculum help

Digital Technologies is the part of the Australian Curriculum where children learn how computers represent information, how algorithms are designed, and how programs are written and tested. It runs from the first year of school to Year 10, and by the end it expects real programming in a general-purpose language and some thinking about security and privacy. Many parents never studied anything like it. This page sets out how Version 9.0 is organised, quotes what ACARA says students should manage at the end of each band, and works one example from the Years 7 to 8 content so you can see the level involved. We are an online school teaching coding and maths; we are not part of ACARA and we do not write anyone's school assessment.

In short

Digital Technologies is the Australian Curriculum subject about computing. Version 9.0 has two strands, Knowledge and understanding and Processes and production skills, each split into sub-strands, and it is written in bands from Foundation to Year 10. Students move from following simple algorithms to writing visual programs by Year 6, then a general-purpose language in Years 7 and 8 and an object-oriented one by Year 10.

How the subject is organised

Two strands, several sub-strands, six bands

Every content description in Digital Technologies sits in a strand, a sub-strand and a band. Knowing where a task sits tells you what it is really asking for.

The bands are Foundation, then Years 1 to 2, 3 to 4, 5 to 6, 7 to 8 and 9 to 10. Each band has a band level description, an achievement standard and a set of content descriptions with codes such as AC9TDI8P13, where the digits after TDI name the year at the end of the band and the P or K says which strand it belongs to.

The codes are useful in conversations with a teacher. If a report says your child is working towards a standard in data representation, the code points to exactly which skill, and the elaborations under it show the kind of task schools use.

Not every sub-strand appears in every band. In the early years, for example, the Foundation content is short: recognising digital systems, representing data as objects, pictures and symbols, and identifying data that is personal and owned by the child. The data sub-strand under Processes and production skills appears as the bands rise.

Your state or territory decides how the curriculum is taught. Some publish their own syllabuses built from it, and schools choose how much time it gets. What follows is the national content, the shared reference point underneath those choices.

The two big shifts

The first shift happens between Year 6 and Year 8. Up to Year 6 the curriculum asks for visual programs, the block-based coding most primary schools use. The Years 7 to 8 band description says students should implement algorithms "in a general-purpose programming language", and the Year 8 standard repeats it: students "design and trace algorithms and implement them in a general-purpose programming language."

The second shift comes in Years 9 to 10, where the Year 10 standard adds programs "including in an object-oriented programming language" and asks students to "model cyber security threats and explore a vulnerability." That is real software engineering vocabulary, and students who meet it cold often find it the hardest year.

What does not change

Every band asks students to define problems before solving them and to judge solutions afterwards. Even in Years 1 to 2, the standard begins with showing how a digital solution meets a need for known users.

Privacy and security also run the whole way through, from identifying personal data in Foundation to recorded usernames and passwords in Years 1 to 2, memorised passwords in Years 3 to 4, unique passphrases in Years 5 to 6, and authentication and threat models in secondary school.

A worked example from Years 7 to 8

Why a stolen password is not enough: AC9TDI8P13 in numbers

One Years 7 to 8 content description asks students to "explain how multi-factor authentication protects an account when the password is compromised and identify phishing and other cyber security threats". Here is how we teach it, with arithmetic a Year 8 student can follow.

ACARA's elaboration describes multi-factor authentication as preventing "unauthorised access by prompting the account owner for a token or single-use password", and gives the example of a funds transfer that needs "a one-time password received via SMS" as well as a login.

The point students need to grasp is that the second factor is something the attacker does not have, even after a password leak. We make that concrete by counting. A six-digit one-time password can be any number from 000000 to 999999, so there are 1,000,000 possible codes, and a fresh one is issued each time.

Suppose an attacker has the password and a site allows five guesses at the code before locking the attempt. The chance of guessing right is at most 5 in 1,000,000, or 0.0005 per cent. Without the second factor, the leaked password alone gets them in every time.

Students then test the idea against the phishing half of the description. ACARA's elaboration lists techniques such as "using an email address from an unofficial domain when pretending to be an online retailer". If a fake page asks for the code as well as the password, the maths stops protecting you, which is why the lesson ends with spotting the fake page rather than trusting the numbers.

How hard is it to guess the second factor? Our arithmetic, for teaching
Second factorPossible valuesChance with 5 guesses
None (password only, already stolen)Not applicableCertain
4-digit code10,0005 in 10,000 (0.05 per cent)
6-digit code1,000,0005 in 1,000,000 (0.0005 per cent)
6-digit code given away on a phishing page1,000,000Certain: the attacker was handed it

The last row is the reason the content description pairs authentication with phishing. A good lesson does not stop at "turn on two-factor". It shows why it works, then shows exactly how people defeat it, which is the habit of thinking about an attacker that the Years 9 to 10 band later formalises as a threat model.

Helping at home

What families can usefully do in each band

You do not need to code to help. Most of the support a child needs is conversation about problems, steps and checking.

Foundation to Year 2

Talk through everyday algorithms: getting dressed, a recipe, the route to school. Ask what happens if a step is missed or swapped. That is the sequencing and branching the early standards describe.

Years 3 to 6

Ask to see the visual program, then ask your child to predict what it will do before pressing run. Predicting first is the habit that later becomes tracing and debugging.

Years 7 to 10

Ask how they tested it. A program that works on one example is not finished. The Years 9 to 10 content asks students to "validate algorithms and programs by comparing their output against a range of test cases".

Security is the area where family habits matter most. Content descriptions from Years 1 to 2 onwards deal with usernames, passwords and personal data. Setting up a passphrase together, or turning on a second factor on a shared account, makes the classroom content real.

The Years 9 to 10 band also asks students to "investigate how hardware and software manage, control and secure access to data in networked digital systems" (AC9TDI10K01) and to apply the Australian Privacy Principles to the data that services collect (AC9TDI10P14). Reading a privacy policy together is a genuine curriculum activity.

When a teenager gets stuck on a project, the most useful question is about the problem, not the code: who is it for, and how will they know it works? The curriculum calls these user stories and design criteria, and most stalled projects are missing one or the other.

If the jump to text code feels steep

Most of the difficulty is typing and syntax, not ideas. Students who could build a working block program already understand loops and conditions; they need time with a text editor.

Short daily practice beats occasional long sessions while that adjustment happens.

Questions about Digital Technologies

What Australian families ask

What is Digital Technologies in the Australian Curriculum?

It is the computing subject within the Technologies learning area. It teaches how digital systems work, how data is represented and analysed, and how to design, build and evaluate digital solutions, including programs.

What is the difference between the two strands?

Knowledge and understanding covers digital systems and data representation. Processes and production skills covers what students do: acquiring and analysing data, defining problems, designing, programming, evaluating, collaborating, and privacy and security.

When does text-based coding start?

Up to Year 6 the content asks for visual programs. The Years 7 to 8 band asks students to implement algorithms in a general-purpose programming language, and Years 9 to 10 add object-oriented programming.

What does a code like AC9TDI8P13 mean?

AC9 marks Australian Curriculum Version 9.0, TDI marks Technologies: Digital Technologies, the number is the year at the end of the band, P means Processes and production skills (K means Knowledge and understanding), and the last digits number the content description.

Is Digital Technologies taught the same way in every state?

No. The national curriculum is the shared reference, but each state and territory decides how it is implemented, and some publish their own syllabuses built on it.

What is multi-factor authentication, in curriculum terms?

A login that asks for more than a password, such as a one-time code. The Years 7 to 8 content asks students to explain how it protects an account when the password is compromised.

Can you help with a school project?

We teach the skills a project needs, such as defining the problem, designing the algorithm and testing. The project itself stays the student's own work under the school's rules.

Which language do you teach first?

For most students in Years 7 and above we start with Python. Younger students begin with visual programming, as the curriculum does.

Are your classes linked to ACARA?

No. ACARA writes the curriculum; we are an independent online school whose lessons build the same skills.

What time would lessons be for us?

We agree a weekly slot after the trial class, usually after school in your state. Every Australian state and territory is ahead of India time, by between two and a half and five and a half hours depending on the state and the season.
