TMUA preparation: the Test of Mathematics for University Admission

The TMUA looks like a maths test and is half a logic test. Its first paper asks you to apply school mathematics in unfamiliar ways; its second, Mathematical Reasoning, asks whether a statement follows, whether a condition is necessary or sufficient, and where an argument breaks. Both papers are 75 minutes for 20 multiple-choice questions, with no calculator. Cambridge, Imperial, Oxford and Warwick use it, among others, for mathematics, computer science and some economics courses. This page sets out the format and windows for 2027 entry from those universities' own admissions pages, explains how the scaled score works, works through the kind of logic Paper 2 tests with examples of our own, and is honest about how much preparation the universities say you need.

In short

The TMUA (Test of Mathematics for University Admission) is a computer-based admissions test run by UAT-UK at Pearson VUE centres. It has two papers, Applications of Mathematical Knowledge and Mathematical Reasoning, each with 20 multiple-choice questions in 75 minutes, no calculator, and no marks lost for wrong answers. Imperial lists seven universities that use it for some courses: Imperial, Cambridge, Durham, LSE, UCL, Oxford and Warwick. Windows for 2027 entry are 12 to 16 October 2026 and 4 to 8 January 2027. The two papers

What is on the TMUA, and how long is it?

Two papers of 75 minutes, each with 20 multiple-choice questions, two and a half hours in total. Imperial and Warwick describe Paper 1 as applying mathematics in new situations and Paper 2 as reasoning and simple ideas from elementary logic.

The content is school mathematics

Oxford's Mathematical Institute summarises the content specification as mathematics "usually taught at AS-level or Higher Level GCSE, plus a small number of topics from the full A-level Mathematics". There is no Further Maths in it, which is why the test can be set to applicants from many school systems.

What makes it hard is the setting. A Paper 1 question rarely announces its topic; a student has to recognise that a question about areas is really about a quadratic, or that a sequence question is really about inequalities.

Paper 2 is the unusual one

The second section of the specification applies only to Paper 2, Oxford says, and covers logic and proof: what a statement means, what follows from it, and how arguments go wrong. Most A Level courses touch these ideas lightly, if at all.

Cambridge and Warwick both recommend reading the specification and the notes on Logic and Proof that UAT-UK publishes. For most applicants, those notes are the single most useful document, and they are free.

Sit it once

A student applying to several of these universities takes the test once per cycle. Imperial says "Only your first test score will be considered", and Warwick says it accepts the results from one test in each admissions cycle. A student who sits in October cannot sit again in January to improve.

Because Oxford and Cambridge require the October window, anyone applying to either must sit in October, and that score is the one every other university sees too.

Read the course, not the list

Warwick shows why. Computer Science requires the TMUA; Mathematics accepts TMUA or STEP; Economics and Statistics make it optional, with Warwick saying applicants who skip it are not at a disadvantage for Statistics. The same university, four different rules.

For computer science applicants, note that Warwick excludes Computer Systems Engineering (G406, G408) from the requirement. Course codes matter, and each university lists them.

Paper 2, ours

How do you answer TMUA Paper 2 logic questions?

By reading the statement before doing any mathematics. Most Paper 2 errors are not calculation errors; they are a converse taken for the original, a condition called sufficient when it is only necessary, or a proof accepted because its conclusion is true. These four examples are ours, written for this page.

Converse and contrapositive

Take "if n is a multiple of 6, then n is even". True. Its converse, "if n is even, then n is a multiple of 6", is false: n = 4. Its contrapositive, "if n is odd, then n is not a multiple of 6", is true, and always agrees with the original.

Necessary or sufficient

Is x > 2 sufficient for x2 > 4? Yes: any x above 2 has a square above 4. Is it necessary? No: x = −3 gives x2 = 9 without x > 2. The exact condition is x > 2 or x < −2.

Negating "for all"

The negation of "every student passed some test" is not "no student passed any test". It is "some student passed no test". Swap each quantifier and negate what is left.

Our flawed proof. Claim: x2 ≥ x for every real number x. "Proof": x2 − x = x(x − 1). If x ≥ 1, both factors are at least zero, so the product is too. If x ≤ 0, both factors are at most zero, so the product is at least zero. Therefore x2 ≥ x always.

Where it fails

Each case is argued correctly. The error is in what is missing: the cases x ≥ 1 and x ≤ 0 do not cover the numbers strictly between 0 and 1. There, x is positive and x − 1 is negative, so the product is negative. Try x = 1/2: x2 = 1/4, which is less than 1/2. The claim is false.

Paper 2 questions of this kind usually offer options such as "the proof is correct", "the error is in line 2" or "the error is that a case is omitted". Reading each line for truth will not find this mistake; asking "have all possibilities been covered?" will.

A Paper 1 contrast

Paper 1 rewards spotting structure quickly. Our example: the roots of x2 − 6x + k = 0 differ by 2. Find k. Call the roots r and r + 2; their sum is 6, so 2r + 2 = 6 and r = 2. The roots are 2 and 4 and k is their product, 8. No quadratic formula needed.

In the test, that is a forty-second question if the student thinks about sum and product first and a four-minute one if they do not. The time saved goes to a Paper 2 question that needs it.

Scores and preparation

How is the TMUA scored, and what is a good score?

Scores run from 1 to 9 to one decimal place, with no pass mark and no grade boundaries. Universities read them alongside the rest of the application, and only Warwick, among the pages we read, has published anything like a figure.

What the universities say about preparing

Cambridge and Warwick use the same sentence, and it belongs on any page selling TMUA classes: "there is no need to take a preparation course or to do much preparation beyond familiarising yourself with the test format and style of questions." Both recommend the specification and the Logic and Proof notes.

There is also free structured help. Warwick points to MEI, an education charity, which runs a free online TMUA course for Year 13 students at state-funded schools, academies and colleges.

Where a class still helps

A student whose school course never mentioned contrapositives or necessary conditions may find Paper 2 genuinely new, and some international curricula reach AS-level topics in a different order. A few weeks of teaching can close that gap faster than reading alone.

Some students simply work better with a teacher and a group. That is a fair reason too, provided nobody pretends the test needs coaching to pass. It has no pass mark, and we promise no score.

Score statements from Imperial's Understanding your ESAT and TMUA scores page and Warwick's and Cambridge's admissions pages, read 31 October 2026. Warwick's 5.0 describes its own 2025 offers and is not a requirement elsewhere.

If January is the window

Booking is open until 21 December 2026. Spend the first fortnight on the Logic and Proof notes and the rest on timed practice papers.

Use Year 12 to make A Level pure fluent and to learn logic properly. Neither needs test papers yet.

TMUA questions

What TMUA candidates and parents ask

What is the TMUA?

The Test of Mathematics for University Admission, a computer-based admissions test administered by UAT-UK at Pearson VUE centres. Universities use it for certain mathematics, computer science and economics courses.

How long is the TMUA?

Two and a half hours: two papers, each of 75 minutes with 20 multiple-choice questions. Paper 1 is Applications of Mathematical Knowledge and Paper 2 is Mathematical Reasoning.

When is the TMUA for 2027 entry?

Imperial lists two windows: 12 to 16 October 2026 and 4 to 8 January 2027. Booking for January opened on 28 October 2026 and closes on 21 December 2026 at 18.00 UK time.

Which universities use the TMUA?

Imperial lists Imperial, Cambridge, Durham, LSE, UCL, Oxford and Warwick. Each decides which courses need it, so check the course page; at Warwick, for example, it is required for Computer Science and optional for Economics.

Can I use a calculator in the TMUA?

No. Cambridge and Warwick say you will not be able to use a calculator or dictionary, and Oxford says no calculators are permitted for the test.

Is there negative marking in the TMUA?

No. Cambridge and Imperial say you do not lose marks for wrong answers, so it is worth answering every question, even with a guess.

What is a good TMUA score?

Scores run from 1 to 9 to one decimal place and there is no pass mark. Warwick says most of its 2025-entry Mathematics offers went to applicants scoring 5.0 or above, but that figure is Warwick's own and not a target elsewhere.

Can I take the TMUA twice?

Only once per admissions cycle counts. Imperial says only your first test score will be considered, and Oxford and Cambridge applicants must sit the October window.

Do I need a TMUA tutor?

Cambridge and Warwick say there is no need for a preparation course beyond knowing the format and question style. A class helps most when logic and proof are new, or when a student wants a teacher to practise with.

What days are classes on?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

Are you connected with UAT-UK?

No. Our classes are entirely separate from UAT-UK, Pearson VUE and every university on this page. Students register and book the test themselves.
