ACSL competition preparation

The American Computer Science League runs four computer science contests across the school year, with an invitational Finals in May. Unlike most coding contests, half of each ACSL test is pencil-and-paper computer science: number bases, Boolean algebra, graphs, recursion traced by hand. This guide is built from the league’s own 2026-27 pages and gives the divisions, the contest windows, the topics in each contest, how HackerRank scores the programming problem, a practice set we wrote ourselves with answers, and a 13-week plan.

In short

ACSL, the American Computer Science League, is a team contest for K-12 students run in four online contests each season. Every contest has a short answer test on computer science topics, and the Junior, Intermediate and Senior divisions add a programming problem in Python, C++ or Java solved within 72 hours. For 2026-27, Contest #1 opens October 19, 2026, team registration closes December 31, 2026, and the Finals are on May 29, 2027. Modern Age Coders teaches this live online for US students, in a batch or one to one, and the first class is free.

Why families choose it

ACSL tests computer science, not only coding speed. A student who has never written a line of Java can still score on number systems and Boolean algebra, which makes it a gentle first contest for a curious middle schooler.

Advisors pick when to run each contest within a long window, so a school can fit it around exams and holidays instead of a single national test day.

How it differs from USACO

USACO is individual and almost entirely programming. ACSL is team based, half theory, and spread over four contests with short tests.

A student can do both in one year: ACSL builds the theory and hand tracing, and that discipline helps in any contest that uses hidden test data.

Source: acsl.org and its Advisor Guide, read 17 October 2026. The league sets its own rules and fees; Modern Age Coders is independent of ACSL and teaches the skills only.

Picking between Junior and Intermediate

The Divisions page is firm that "No student beyond grade 9 may compete in the Junior Division." A 9th grader new to programming can choose Junior; a confident 8th grader who already writes Python functions may get more from Intermediate.

The short problems in Intermediate and Senior share the same topics, so a student who moves up a division next year is not starting over.

Teams, rosters and scores

A team counts either its top 5 scores or its top 3 in each contest. A 5-score team has up to 10 students on its roster, a 3-score team up to 6, and a school may register several teams in one division.

A student belongs to one team only, and after Contest #1 cannot switch. Plan rosters before October so nobody is stuck in the wrong division.

Windows overlap on purpose

Contest #2 opens before Contest #1 closes, and the same is true down the list. That gives advisors room, but it also tempts teams to leave everything until the last weeks.

Our advice is to sit each contest early in its window, once the topics have been taught, and keep the spare weeks for anyone who was sick on the planned day.

Late starters

Because registration runs to December 31, 2026 and the Contest #1 window to January 10, 2027, a team that forms in December can still take all four contests this season.

The topics for each contest are published in advance, so a late team can study Contest #1 topics over the winter break and sit it in early January.

The topics that cost the most points

The topics school rarely covers, bit-string flicking, LISP and assembly language, are the ones a student is least likely to have met before. Each is learnable in a few lessons, but only with drill, because the notation is unfamiliar.

Recursive functions and What Does This Program Do? reward careful tracing on paper. Students who try to do them in their head under a 30-minute limit make small slips.

Where the official material lives

Each topic on the Study Materials page links to the ACSL Wiki with explanations, videos and sample problems, and there is a full sample contest from a past year for every division.

ACSL also sells downloadable collections of previous contests to advisors. Past papers are the strongest practice there is, because the style of question repeats.

Contest day

How the HackerRank tests are scored

Every contest is taken online through links HackerRank emails to each student. The Student Guide explains the details that change how you should work.

  1. The email arrives. The advisor sends the invitation; Junior, Intermediate and Senior students receive a second link for the programming part. Each link is personal and must not be shared.
  2. Sign the honesty declaration. HackerRank checks programs for plagiarism, and "Students found to be collaborating will score 0 points for that test."
  3. Short problems. All are multiple choice. The test submits itself when time runs out, and once submitted the student cannot see the answers again.
  4. The programming problem. Boilerplate already handles input and output. In ACSL’s words, "Your job will be to complete a function with specified parameters, and return a value in the correct format."
  5. Hidden tests. There are up to 12 test cases. The last 6 are hidden, and each hidden case passed is worth one point. The last run before time ends is the score.
  6. Results. Scores appear on the ACSL Leaderboard within 2 hours.

What hidden tests mean for practice

Passing the six visible cases proves very little. Hidden cases often probe edges: an empty string, a zero, the largest input allowed. We teach students to write three extra test inputs of their own before they press run.

The return format is checked exactly. A space too many or a missing leading zero fails a case that the logic got right.

Using the 72 hours well

Seventy-two hours is generous, and that is the trap: students start late and rush. A better rhythm is to read the problem on day one, write a solution on day two, and test edge cases on day three.

Try the HackerRank Sample Test before Contest #1 so the editor is familiar on the day that counts.

Why both by hand and by machine? In the contest there is no time to write a program for a short answer question, and in the programming problem there is no partial credit for a correct idea with a wrong return format. Students need the speed of the first and the precision of the second.

For parents

An advisor must register the team, and ACSL allows that to be a parent with computer science experience, so homeschool groups can take part.

Steady practice across the season matters more than a burst in May, because Finals invitations are based on scores from the regular season.

For students

Keep a notebook of every mistake by topic. The pattern shows you which two topics to drill next.

Always write down the answer format before you code. Hidden tests check it exactly.

ACSL: common questions

What is ACSL?

The American Computer Science League organises computer science and programming contests for K-12 schools, organizations and local groups. The 2026-27 season is its 49th year of continuous operation.

When is ACSL Contest #1 in 2026-27?

Its window runs from October 19, 2026 to 11:59 p.m. Eastern on Sunday, January 10, 2027, and the advisor picks the day students take it.

Which division should my child enter?

Elementary is for grades 3 to 6, Junior for middle school students up to grade 9, Intermediate for high schoolers newer to programming, Senior for experienced programmers, and Classroom for any grade without much programming.

What programming languages can be used?

The official home page says the upper divisions solve a problem using Python, C++ or Java.

Can a student enter ACSL without a school team?

Students compete on teams registered by an adult advisor, and the Advisor Guide allows a parent with computer science experience to take that role, so a club or homeschool group can form a team.

What is the registration deadline?

The Registration page gives December 31, 2026 as the deadline for registering teams for the 2026-27 season.

How is the programming problem scored?

There are up to 12 test cases and the last 6 are hidden. Each hidden case passed is worth one point, and the last run before time ends is the score.

How do students qualify for the ACSL Finals?

Invitations are based on regular-season scores: at least 28 points in the Junior, Intermediate and Senior divisions, 24 in Classroom or 14 in Elementary. The 2027 Finals are online on Saturday, May 29, 2027.

Can students use AI or get help during a contest?

No. Students must work independently, collaboration scores 0 for that test, and the Finals run in a secure mode with full-screen and webcam checks.

When are your ACSL classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

How does Modern Age Coders prepare students?

A regular teacher works through the official topics in contest order, drills hand tracing, practises programming problems against hidden-style tests and reviews timed past papers. We never help during a live contest.
