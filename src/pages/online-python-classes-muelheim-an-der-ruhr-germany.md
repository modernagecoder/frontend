### What the mentor does

In a live class the mentor reads your code while you write it. When a loop runs once too often, they ask you what the last value of the counter was, instead of fixing it. That question is the lesson.

A mentor also notices patterns over weeks: a learner who always forgets to convert text to a number, or who writes long programs before testing any part. Those habits are hard to see from inside a video course.

A laptop or desktop, a browser, earphones, and for teenagers and adults Python itself, which the mentor helps install in the first class. Children can begin in a browser editor.

Classes are sixty minutes, twice a week. Between classes there is one short task, written so that twenty minutes is enough.

## What was July 2025 like at the Mülheim weather station? One rule per column

Stadt Mülheim an der Ruhr runs its own weather station and publishes every reading as open data. Our learners use a year of it to meet the mistake almost every beginner makes with data: treating every column the same.

### The data

The city describes the dataset as all data from the Mülheim weather station, recorded since mid-2006, and publishes one file per year. The 2025 file has 51,465 readings, one every ten minutes, with the time as a UNIX timestamp in UTC. It is published under the Creative Commons Attribution 3.0 Germany licence (Datenquelle: Stadt Mülheim an der Ruhr). We downloaded it on 3 November 2026.

The values are stored as whole numbers: temperature in tenths of a degree, rain in tenths of a millimetre per ten minutes, sunshine in minutes per ten minutes, and the strongest gust in knots.

### The question

Summarise July 2025 in four numbers: how warm, how wet, how sunny, how windy. It sounds like "take the average of each column". It is not, and finding out why is the lesson.

Every column measures a different kind of thing. Temperature is a level, rain and sunshine pile up, and a gust is a peak. Each kind needs its own rule.

**Step 1: the lazy way, one rule for everything** (Python)
```python
import csv
from datetime import datetime, timezone

july = []
with open("wetterdaten_2025.csv", encoding="utf-8") as raw:
    for r in csv.DictReader(raw, delimiter=";"):
        moment = datetime.fromtimestamp(int(r["time"]), timezone.utc)
        if moment.month == 7:
            july.append(r)

print("Readings in July:", len(july), "of", 31 * 24 * 6)

def average(column):
    return sum(int(r[column]) for r in july) / len(july)

print("Average of every column, the lazy way:")
for column in ["T0", "M0", "S0", "V0_max"]:
    print(" ", column, round(average(column), 2))
```
It prints Readings in July: 4462 of 4464, then the lazy averages T0 213.56, M0 0.17, S0 3.32 and V0_max 8.24.

None of those four numbers answers the question. 213.56 is a temperature in tenths of a degree. 0.17 is the average rain per ten minutes, in tenths of a millimetre, which no one would ever say about a month. 3.32 is sunshine minutes per ten-minute slot. And 8.24 knots is the average of the strongest gusts, not the strongest gust.

**Step 2: one rule per column** (Python)
```python
rules = {
    "T0": ("mean temperature, C", lambda v: sum(v) / len(v) / 10),
    "M0": ("total rain, mm", lambda v: sum(v) / 10),
    "S0": ("total sunshine, hours", lambda v: sum(v) / 60),
    "V0_max": ("strongest gust, km/h", lambda v: max(v) * 1.852),
}

print("July 2025, one rule per column:")
for column, (label, rule) in rules.items():
    values = [int(r[column]) for r in july]
    print(" ", label, round(rule(values), 1))
```
Run after Step 1. It prints mean temperature, C 21.4, total rain, mm 75.9, total sunshine, hours 247.1 and strongest gust, km/h 66.7.

The dictionary holds a label and a rule for each column. Temperature is averaged and divided by ten. Rain and sunshine are summed, because they accumulate. The gust takes the maximum, because the question is about the peak, and is converted from knots at 1.852 km/h per knot. The data never changed; the rules did.

**Step 3: rain by month, and how complete each month is** (Python)
```python
import calendar

months = {}
with open("wetterdaten_2025.csv", encoding="utf-8") as raw:
    for r in csv.DictReader(raw, delimiter=";"):
        m = datetime.fromtimestamp(int(r["time"]), timezone.utc).month
        months.setdefault(m, []).append(int(r["M0"]))

for m, rain in sorted(months.items()):
    slots = calendar.monthrange(2025, m)[1] * 24 * 6
    share = 100 * len(rain) / slots
    note = "" if share >= 95 else "  <- incomplete"
    print(calendar.month_abbr[m], round(sum(rain) / 10, 1), "mm", f"{share:.0f} % of readings{note}")
```
Run after Step 1. It prints twelve lines, from Jan 104.2 mm 94 % of readings <- incomplete to Dec 54.4 mm 84 % of readings <- incomplete.

**Rain at the Mülheim station in 2025, by month (our calculation)**

| Month | Rain, mm | Share of readings present |
|---|---|---|
| January | 104.2 | 94 % |
| February | 8.6 | 98 % |
| March | 9.8 | 100 % |
| April | 84.9 | 100 % |
| July | 75.9 | 100 % |
| September | 98.3 | 100 % |
| December | 54.4 | 84 % |

Data: Stadt Mülheim an der Ruhr, open data Wetterdaten, file for 2025, licence Creative Commons Attribution 3.0 Germany, downloaded 3 November 2026. All totals, averages and shares are ours; months are counted in UTC. The 2025 file ends on 27 December 2025, which is why December is incomplete. January had the most rain in the file, but with 6 percent of its readings missing; September, with every reading present, comes next.

1. **Children, 9 to 12.** Write down the temperature and whether it rained at 8:00, 12:00 and 18:00 for three days. Which numbers should you add, and which should you average?
2. **Teenagers.** Add a fifth rule for humidity (F0). Is it a level, a pile or a peak?
3. **Adults.** Turn Step 2 into a function that takes any month and returns the four numbers, then print a twelve-row table.
4. **Everyone.** Explain why January cannot be called the wettest month of 2025 with full confidence.

| Mülheim | India now (to 28 March 2027) | India after 28 March 2027 |

Under the Einheitenzeitgesetz, Germany uses MEZ (UTC+1) in winter and MESZ (UTC+2) in summer. The clocks went back on 25 October 2026 and spring forward on 28 March 2027, while India stays on UTC+5:30 throughout. So India is four and a half hours ahead of Mülheim this winter and three and a half hours ahead from the spring. These are sample conversions, not batches.
