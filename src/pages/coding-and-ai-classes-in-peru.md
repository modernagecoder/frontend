## How is school organised in Peru, and where does coding fit?

Peruvian schooling follows one national framework set by the Ministry of Education, MINEDU, and one national curriculum, the CNEB. That makes the national picture easier to describe than in many countries, and it also shows clearly where programming is missing.

### Inicial, primaria, secundaria

Under the Ley General de Educación, Ley 28044, regular basic education has three levels: inicial, primaria and secundaria. The law says primaria lasts six years and secundaria lasts five, so a pupil who starts on time finishes 5° de secundaria at about 16 or 17.

The size of the system is worth knowing. MINEDU reported that 53,631 schools of regular basic education opened their doors to 6,225,000 students when the 2026 school year began on 16 March 2026. Under RM 501-2025-MINEDU the 2026 year has 36 teaching weeks in four blocks with short breaks between them, and the long holiday falls in the southern summer, between January and March.

### Computing is a competency, not a subject

Among the CNEB's competencies, number 28, which MINEDU titles "Se desenvuelve en entornos virtuales generados por las TIC", covers using and creating in digital environments, and the curriculum marks it as transversal: every area is meant to touch it, so no single teacher owns it.

The weekly timetable for secundaria under the regular school day shows the effect. Matemática gets 4 teaching hours a week in every grade, Comunicación 4 and Inglés 3, and each teaching hour is 45 minutes, but there is no area called computing or programming. Schools on the longer Jornada Escolar Completa give maths 6 hours, and still no programming area.

Programming has started to appear around the edges. In July 2025 MINEDU launched a pilot called Fábrica de Programadores for 1,000 public-school pupils in 4° and 5° de secundaria in Lima Norte, teaching Python and preparing them to sit the PCEP exam. MINEDU describes it as a first stage that it hopes to extend to the whole country. For everyone outside the pilot, coding still happens after school or not at all.

Our classes are not a school and they lead to no Peruvian qualification. Your child's colegio stays in charge of grades and promotion. What we add is regular hands-on practice: live lessons where a learner writes a program, tests it and explains it aloud, or reasons through maths with a mentor, wherever they stand in the Peruvian system.

## Which grade is my child in? A birthday bug in eleven lines

Peruvian parents meet this question every March, and so does every school secretary who enrols a new pupil. MINEDU answers it with a single date, and that date makes a good first lesson in why programs need careful tests.

### The rule

MINEDU publishes a table of normative ages, the age a child is expected to have when enrolling in each grade, and adds one sentence that matters more than the rest: "Se considera la edad normativa al 31 de marzo de cada año." A child counts as 6 for 1° primaria only if they have turned 6 by 31 March of that school year.

So two children born a day apart can belong to different grades. A child born on 31 March is exactly 6 on the cutoff; a child born on 1 April is still 5 and, by the table, belongs in Inicial 5 años for one more year.

### The task

Students write a program that takes a list of children and their birthdays and prints the grade each one belongs in for the 2027 school year, using MINEDU's table from Inicial 3 años to 5° de secundaria.

The five children are invented by us. They are chosen so that one is born well before the cutoff, one exactly on it, one the day after, and two who are older and whose birthdays fall on either side of 31 March.

**Version 1: age is the year of the cutoff minus the year of birth** (Python)
```python
from datetime import date

CUTOFF = date(2027, 3, 31)   # MINEDU: the normative age is taken on 31 March

GRADES = {3: "Inicial, 3 años", 4: "Inicial, 4 años", 5: "Inicial, 5 años"}
for age in range(6, 12):
    GRADES[age] = f"{age - 5}° primaria"
for age in range(12, 17):
    GRADES[age] = f"{age - 11}° secundaria"

children = [
    ("Valeria", date(2021, 2, 14)),
    ("Mateo", date(2021, 3, 31)),
    ("Camila", date(2021, 4, 1)),
    ("Diego", date(2014, 9, 3)),
    ("Lucía", date(2011, 1, 22)),
]

def age_on_cutoff(born):
    return CUTOFF.year - born.year

for name, born in children:
    age = age_on_cutoff(born)
    print(name, born, "->", age, GRADES.get(age, "outside school age"))
```
It prints Valeria 2021-02-14 -> 6 1° primaria, Mateo 2021-03-31 -> 6 1° primaria, Camila 2021-04-01 -> 6 1° primaria, Diego 2014-09-03 -> 13 2° secundaria and Lucía 2011-01-22 -> 16 5° secundaria.

Three of the five lines are right, and that is the trap. Camila turns 6 on 1 April 2027, a day after the cutoff, so on 31 March she is 5 and belongs in Inicial 5 años, not 1° primaria. Diego turns 13 in September 2027, so on the cutoff he is 12 and belongs in 1° de secundaria. Subtracting one year from another only gives an age if the birthday has already happened; the program never asked.

**Version 2: check whether the birthday has arrived by 31 March** (Python)
```python
def age_on_cutoff(born):
    age = CUTOFF.year - born.year
    if (born.month, born.day) > (CUTOFF.month, CUTOFF.day):
        age -= 1   # the birthday comes after 31 March
    return age
```
With this function the program prints Camila 2021-04-01 -> 5 Inicial, 5 años and Diego 2014-09-03 -> 12 1° secundaria; Valeria, Mateo and Lucía are unchanged.

The fix compares two pairs, month then day, the way Python compares tuples: (4, 1) > (3, 31) is true because 4 is bigger than 3, and the day is only looked at when the months are equal. Mateo, born on 31 March, gives two equal pairs, so nothing is subtracted and he stays 6. That one line is why the test list includes a child born exactly on the cutoff: if the comparison had been written with >=, Mateo would wrongly drop a year.

**The five invented children, what Version 1 says and what MINEDU's rule says (our run of both programs)**

| Child, born | Version 1 | Version 2, the rule | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Valeria, 14 February 2021 | 1° primaria | 1° primaria | Birthday well before 31 March |
| Mateo, 31 March 2021 | 1° primaria | 1° primaria | Turns 6 on the cutoff itself |
| Camila, 1 April 2021 | 1° primaria | Inicial 5 años | Turns 6 one day too late |
| Diego, 3 September 2014 | 2° secundaria | 1° secundaria | Turns 13 after the cutoff |
| Lucía, 22 January 2011 | 5° secundaria | 5° secundaria | Birthday well before 31 March |

Rule: MINEDU, Edades normativas, ages 3 to 16 against Inicial 3 años to 5° de secundaria, measured on 31 March. The children, the 2027 run and the outputs are ours. The normative age is a reference for enrolment; schools and UGELs apply the detailed enrolment rules, and a family should always confirm a placement with the school.

1. **Ages 7 to 9, on paper.** Write the birthdays of five friends or cousins on cards. Put each card on the left of a line marked 31 March if the birthday comes first in the year, on the right if it comes after. Which side has to subtract one?
2. **Ages 9 to 12, Scratch.** Ask for a birth month and day, then make a sprite say whether the birthday is on or before 31 March. Try 3 and 31, then 4 and 1.
3. **Ages 12 to 16, in Python.** Type in Version 1 and spot the two wrong lines before reading Version 2. Then write one test with a child born on 31 March and one born on 1 April.
4. **Going further.** A child born on 29 February 2020 has a birthday only in leap years. Does Version 2 still give the right grade for 2027, and why does the tuple comparison never need to know?

## Can my child learn coding online from anywhere in Peru?

Yes, if there is a stable connection and a proper computer. INEI's household survey shows the first is common across Peru and the second much less so, which is worth checking before the first class.

### The connection

INEI's technology bulletin for the second quarter of 2026 found that 84.2% of people aged 6 and over in Peru used the internet, 92.4% in Lima Metropolitana, 86.1% in other urban areas and 63.7% in rural areas. Among children aged 6 to 11 the figure was 68.2%.

A live class needs about the same connection as a video call. If a call with relatives works smoothly, a class will too.

### The computer

The same INEI bulletin found that 36.4% of households in Peru had at least one computer. For coding that matters more than the connection: typing a program, switching windows and reading errors is hard on a phone. A laptop or desktop with a keyboard is what we ask for; for the youngest Scratch learners a tablet can be enough.

Nothing else has to be bought. Scratch and Python, the two tools almost every beginner starts with, cost nothing to use.

The ONEM school-stage paper, as described in the UGEL 06 circular for 2026, has ten questions, each answered with a single result the pupil works out and writes down, and lasts two hours. ## What time are the classes in Peru?

At whatever hour you choose. Peru keeps one time zone with no daylight saving, so the gap to India never changes: India is 10 hours 30 minutes ahead of Peru on every day of the year.

**Some Peru times converted to India time (examples only, not a timetable)**

| Time in Peru | Time in India | Same day in India? |
|---|---|---|
| 07:00 | 17:30 | Yes |
| 09:00 | 19:30 | Yes |
| 11:00 | 21:30 | Yes |
| 13:00 | 23:30 | Yes |
| 16:00 | 02:30 | No, already the next day |
| 19:00 | 05:30 | No, already the next day |
| 21:00 | 07:30 | No, already the next day |

Peru uses UTC-5 all year; the last Peruvian summer time ended on 1 April 1994, according to the tz database maintained by IANA (entry America/Lima), and INACAL keeps the Hora Oficial del Perú. India uses UTC+5:30 all year (entry Asia/Kolkata). The conversions are ours, checked for January, July and March dates.

Because the gap is fixed, a time agreed in March still works in October. Any afternoon or evening in Peru is already the next calendar day in India, which only matters when you read a date on a calendar invite. The booking form for the free class shows times in your own Peru clock; the mentor receives the matching moment in India time.

## Coding and AI classes in Peru: common questions

### Are the classes in Spanish?

No. All of our classes are taught live in English, by a mentor in India who speaks clearly and adjusts to how fast the learner works and how they speak. There are no lessons in Spanish. Many Peruvian families treat that as a benefit: coding or maths and English practice in the same hour.

### How young can a child start coding with you?

At 6, which is the MINEDU normative age for 1° primaria. Primaria children begin with Scratch blocks and move to typed Python at about 9 or 10. Teenagers and adults start at whatever level the free first class shows.

### Is programming taught in Peruvian schools?

Not as a subject of its own. The CNEB treats digital skills as a transversal competency, number 28, and the secundaria timetable has no programming area. In July 2025 MINEDU started Fábrica de Programadores, a Python pilot reaching a thousand public-school pupils in Lima Norte.

### What times are the classes in Peru?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. In Peru the clock stays at UTC-5 all year while India runs 10 hours 30 minutes ahead, so you always pick the time in your own clock.

### Where are the mentors?

In India. They teach over live video, and a learner is not passed from one mentor to another. Because neither country changes its clocks, the time you agree on stays the same all year.

### How are fees paid from Peru?

Peru fees are priced in US dollars and charged to a debit or credit card, and your bank converts the amount into soles. The first class is free and asks for no card.

### Do you prepare pupils for ONEM or the OPI?

We teach olympiad maths and competitive programming with problems we write ourselves, never the organisers' own papers. Entry to ONEM is through the school, and the OPI is run by UTEC. We are not connected with either organiser.

### Can you help with the IB Diploma?

Yes, alongside school. We teach IB Maths AA and AI and IB Computer Science in English. The school and the IB set and mark the work, and we do not award any IB or Peruvian qualification.

### Do you prepare students for university admission exams in Peru?

Not for any specific exam. Peruvian admission exams are in Spanish and each university decides who it admits. We offer maths reasoning practice in English, and we promise no admission result.

### Does my child need a computer?

Yes, a laptop or desktop with a keyboard for typed code. INEI found that 36.4% of households in Peru had a computer in the second quarter of 2026, so it is worth checking first. A tablet is enough for the youngest Scratch learners.

### Do you teach adults in Peru?

Yes. Adults and university students learn Python, Java, automation and AI coding agents with us, in English, at hours that fit around work or study.
