IB Computer Science is an unusual subject for a student in Peru: the national curriculum has no computing subject at all, so a Diploma student may meet real programming for the first time at 15 or 16. ## Who needs an IB Computer Science tutor in Peru?

Any Diploma student taking the subject who wants more time with programming, theory or the internal assessment than a school class allows. In Peru that need is sharper than in many countries, because school before the Diploma usually teaches no computing.

### First-time programmers

A student who chose computer science out of curiosity and has never written a loop. The first months of the Diploma move quickly; a tutor can make sure variables, conditions, loops and functions are solid before the course builds on them.

### Strong coders, weak theory

A student who has taught themselves Python from videos but loses marks on networks, databases or written explanation. For them the work is precise English, exact definitions and the habit of answering what the question asks.

### Students heading abroad

MINEDU reports 2,502 IB Diplomas earned in Peru in 2025. Students who plan to study computing at a university abroad want their programming, and their ability to talk about it in English, well ahead of the Diploma minimum.

The school stays in charge. Teaching the course, setting deadlines and submitting assessed work are the IB school's job; ours is extra teaching beside it, in English. We are not connected with or endorsed by the IB, COAR, MINEDU, UTEC or any school, and the descriptions of the course on this page are our own.

## What changed in IB Computer Science for exams from 2027?

Quite a lot, according to the course page we teach from. The IB website turned our research request away, so what follows is how our own course reads the change; the official text is with the school's IB coordinator.

### What our course page says changed

Our course page describes a new guide launched in February 2025 for first teaching in August 2025, with first assessment in May 2027. It says Paper 3 has been removed at both levels, the options have gone, and the case study now applies to SL and HL students alike, assessed inside Paper 1.

It also says databases and object-oriented programming are now studied at both levels, not only at HL, and that the course is organised in two themes: concepts of computer science, and computational thinking and problem-solving.

### What that means for a student in Peru

Schools choose an exam session, and a Peruvian school on a November calendar may meet the new papers at a different moment from a school sitting in May; ask the coordinator which applies to you. Either way, the content is new for everyone, including teachers.

Paper 2, as our course page describes it, now runs in two versions, one in Python and one in Java, and a school picks one at department level. A tutor must therefore teach in the school's language, not their own favourite. We teach Python by default and support Java.

The internal assessment, a computational solution to a real problem, keeps its weight in the course, and our course page notes a stronger emphasis on documenting the computational thinking behind it. We support planning, technique and documentation habits; the solution, the code and the writing must be the student's own, under the school's academic integrity rules.

## Is computer science taught in Peruvian secondary schools?

Not as a subject. The national curriculum, the CNEB, has no computing or programming area in its plan of studies. Digital skills are a cross-curricular competency, and coding appears mainly in pilots and competitions.

### Where digital skills sit

The CNEB lists competency 28, "Se desenvuelve en entornos virtuales generados por las TIC", among the competencies that cut across all areas. It is about using and creating in virtual environments, not about algorithms; no area of the timetable owns it.

Two areas come closest. Ciencia y Tecnología includes a competency about designing and building technological solutions to problems, and Educación para el Trabajo has two periods a week on the regular secundaria day and three on the full day. Neither is a programming course.

### Where coding does appear

In July 2025 MINEDU launched Fábrica de Programadores, a pilot teaching Python to 1,000 public-school pupils in 4th and 5th of secundaria in Lima Norte, with the chance to sit the PCEP exam. It is a promising pilot, not a national subject.

Then there are competitions. UTEC runs the Peruvian computing olympiads, open free of charge to every secondary pupil in Peru: OPI for programming, OPEIA for artificial intelligence and OPECIA for cybersecurity.

At home, the picture matters too. INEI found that 36.4% of Peruvian households had at least one computer in the second quarter of 2026. An IB CS student needs a computer they can program on for hours, so we check that early, and suggest class hours when a shared machine is free. ### How the team is chosen

Peru's IOI team comes through OPI, run by UTEC. Under the 2025 rules, the first phase was virtual and allowed C++ or Python; the in-person final at UTEC in Lima lasted five hours and allowed only C++. The top four of the final were selected for the IOI.

Entry is free for secondary pupils. The 2025 rules set a maximum age of 20 on 1 July 2025 and required contestants to be at a Peruvian school in 2025 or to have left in 2024. For 2026, UTEC's page gave 18 March 2026 as the registration deadline and 11 April 2026 as the final.

### What it means for an IB student

Olympiad problems are much harder than IB Paper 2, but the habits overlap: read the problem exactly, reason about cases before typing, test the edges. An IB student who enjoys Paper 2 algorithms may enjoy OPI too.

The OPI 2025 rules also ban generative AI for solving problems. That matches how we teach: students think through a problem themselves first. We are not connected with UTEC, OPI or the IOI, and we never reproduce their problems.

## What does IB Computer Science tutoring look like? NULL in SQL, with Python

With databases now in the course at both levels, as our course page describes, this is a lesson we teach early. It uses Python's built-in sqlite3, so it runs anywhere, and it shows why a missing value in a database is not the same as zero.

### The data

A school coding club keeps a table of entries: student, grade and score. Two students, Bruno and Diego, registered but have not been marked yet, so their score is NULL. All names and numbers here are invented by us.

Version 1 asks three innocent questions. How many rows, how many scores, and what is the average? Who has no score? And who scored below 50 or at least 50, which surely means everyone?

### What goes wrong

The first line says 5 rows but only 3 scores, and an average of 58.3: AVG quietly ignored the two NULLs. The second query, score = NULL, returns nothing at all, because in SQL nothing equals an unknown, not even another unknown.

The third query is the real surprise. A condition that covers every possible number still returns only three students. A comparison with NULL is neither true nor false but unknown, and WHERE keeps only rows that are true.

**Version 1: three questions, three surprises** (Python)
```python
import sqlite3

db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
db.execute("CREATE TABLE entries (student TEXT, grade INTEGER, score INTEGER)")
db.executemany("INSERT INTO entries VALUES (?, ?, ?)", [
    ("Ana", 4, 55), ("Bruno", 5, None), ("Carla", 4, 80),
    ("Diego", 3, None), ("Elena", 5, 40),
])

print(db.execute("SELECT COUNT(*), COUNT(score), AVG(score) FROM entries").fetchone())
print(db.execute("SELECT student FROM entries WHERE score = NULL").fetchall())
print(db.execute("SELECT student FROM entries WHERE score < 50 OR score >= 50").fetchall())
```
It prints (5, 3, 58.333333333333336), then [], then [('Ana',), ('Carla',), ('Elena',)]. Bruno and Diego vanish from a condition that seems to cover every score.

**Version 2, run after Version 1: ask about NULL properly** (Python)
```python
print(db.execute("SELECT student FROM entries WHERE score IS NULL").fetchall())
print(db.execute(
    "SELECT COUNT(*), SUM(CASE WHEN score IS NULL THEN 1 ELSE 0 END), "
    "ROUND(AVG(COALESCE(score, 0)), 1) FROM entries").fetchone())
```
It prints [('Bruno',), ('Diego',)] and (5, 2, 35.0): IS NULL finds the missing scores, and treating them as zero drops the average from 58.3 to 35.0.

Which average is right, 58.3 or 35.0? Neither, until someone says what NULL means here. If Bruno and Diego have not been marked yet, 58.3 is the average of the marked work. If they missed the test and the club rule says a missed test scores zero, 35.0 is right. **NULL means unknown, not zero, and only a human can decide which it should become.**

That last sentence is the IB part. Exam questions about databases reward students who can explain behaviour precisely, and a strong internal assessment states its assumptions about data plainly. A student who writes "missing scores were excluded from the average because they were not yet marked" has shown the computational thinking the course asks for.

1. **Before the Diploma.** Without running anything, predict what COUNT(score) returns for the table. Then explain the difference from COUNT(*) in one English sentence.
2. **SL.** Rewrite the third query so that it returns all five students. There are at least two ways; find both.
3. **HL.** Add a second table of grades with a JOIN. What happens to a student whose grade has no matching row with an inner join, and with a left join?
4. **IA practice.** Write the assumption you would put in your documentation about missing data for a project of your own, in two sentences.

## What does an IB Computer Science tutor do across the Diploma?

A plan that runs from the first loop to the final paper, adjusted to the school's order. This is ours, not the IB's, and every school organises its own calendar.

1. **Make programming automatic.** In the first months, short daily problems in the school's Paper 2 language until variables, conditions, loops, lists and functions need no thought.
2. **Teach theory in exact English.** Networks, system fundamentals, databases and machine learning are learnt as precise definitions and explanations, practised in writing.
3. **Work the case study properly.** When the school releases it, we read it closely with the student, build the vocabulary and practise answering questions on it, rather than skimming it the week before Paper 1.
4. **Practise object-oriented design.** Classes, objects and relationships, drawn and coded, because our course page says OOP is now studied at both levels.
5. **Support the internal assessment honestly.** Choosing a realistic problem, planning, testing and documenting: we teach the techniques; the student writes the solution and the documentation.
6. **Rehearse the papers.** In the final months, timed papers in the student's language, with every lost mark sorted into knowledge, method, reading or time.

## Can an IB Computer Science student use AI tools?

Carefully, within the school's rules and the tools' own age terms. We teach students to reason through a problem and write the code themselves first; AI comes later, if at all, as a reviewer.

### Age terms first

Most Diploma students are under 18. Anthropic's consumer terms set 18 as the minimum age for Claude, and OpenAI requires a parent's permission for ChatGPT users under 18. We follow those terms in class.

### Assessed work is yours

The internal assessment and any assessed task must be the student's own. The school's IB coordinator explains exactly what is allowed; we never write or edit assessed code or documentation.

### Practise without help

Peru's own olympiad, OPI, bans generative AI for solving problems. A student who can solve problems unaided is ready for OPI, for Paper 2 and for university.

The lesson above shows why. An AI tool may confidently report an average of 58.3 without mentioning the two missing rows. A student who has learnt what NULL means asks the question the tool did not.

## Milestones for an IB Computer Science student

We wrote these milestones ourselves. A predicted grade says little about what to do next; these say exactly that.

**IB Computer Science milestones (ours)**

| Milestone | When | The student can |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Writes code fluently | First term | Solve short problems in the school's language without notes |
| 2. Traces by hand | First year | Predict a program's output before running it |
| 3. Explains theory exactly | First year | Define and explain each theory topic in precise English |
| 4. Questions the data | First year onwards | Say what missing or odd data does to a result, as in the lesson |
| 5. Owns the IA | Second year, before the internal deadline | Explain every design choice and line of their own solution |
| 6. Finishes papers in time | Final months | Complete a timed paper, then find and fix each lost mark |

### For parents

Ask your child what their program does before they run it. Milestone 2 is where real understanding shows.
A student who can explain why a result might be wrong is further ahead than one who only gets results.

### For students

Every time a query surprises you, write down why. Those notes become exam answers.
Code that runs is not code that is right. Test the empty, the missing and the odd case.
