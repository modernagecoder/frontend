AI and Python classes for teens in Fredericton

How can a Fredericton teenager learn Python and AI with a live teacher, around a New Brunswick timetable?

Modern Age Coders teaches Python and AI to Fredericton teenagers between 13 and 18 in live online classes, in a group of fifteen to twenty or one to one. Students normally begin with Python for Teens, then take AI and Machine Learning for Teens, Data Science for Teens or CCC preparation. Every class is booked on Atlantic time, and the first lesson is free.

Fredericton is New Brunswick’s capital and sits in Canada’s only officially bilingual province, so a teenager here often moves between English and French in a single day. New Brunswick’s high school computer science course now mentions artificial intelligence directly, and even lists AI-assisted debugging tools as one way to test and refine an algorithm. At the same time, the national programming contest bans AI inside the editor. A Fredericton student therefore needs both habits: using AI tools carefully where they are allowed, and thinking without them where they are not. This page quotes New Brunswick’s curriculum and CEMC’s rules, explains the age limits on AI tools for younger teens, and walks through a text classifier our students build on the City of Fredericton’s bilingual list of recreation amenities.

That last row is unusual. Most curriculum documents we read in 2026 mention artificial intelligence as a topic; New Brunswick’s Computer Science 110 also lists AI-assisted debugging tools as an accepted way to refine an algorithm. For a Fredericton teen this is a reasonable rule for school work, and it sits beside a stricter rule elsewhere: in the CCC, AI help inside the editor is banned. We teach students to handle both. In practice that means writing and testing code by hand until they can find their own bugs, then learning how an AI debugger can speed up that work without replacing it, and how to check what it suggests. A student who can only debug with an assistant is stuck in February; one who can debug alone uses an assistant far better.

City of Fredericton data project

Can a computer tell from a description, in English or French, whether a Fredericton amenity is open all year?

The City of Fredericton lists its recreation amenities, from playgrounds and ball diamonds to dog parks, with a description in English, a description in French and a season. Our students hide the season and train a naive Bayes classifier on the words. All accuracy figures here are our own results; the descriptions and seasons are the City’s.

Our naive Bayes results on City of Fredericton Recreation Amenities (283 amenities tagged Summer or Year-Round, fetched 22 October 2026; each one tested while the other 282 are used for training)
ModelWhat it readsShare of amenities whose season it guesses right
BaselineNothing: always says Year-Round, the commoner season54.1%
Naive Bayes, EnglishThe words of the English description88.7%
Naive Bayes, FrenchThe words of the French description85.2%

This is the complete program, using only Python’s standard library. It downloads the amenity list, turns each description into a set of lower-case words, and for every amenity in turn trains on all the others and guesses the left-out one, a method called leave-one-out that suits a small dataset. The add-one in the formula keeps a word the model has never seen with a season from zeroing out the whole calculation. Its output on 22 October 2026 is the three-row table above:import json, math, re, urllib.request from collections import Counter URL = ("https://services2.arcgis.com/iLWAxhpxafhOza2U/arcgis/rest/services/" "Recreation_Amenities_view/FeatureServer/0/query?where=1%3D1" "&outFields=Description,DescriptionFR,Season&returnGeometry=false&f=json") rows = [f["attributes"] for f in json.load(urllib.request.urlopen(URL))["features"]] rows = [r for r in rows if r["Season"] in ("Summer", "Year-Round")] def words(text): return set(re.findall(r"\w+", (text or "").lower())) def train_and_guess(train, sample): seasons = Counter(season for _, season in train) counts = {s: Counter() for s in seasons} for bag, season in train: counts[season].update(bag) vocab = set().union(*(bag for bag, _ in train)) def score(s): total = sum(counts[s].values()) return math.log(seasons[s] / len(train)) + sum( math.log((counts[s][w] + 1) / (total + len(vocab))) for w in sample if w in vocab) return max(seasons, key=score) def leave_one_out(field): data = [(words(r[field]), r["Season"]) for r in rows] right = sum(train_and_guess(data[:i] + data[i + 1:], bag) == season for i, (bag, season) in enumerate(data)) return round(100 * right / len(data), 1) top = Counter(r["Season"] for r in rows).most_common(1)[0] print(len(rows), "amenities; always", repr(top[0]), "is right", round(100 * top[1] / len(rows), 1), "% of the time") print("naive Bayes on English descriptions:", leave_one_out("Description"), "%") print("naive Bayes on French descriptions:", leave_one_out("DescriptionFR"), "%")

1. Words as evidence

Naive Bayes treats each word as a small piece of evidence. In the English descriptions, “diamond” and “ball” appear 25 times each, always on summer amenities, while “playground” appears 57 times on year-round amenities and only twice on summer ones.

2. Surprising clues

The word “drop”, from “drop-in”, appears 31 times, every time on a summer amenity. The model has no idea what drop-in means; it only learned that the word travels with summer. That is the strength and the weakness of statistical learning.

3. English or French?

The French descriptions give 85.2 per cent against 88.7 in English. Students investigate why: French joins words with apostrophes, uses more forms of each word, and spreads the same evidence over more spellings. Handling that is real natural language processing.

Where the model fails, and why that is useful

The English model makes 32 mistakes. Seven are summer parking areas called year-round and four are year-round parking areas called summer: a parking description says little about the season, and the City lists 28 parking rows as year-round and 11 as summer. Six more are community school gyms. When a model fails on one category again and again, the honest conclusion is that the text does not contain the answer, not that a cleverer model is needed.

A few notes on the data. Of the 295 amenities in the layer, 11 are tagged Winter and one has no season, so the model compares only Summer (130) and Year-Round (153). The descriptions were written by people, not generated, which is why identical amenities often share a template sentence and why the model does well on them. The dataset sits in the City’s Fredericton Open Data catalogue, published under the Open Government License - City of Fredericton, version 1.0, which the City says is based on the Open Government Licence - Canada and whose grant extends to use “including for commercial purposes”. Source: City of Fredericton, Recreation Amenities, City of Fredericton open data. Nothing here carries the City’s endorsement.

Debug before you delegate

Why a Fredericton teen should learn to debug alone before using AI to debug

Because New Brunswick allows the tool in class, the CCC forbids it, and only a student who can do both is ready for either.

An AI debugger is useful in the hands of someone who knows what a correct program should do. In the hands of someone who does not, it just moves errors around. A teen who has found a dozen bugs by reading their own output knows what to ask a tool, and knows when its answer is wrong.

So we keep a fixed order. Understand the problem, write the steps, predict the output, run it, find the mistakes, and only then, within the age rules, try a tool and compare its suggestion with your own. Students taught this way get more from AI, not less.

In the CCC the order is enforced for everyone: no AI in the editor, so every fix has to come from the student. The longer argument is in a fuller case for coding in 2026.

Questions from Fredericton families

What parents and teenagers in Fredericton ask us most.

Are your Fredericton classes online or in person?

Online and live. There is no Fredericton centre; teens join from home, in a group of fifteen to twenty or with a private teacher.

Are lessons in English or French?

English, with French vocabulary given for the main ideas. The CCC paper itself can be written in French, which CEMC allows.

Do your courses count as New Brunswick Computer Science 110?

No. We teach alongside school and do not grant provincial credits; Computer Science 110 and 120 credits come from the school.

Computer Science 110 mentions AI debugging tools. Do you teach them?

Yes, once a student can debug without them, and only using tools the student may legally use at their age. Contest practice stays AI-free.

Is my 14 year old allowed to use ChatGPT?

Fourteen is above OpenAI's minimum of 13, but until 18 a parent or guardian has to give permission. We involve it only after that yes.

Do teenagers use Claude in your classes?

No. Claude is restricted to adults of 18 and over by Anthropic's terms, so teen lessons leave it out.

When must a school order the CCC?

CEMC lists the ordering deadline as Thursday 11 February 2027, for a contest on Wednesday 17 February 2027. Students enter only through a school.

Is Fredericton one hour ahead of Toronto all year?

Yes. Both change clocks on the same dates, 1 November 2026 and 14 March 2027, so Atlantic time stays one hour ahead of Eastern.

When are classes for Fredericton students?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Times are confirmed in Atlantic time.

Is the amenity data project real?

Yes. The amenity list comes straight from the City of Fredericton's open data, in both languages, under its open government licence, and students write the classifier on their own. No City endorsement is implied.
