School science

What are Junín's schools doing in science and technology?

Two notes from the Dirección Regional de Educación de Junín in 2026 give a picture: a large maintenance effort across thousands of school buildings, and a Concepción school winning abroad with a science project.

Thousands of school buildings

On 11 February 2026 the DRE Junín reported funds for the "mantenimiento preventivo y correctivo de 3,322 locales educativos públicos" for the new school year, benefiting more than 277 thousand students.

Part of the money went to Rutas Solidarias, which maintains bicycles and safety equipment "para estudiantes de zonas rurales de la sierra y selva de Junín". It is a reminder that for many rural pupils, just reaching school is an effort.

From Concepción to Paraguay

On 20 October 2026 the DRE reported that two pupils from the I.E. "9 de Julio" in Concepción won first place at the Feria Internacional Aurora in Paraguay in 2026, with a project on nanoparticles made from discarded batteries to fight bacteria in water from the Mantaro river.

The same project had come third nationally in 2025 in the Clubes de Ciencia y Tecnología category of the EUREKA school science fair, and earned a place at the FENIC 2027 fair in Brazil.

Science projects like that run on measurement: samples, counts, tables, and the patience to check them. Programming is the tool that turns those tables into answers, which is why the sunlight project further down uses a real scientific dataset from Junín itself. We have no connection with the DRE Junín, the school or the fairs; we mention them only as the DRE published them.

The national curriculum

Is programming a school subject in Junín?

No. Junín's schools follow Peru's national curriculum, which asks every area to build digital skills but gives programming no lesson of its own.

From Ley 28044 (six years of primaria, five of secundaria), MINEDU's normative ages counted on 31 March, and the CNEB secundaria plan of studies. Read in November 2026.

A competency without a lesson

The CNEB includes a technology competency, number 28, about working in ICT-based virtual environments. It is transversal, meaning every area shares it, and none of its four capacities mentions writing a program.

In secundaria, a regular week gives Matemática four hours, Comunicación four, Inglés three and Educación para el Trabajo two, with nothing set aside for programming.

Contests and pilots

The ONEM maths olympiad, entered through the school, drew more than 1.7 million pupils nationwide in 2026, with its first round on 8 July. MINEDU's Python pilot, Fábrica de Programadores, began in July 2025 with 1,000 pupils in Lima Norte.

Nothing in those programmes reaches a typical Junín pupil who wants to learn to code this year, which is the gap a live online class fills.

A Junín data project

When are the sunniest three hours of the day at Huayao?

The Instituto Geofísico del Perú measures sunlight every hour at its Huancayo observatory at Huayao and publishes the readings as open data. Our older students use them to learn the sliding window, one of the most useful patterns in programming, and what to do when a day has holes in it.

The dataset

The IGP's atmospheric physics and radiation laboratory, LAMAR, publishes "Estación BSRN para el Balance Energético Solar Tierra-Atmósfera de LAMAR - Junín, Perú" on datosabiertos.gob.pe, with an Open Data Commons Attribution licence (ODC-By). It was last updated on 29 May 2026. The station is at the Huancayo-Huayao observatory, at latitude -12.0424 and longitude -75.3189; its location code, 120904, is the district of Huáchac in Chupaca province.

Our copy of the 2025 file, saved as bsrn-2025.csv, has one row for every hour of 2025, 8,760 in all. We use the column global_SW_rad, the global shortwave irradiance in watts per square metre: in plain words, how strongly the sun is shining on a flat surface.

The holes

The column is blank in 1,684 of those hours. The IGP explains part of it: the station was moved to a six-metre tower, and because of trouble with its solar tracker, "No se cuenta con datos durante todo el mes de marzo del 2025." March is entirely empty, and other months have scattered gaps.

So the question, which three hours in a row get the most sun on a typical day, has to be answered with data that is missing in places. That is the realistic version of almost every data problem.

Version 1 works, but look at what it threw away. It only uses days with no gap at all, and because a single blank hour, even at night, ruins a day, only 92 of 365 days survive. It also adds up every three-hour slice from scratch, which is fine here and wasteful on longer data. The answer, a window starting with the reading labelled 11, is reasonable, but it rests on a quarter of the year.

Version 2 slides a three-hour window along each day. It keeps a running total and a count of gaps: when an hour enters the window, it is added (or the gap count goes up); when an hour leaves, it is taken away (or the gap count goes down). A window is only considered when its gap count is zero, so a blank hour at midnight no longer spoils the afternoon. It also flags any day whose peak window starts before six in the morning, because that cannot be right for sunlight.

Now 317 days count instead of 92, and the answer holds: on 139 of them the sunniest three hours begin with the reading labelled 11, and on most of the rest they begin at 10 or 9. The check catches one day, 21 October 2025. Opening it shows why: that day has daylight readings only at 6, 12, 13, 15 and 16, so every window covering the middle of the day contains a gap, and the "peak" complete window is in the early morning. The program did exactly what it was told; the flag makes sure a person looks before anyone trusts that day. One more detail for the curious: running totals of decimals can leave a tiny leftover such as 0.00000000000003 instead of zero, which is why Version 2 ignores totals under 1.

  1. Under 8, outdoors. Put a stick in the ground and mark the end of its shadow three times in a morning. When is the shadow shortest? What does that tell you about the sun?
  2. Blocks, around 8 to 10. Make a list of twelve numbers for the hours of daylight and find the biggest single number. Then find the biggest sum of two numbers side by side.
  3. First Python, around 10 to 12. Run Version 1. Change the window from three hours to two, then to four. Does the peak starting hour move?
  4. Ages 13 and over. In Version 2, print the window totals for 21 October 2025 hour by hour and explain, in two sentences, why the peak gap-free window is so early.
  5. Going further. Group the 317 usable days by month and find, for each month, the most common starting reading. Is the pattern the same in the rainy and the dry months?

More every year. INEI's household survey shows Junín climbing quickly between 2022 and 2025, though home computers are still in fewer than one home in three.

A fast change

The share of Junín residents aged six and over who use the internet rose from 62.4 per cent in 2022 to 81.6 per cent in 2025, now level with the national figure. Homes with an internet service went from 42.5 to 49.6 per cent.

Computers lag behind: 31.9 per cent of Junín homes had one in 2025. Many people go online by phone.

In English

Do the mentors teach in Spanish if a child needs it?

No. Every class is in English only, live and online, with mentors in India who adjust their speed and phrasing to each learner. We explain here how that works for Junín learners.

Junín on the EF index

In EF's English Proficiency Index 2025, Peru scores 519, in the moderate band. On the same page Junín scores 497 and the city of Huancayo 499, both just inside EF's low band.

That points to learners who recognise a fair amount of English but need time to answer. Our classes are built for exactly that.

How the mentor adapts

Instructions are short and repeated in the same words each week. Every idea is shown on screen as well as said. A learner who replies partly in Spanish is understood and given the English words back.

Python helps here too: most of its keywords are everyday English, so learning the code teaches the vocabulary along the way.

At whatever hour suits the learner. Junín keeps Peru time all year, and the mentor in India schedules around it.

Peru keeps UTC minus 5 hours with no daylight saving (tz database, zone America/Lima, unchanged since 1994). India keeps UTC plus 5 hours 30 minutes. The gap is 10 hours 30 minutes every day of the year.

With no clock changes on either side, a class hour stays the same from one season to the next. You pick a time in your own clock when booking. The 2026 school year in Peru opened on 16 March 2026, and families often start in the long summer holiday and keep the hour when school resumes.

Online coding and AI classes in Junín: questions answered

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. All teaching is in English, live and online. Mentors in India speak slowly, repeat key phrases, adapt to how each learner talks, and when a learner answers in Spanish they help with the English words while carrying on in English.

Can we join from Satipo, La Merced or Tarma?

Yes. Every class is online, so selva and sierra towns join exactly like Huancayo. You need a steady connection and, for typed code from about nine, a computer with a keyboard.

At what age can my child start?

At six, with blocks that need no typing. Most children are ready for Python at nine or ten. Teenagers and adults can begin from zero whenever they like.

What time are the classes?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India is ten and a half hours ahead of Junín throughout the year, so 09:45 in Huancayo is 20:15 for the mentor.

Is coding taught at school in Junín?

Not as a subject. Digital skills are meant to run through all areas of the national curriculum, but secundaria's regular timetable has no programming hours.

My teenager enjoys science fairs. Will this help?

Very likely. Science projects depend on measurements and tables, and Python and data science make those far easier to analyse and present. We teach with original data projects such as the Huayao sunlight one on this page.

Do you teach AI?

Yes, at every age in a suitable form. Children learn how simple AI decides; teenagers build and test models. Under-13s never use adult chatbot accounts with us, and teenagers only use tools that allow their age.

Are there classes for adults?

Yes, in adult groups or one to one. Python, data analysis, C++ and app building are the usual choices, scheduled around work and study.

How do we pay?

Monthly by card in US dollars, converted to soles by your bank. Visitors from India see rupees on this page. The first class is free.

Is there a certificate?

Where a course includes one, the learner receives our own certificate of completion. It is not a Peruvian qualification and is not issued or backed by MINEDU, the DRE Junín or any school; it records the learner's work.
