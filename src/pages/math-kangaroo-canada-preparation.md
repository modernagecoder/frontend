# Math Kangaroo Canada preparation, Grades 1 to 12

Math Kangaroo is the Canadian branch of an international multiple-choice contest that began in France in 1991 and now runs in dozens of countries. In Canada it is organized by the Canadian Math Kangaroo Contest, a volunteer-run, not-for-profit body, and written in more than 50 cities by children from Grade 1 to Grade 12, each on a version for their own pair of grades. It is one of the few contests in Canada that a parent, not only a school, can enter a child for. This guide sets out what we could verify from primary sources on 22 October 2026, says plainly what we could not, and then does what a preparation page should: a method for young problem solvers, eight practice problems of ours across the six versions, and a plan for the weeks before the contest.

At a glance: Organiser in Canada: Canadian Math Kangaroo Contest (CMKC); Grades: 1 to 12, in six versions; Format: Multiple choice, easier to harder; Grades 1 and 2: 18 questions, 45 minutes; Grades 3 and 4: 24 questions, 60 minutes; Grades 5 to 12: 30 questions, 75 minutes; Classes: Weekdays or weekends, flexible; First step: A free first class. Kangourou sans Frontières describes itself as "an international association founded in France" whose main activity "is designing the annual Kangaroo Mathematics Competition". It says the questions "are not standard textbook problems".

Our observation from the arithmetic: every version gives an average of two and a half minutes a question. Because the questions get harder as the paper goes on, that average is misleading in a useful way. A child who spends the same time on every question will run out of time exactly where the questions become interesting.

## The habit we teach first: turn the words into a picture

Kangaroo problems ask for creativity and a different perspective, in the host-centre page's words. For children, the most reliable way to find a different perspective is to draw the problem before doing any arithmetic. Here is one of our problems, worked the way we teach it.

**Problem 6 · our Grade 5 and 6 problem, solved by drawing** (text)
```text
Problem 6 (ours, Grades 5 and 6). A rectangle 6 squares wide and
4 squares tall is drawn on squared paper. A straight line is drawn
from one corner to the opposite corner. How many of the small
squares does the line pass through (not just touch at a corner)?
(A) 6   (B) 7   (C) 8   (D) 10   (E) 12

Draw it first.
  The diagonal goes 6 across and 4 up. Both share the factor 2,
  so the line passes exactly through one grid corner in the middle,
  at 3 across and 2 up. That splits it into two equal halves,
  each a 3 by 2 rectangle's diagonal.

Count one half on the drawing.
  In a 3 by 2 rectangle the diagonal crosses 2 vertical lines
  and 1 horizontal line inside it, never at a corner, so it
  enters a new square 3 times after the first one:
  1 + 2 + 1 = 4 squares.

Both halves: 4 + 4 = 8.  Answer (C).

Check with the drawing: shade the squares the line passes through
and count them. A child who shades 10 has included the two squares
that the line only touches at the middle corner.
```
The drawing does two jobs. It finds the answer, and it checks the answer: shading the squares on paper catches the most common wrong count. A formula for this exists, but a child who has drawn ten of these understands why it works.

**Our four drawing moves for young problem solvers (ours)**

| Move | When to use it | Example on this page |
|---|---|---|
| Draw the objects | A story with people or things | Problem 3, the line of children |
| Draw a number line or a calendar | Days, positions, steps | Problem 2, the day of the week |
| Draw a diagram with overlaps | Groups that share members | Problem 5, the two clubs |
| Draw the shape, then add one line | Geometry | Problem 7, the square in the circle |

### Why drawing beats speed

Young children often lose marks on Kangaroo-style questions not because the math is hard but because they read a word problem quickly and compute with the wrong numbers. A drawing slows the reading down just enough.

Over a term, the drawings get smaller and quicker, and eventually they move into the child's head. That is the goal, but it cannot be skipped.

### Teaching to think, not to copy

We never hand a child a list of tricks for "question types". A child who understands why a picture works can face a problem nobody has shown them, which is what Kangaroo papers are full of.

Our teachers ask "what does it look like?" far more often than "what is the formula?".

## Original practice problems for every version, with solutions

All eight are our practice problems, written by our teachers. None is a Math Kangaroo problem, and none is copied or adapted from a past paper. They run from the Grades 1 and 2 version to the Grades 11 and 12 version, and every answer was checked in Python.

Problem 1 (Grades 1 and 2). Our practice problem, not a Math Kangaroo problem. Mia has 3 more stickers than Leo. Together they have 15 stickers. How many stickers does Mia have? (A) 6 (B) 8 (C) 9 (D) 12 (E) 18

Solution. Draw two rows of stickers, Mia's row 3 longer than Leo's. Take away Mia's 3 extra: 12 are left, shared equally, 6 each. So Leo has 6 and Mia has 6 + 3 = 9. Answer (C). Checking: 9 + 6 = 15.

Problem 2 (Grades 3 and 4). Our practice problem, not a Math Kangaroo problem. Today is Wednesday. What day of the week will it be 100 days from today? (A) Monday (B) Wednesday (C) Thursday (D) Friday (E) Saturday

Solution. Every 7 days it is Wednesday again. 98 = 7 x 14, so 98 days from today is a Wednesday. Two more days: Thursday, then Friday. Answer (D). Drawing a week as a circle of seven days makes the "two more" step easy to see.

Problem 3 (Grades 3 and 4). Our practice problem, not a Math Kangaroo problem. Sam is standing in a line. Sam is 7th from the front and 9th from the back. How many children are in the line? (A) 14 (B) 15 (C) 16 (D) 17 (E) 63

Solution. Draw Sam as a dot. There are 6 children in front of Sam and 8 behind. With Sam: 6 + 1 + 8 = 15. Answer (B). Answer (C) counts Sam twice, which the drawing prevents.

Problem 4 (Grades 5 and 6). Our practice problem, not a Math Kangaroo problem. The digits 1, 2, 3 and 4 are each used once to make two 2-digit numbers. What is the largest possible sum of the two numbers? (A) 64 (B) 68 (C) 73 (D) 75 (E) 77

Solution. Tens digits are worth ten times as much, so the two largest digits, 4 and 3, go in the tens places. The sum is then (4 + 3) x 10 + (2 + 1) = 73, for example 42 + 31 or 41 + 32. Answer (C).

Problem 5 (Grades 7 and 8). Our practice problem, not a Math Kangaroo problem. In a class of 30 students, 18 are in the chess club, 15 are in the puzzle club and 5 are in neither. How many are in both clubs? (A) 3 (B) 5 (C) 8 (D) 10 (E) 13

Solution. Draw two overlapping circles. Students in at least one club: 30 - 5 = 25. Adding the two clubs, 18 + 15 = 33, counts the overlap twice, so the overlap is 33 - 25 = 8. Answer (C).

Problem 6 (Grades 5 and 6). Our practice problem, not a Math Kangaroo problem. The diagonal of a 6 by 4 rectangle on squared paper: see the worked drawing in the section above. Answer (C), 8.

Problem 7 (Grades 9 and 10). Our practice problem, not a Math Kangaroo problem. A circle fits exactly inside a square of side 10, touching all four sides. A smaller square is drawn inside the circle with all four corners on the circle. What is the area of the smaller square? (A) 25 (B) 40 (C) 50 (D) 64 (E) 75

Solution. Draw it, then add one line: the diagonal of the small square. That diagonal is a diameter of the circle, which equals the side of the big square, 10. A square with diagonal 10 has area 10 x 10 / 2 = 50. Answer (C). The small square has exactly half the area of the big one, a fact worth remembering.

Problem 8 (Grades 11 and 12). Our practice problem, not a Math Kangaroo problem. In how many different orders can the eight letters of the word KANGAROO be arranged? (A) 5040 (B) 10080 (C) 20160 (D) 40320 (E) 80640

Solution. If all eight letters were different there would be 8! = 40320 orders. But the two As can swap without changing the word, and so can the two Os, so each word has been counted 2 x 2 = 4 times. The answer is 40320 / 4 = 10080. Answer (B). Answer (D) forgets the repeated letters.

### Using these at home

Give your child the problem for their own version and one from the version below, with paper for drawing and no calculator. Ask them to explain their drawing to you before saying the answer.

If the answer is wrong, the drawing usually shows where. That conversation is worth more than the right letter.

### The wrong answers matter

Every wrong option in our problems is a real mistake children make: counting someone twice, forgetting a repeat, mixing up the tens. Spotting why an option is wrong is as useful as finding the right one.

In class we spend time on the wrong options on purpose, so that children learn to check rather than guess.

## An eight-week plan before a spring contest

Our plan for a child preparing for a Kangaroo contest in the spring, counted back from the date their own host centre confirms. It assumes two live classes a week; it is ours, not the organizer's.

1. Weeks 1 and 2: drawing. The four drawing moves, one problem at a time, with no clock. The goal is a picture for every problem, even easy ones.
2. Weeks 3 and 4: the grade's own math. Any gap in the child's school math for their grade, found in week 1, gets fixed here, because the contest problems are aligned with provincial curricula.
3. Weeks 5 and 6: the clock. Half-length papers of our own problems, timed with our three-part time plan, so the easy first third is done quickly.
4. Week 7: a full rehearsal. One full-length timed paper of our problems for the child's version, then every wrong answer reviewed by redrawing it.
5. Week 8: rest and confidence. Short, enjoyable sessions only, and nothing new in the last few days. A relaxed child reads more carefully.

### Grades 1 and 2

Shorter sessions, more drawing, a parent nearby. The 45-minute paper is long for a six-year-old, so we build stamina gently.

### Grades 3 to 8

The full plan. These versions reward careful reading most, and the drawing habit pays off quickly.

### Grades 9 to 12

Less drawing practice, more algebra and counting, and links to the CEMC and COMC contests the same students often write.

## Four stages of a young problem solver

Our stages, used in the first class to place a child.

**From quick guesser to careful thinker (ours)**

| Stage | What we see |
|---|---|
| 1. Quick guesser | Answers fast, often with the wrong numbers from the story |
| 2. Drawer | Draws the problem when asked and gets more right |
| 3. Checker | Draws without being asked and checks answers against the picture |
| 4. Explorer | Tries the hard last-third problems and explains an approach even when unsure |

### For parents

Most bright young children start at stage 1, because quick answers are praised at school. Moving to stage 3 takes a few months of patient practice and is worth far more than any contest result.
Math Kangaroo is a good first contest because it is meant to be enjoyable. Keep it that way at home.

### For children

Draw it before you work it out.
If two answers look right, check them both against your drawing.

## Math Kangaroo Canada: frequently asked questions

### What is Math Kangaroo Canada?

It is the Canadian edition of the international Kangaroo math contest, run by the Canadian Math Kangaroo Contest, a volunteer-run, not-for-profit organization. It is a multiple-choice contest for Grades 1 to 12, held yearly in more than 50 Canadian cities.

### When is Math Kangaroo Canada in 2027?

We could not read a national 2027 date from the organizer on 22 October 2026. One host centre, in the Fraser Valley, lists Sunday, March 21, 2027. Check the date with your own nearest centre.

### How long is the contest?

According to a host-centre page, Grades 1 and 2 answer 18 questions in 45 minutes, Grades 3 and 4 answer 24 in 60 minutes, and Grades 5 to 12 answer 30 in 75 minutes. Questions get harder as the paper goes on.

### Which version should my child write?

The one for their own grade. There are versions for Grades 1 and 2, 3 and 4, 5 and 6, 7 and 8, 9 and 10, and 11 and 12.

### Can I register my child myself?

Yes. The host-centre page says parents or teachers can register students, using the registration form on the national site. That is different from CEMC contests, which only schools can order.

### How is Math Kangaroo scored in Canada?

We could not find the Canadian scoring rules on any page we could read, so we do not describe them. Rules can differ between countries, because each national organizer runs its own contest.

### Is Math Kangaroo Canada the same as Math Kangaroo USA?

They belong to the same international family, but the international association says each country organizes its own competition. Dates, fees and rules can differ, so take Canadian details from the Canadian organizer.

### What kind of math is on the paper?

A host-centre page says the problems align with provincial curricula but require creativity, logical thinking or a different perspective. In our experience that means careful reading and picturing a problem matter as much as arithmetic.

### Do you guarantee a result?

No. We teach children to think through problems, and we make no promise of any score, rank or award.
