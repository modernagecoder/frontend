> One to one AI and coding classes put a single learner with the same teacher every week, on a written milestone map built from a goal interview and reviewed with the family monthly, at hours pinned to the learner's time zone. It is the right format for specific deadlines, outliers ahead or behind their age band, quiet students, impossible schedules, and the AI agents courses, which are private-only at every age because acting software needs individual supervision. It is the wrong format for learners who feed on peers, which is why our default advice remains the group batch. ## The honest matrix

One to one wins for: a deadline (exam, competition, interview), because the syllabus can sprint; a learner far ahead or behind their age band; a student who goes quiet in company, at least at first; a schedule no timetable fits; and the agents courses, where it is required. The group wins for: learners motivated by peers, rivals and demo days, and for beginners with no special constraints, where peers plus the lower fee beats bespoke most times. For every three families who ask about one to one, we recommend it to two and talk the third into a batch.

## The same stuck moment, both formats

In a group of seven: the crash waits three minutes for the teacher, the hint arrives ("read what line 12 believes"), and meanwhile a batchmate hits the same bug aloud, so two routes to one answer get seen by the whole room. Slower per student, richer per room. One to one: the teacher was already watching, the gap between belief and code surfaces in the student's own words within four minutes, and then the syllabus bends: the third state bug this month turns today into a state masterclass. The bending is the product. Neither panel wins; the learner decides which one is theirs.

## How a private syllabus actually gets built

1. The goal interview before any teaching: what a win looks like in six months, made concrete.
2. Placement by doing, not questionnaire: ten minutes of real building reads level accurately.
3. The milestone map, written and shared: three to six months of waypoints the family can read.
4. Weekly sessions that bend to the evidence: recurring weaknesses become dedicated sessions.
5. The monthly review with the family: what moved, what stalled, and the honest format conversation when needed.

## Frequently asked questions

**Lonely compared with a batch?** Different: a working partnership many students find more social, minus the peer audience. A term of each is a common pattern.

**Personalised, concretely?** A goal interview, placement by doing, a written milestone map, weekly plans rebuilt from last week's evidence, and monthly family reviews you can sit in on.

**Same teacher always?** Yes, by design; rotation defeats the format. Long-term handovers include the map and notes, and you meet the incoming teacher first.

**Switch formats later?** Both directions, as a conversation: private first months for adults, batch-to-private sprints before exams, quiet children finding their voice privately.

**Siblings share a session?** Each learner gets their own sessions; back-to-back scheduling keeps one household window.

**Which subjects are private-only?** The AI agents family at every age. Everything else runs in both formats.
