# We use competitions as deadlines. The medal is not the point.

> Competitions appear in the twelve-month track as forcing functions rather than as trophies. A fixed external submission date is the only constraint we have found that reliably gets a student project finished, documented and published rather than endlessly improved, and it also forces the least popular part of the work, which is writing it up. We prepare students for open-entry competitions whose timing and subject fit their current project. We make no promise about selection or results, because for school-age students entry frequently runs through the school and judging is not ours to influence.

## A project with no deadline expands until it dies

A student picks a project in week one. By week four it is going well. By week six they have thought of something that would make it better, and by week nine they have thought of three more things.

Nothing has been finished, nothing has been published, and the student is neither lazy nor unable. They have no reason to stop, and a project with no reason to stop never does.

A fixed external date fixes that, and almost nothing else does. Not a teacher saying it should be done by Friday, because a teacher can be negotiated with. A submission deadline set by somebody who has never heard of the student is the only constraint we have found that reliably converts work into a finished thing.

That is why competitions appear in this programme. The medal is not the point, and we would be selling you something dishonest if we implied it was.

## Four things we will not claim

**That we can get anybody selected.** For school-age students, entry to many competitions runs through the school rather than a private provider, and selection is decided by people we have no relationship with.

**That results are predictable.** They are not, and a provider quoting you a success rate on competitions is describing luck as a service.

**That a specific Bahamian competition exists for your child's age and subject.** We would have to check, it changes year to year, and your child's school will know better than we do.

**That winning matters more than finishing.** It does not, and treating it as though it does is how students end up with one polished entry and no portfolio.

## The five weeks before a date

| Week | What happens | Why then |
|---|---|---|
| Five | Scope frozen in writing, including what is not happening | This is where the entry is won or lost |
| Four | Made to run for somebody else, from clean instructions | It always takes longer than expected |
| Three | The error figure, and the baseline it is measured against | If it does not beat the baseline, better to know now |
| Two | The write-up, by the student, reviewed hard | Most entries are separated at this step |
| One | Stop. Submit early. No new features | It removes the entire category of midnight disasters |

The sequence is roughly the opposite of what most people do, which is to build until the last night and write up in a panic.

## What a student gains without placing

Most entrants in any competition do not win, so a programme that only pays off on a win is a bad programme.

What survives regardless is the finished project: something running, documented and published, which goes in the repository and stays useful for years after the result is forgotten. Alongside it, the experience of being assessed by somebody with no relationship to you, which is a shock the first time and better met at fourteen than at twenty-two.

And the write-up, which is the quiet one. Nobody documents anything voluntarily. A deadline makes it the last obstacle to being finished, and the habit tends to survive afterwards.
