## A Lorenz curve for storm overflows

Learners sort 14,238 overflows from most hours to least and ask what share of all spilling the top few account for.

### 1. Sort and add up

Order the overflows by hours spilled, biggest first, and keep a running share of the total as you go down the list.

### 2. Draw the curve

Plot the share of overflows against the share of hours. Perfect equality is a straight diagonal; the further the curve bows away, the more concentrated the spilling.

### 3. One number

The Gini coefficient is the gap between the curve and the diagonal, from 0 for perfect equality to 1 for one overflow doing everything.

**Share of all 2025 spill hours, run 25 September 2026**

| Busiest overflows | How many | Share of spill hours |
|---|---|---|
| Top 1% | 142 | 16.6% |
| Top 5% | 712 | 46.4% |
| Top 10% | 1,424 | 64.6% |
| Top 20% | 2,848 | 82.5% |
| Top half | 7,119 | 98.3% |

One overflow in ten accounts for nearly two thirds of all the hours; one in five accounts for more than four fifths. Eighty per cent of the spilling comes from just 18% of overflows, close to the famous 80/20 rule. The Gini coefficient for spill hours is 0.789. Counting spills instead of hours gives 0.608, less extreme because a count caps how much one long spill can weigh, yet the two measures still rank overflows very similarly, with a rank correlation of 0.91.

### Why a chatbot summary is not the same

Ask an AI tool to summarise storm overflows and it may quote a national total or a headline average. An average hides exactly what this data shows: most overflows spill little and a small minority spill a great deal. A learner who has drawn the Lorenz curve knows to ask how a total is shared, not just how big it is, which matters for rivers, incomes, website traffic and almost everything else measured in large numbers.

## Three things to check before drawing conclusions

Big public datasets reward care. Learners write these checks into their code.

### Missing monitoring

At 1,020 overflows the monitor worked less than 90% of the year, so some totals are too low. Learners flag those rows rather than trusting them.

### Rows without a time

64 rows had no usable duration. They are left out and counted, never silently dropped.

### Hours are not harm

The return records how long an overflow spilled, not how much or how diluted. Concentration of hours is a fact about time, not a verdict on pollution.

Pupils studying GCSE maths or geography can follow the cumulative percentages and the curve; sixth formers can compute the Gini coefficient themselves and compare it across years. ## AI can summarise any spreadsheet. Why should a Bexley teenager learn to do it properly?

Because the most important fact in a dataset is often hidden by its average.

Hand an AI assistant the storm overflow return and ask for a summary, and you may get a total and an average per overflow. Both are true and both mislead: the typical overflow spills far less than the average, and a small group spills most of the time. The Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient exist precisely to show that shape. A learner who has built them from 14,238 rows can see through a summary that hides it, whoever or whatever wrote it.

The habits carry over to every field: check how a total is shared, flag records with missing monitoring, and say clearly what a measure does and does not capture. AI tools will keep writing summaries. People who can tell a fair summary from a flattering one will keep being needed.

Bexley has lived with London's sewage engineering since 1865. A learner here who can read the modern records honestly is carrying on a very Bexley kind of work. The longer argument is in [why teenagers should still learn to code in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
