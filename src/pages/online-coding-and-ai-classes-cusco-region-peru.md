### The region's own answer

The Gerencia Regional de Educación Cusco knows the gap. On 27 May 2026 it announced CREE, a regional centre for specialised educational resources serving more than 360,000 students, with a virtual platform called PLADEC built, in the office's words, to overcome the connectivity gaps that affect rural communities.

GEREDU Cusco describes PLADEC as offline-first, with artificial intelligence built in as an assistant for teachers. That is a school-system tool; our classes are something different, live lessons for families who already have a usable connection at home.

### A practical checklist

For a live class you need a connection that holds a video call for about an hour, a laptop or desktop for anything typed, and a quiet corner. A tablet with a keyboard is enough for younger children.

If your signal drops in the evening, choose a morning slot, when Cusco mornings line up with the Indian evening. If the connection is shared with siblings doing homework, one to one at an agreed hour is often easier than a group. We would rather tell you honestly at the free class than sell a course your connection cannot carry.

### Where coding is missing

The national curriculum, the CNEB, puts digital skills in competency 28, "Se desenvuelve en entornos virtuales generados por las TIC", which is transversal: every subject is meant to use it, but no subject owns it. The regular secundaria week gives Matemática 4 hours and Inglés 3, and has no computing or programming area at all.

MINEDU's Python pilot, Fábrica de Programadores, began in July 2025 with 1,000 pupils of 4° and 5° de secundaria in Lima Norte. It has not reached Cusco, so a Cusco teenager who wants the same path learns it outside school.

### The size of the school system

In 2025 Cusco had 121.3 thousand pupils in public primaria and 99.9 thousand in public secundaria, with 27,832 teachers across all levels and sectors. Across the country, 6,225,000 students and 53,631 schools started the year on 16 March 2026.

Primaria lasts six years and secundaria five, under Law 28044, and MINEDU places children in a grade by their age on 31 March, so a six year old by that date starts 1° de primaria.

Strong maths results are a good reason to add coding, not to skip it. A pupil who is already comfortable with fractions and proportion is exactly the pupil who enjoys turning them into programs, and who is ready for the national contests: the ONEM for maths, whose 2026 school stage ran on 8 July 2026 with ten questions in two hours, and the OPI for programming, which UTEC runs free for every secundaria student and whose 2025 final banned generative AI. Students enter both through the organisers' own routes; we are not connected with either. ## What is the average quinoa yield in the Cusco region?

The regional agriculture office publishes its crop figures, district by district, as open data. Our students use the quinoa rows to learn something that catches out adults too: the average of 84 districts' yields is not the yield of the region.

### The data

The file is "Producción Agrícola periodos 2023 al 2025 en la Región de Cusco", released by the Gerencia Regional de Agricultura Cusco (GERAGRI) on the Plataforma Nacional de Datos Abiertos. Its licence is ODC-By, the Open Data Commons Attribution Licence, which allows reuse with credit. We downloaded it on 9 November 2026.

It has 3,672 rows covering 75 crops in two farming seasons, 2023-2024 and 2024-2025. Each row gives a district, a crop, the hectares sown and harvested, the tonnes produced, and a yield column, Rendimiento, in kilograms per hectare.

### The question

Quinoa is grown in 84 districts in the 2023-2024 season. A student wants one number: how many kilograms does a hectare of quinoa give in the Cusco region?

The first idea is natural. There is already a yield column, so take the 84 yields and average them.

**Version 1: average the district yields** (Python)
```python
import csv

yields = []
with open("DataSet_Estadistica_Agricola_2023_2024_2025.csv", encoding="latin-1") as fh:
    for line in csv.DictReader(fh, delimiter=";"):
        if line["Cultivo_Agricola"].strip() == "QUINUA" and line["Campania_Agricola"] == "2023-2024":
            yields.append(float(line["Rendimiento"]))

print("Districts growing quinoa:", len(yields))
print("Average yield, kg per hectare:", round(sum(yields) / len(yields), 1))
```
It prints Districts growing quinoa: 84 and Average yield, kg per hectare: 1494.2.

That average treats every district as equal. A district that harvested two hectares counts exactly as much as one that harvested five hundred. But a hectare in a big quinoa district is just as much a hectare as one in a small district, so the honest regional yield is all the quinoa produced divided by all the land it came from.

**Version 2: total production divided by total harvested area** (Python)
```python
import csv

tonnes = 0.0
hectares = 0.0
fields = []
with open("DataSet_Estadistica_Agricola_2023_2024_2025.csv", encoding="latin-1") as fh:
    for line in csv.DictReader(fh, delimiter=";"):
        if line["Cultivo_Agricola"].strip() == "QUINUA" and line["Campania_Agricola"] == "2023-2024":
            tonnes += float(line["Produccion"])
            hectares += float(line["Cosechada"])
            fields.append((float(line["Cosechada"]), line["Distrito"], float(line["Rendimiento"])))

print("Harvested:", hectares, "ha, produced:", round(tonnes, 1), "t")
print("Yield of the region, kg per hectare:", round(1000 * tonnes / hectares, 1))
fields.sort(reverse=True)
print("Biggest grower:", fields[0])
```
It prints Harvested: 2469.0 ha, produced: 4624.3 t, Yield of the region, kg per hectare: 1873.0 and Biggest grower: (525.0, 'COLQUEMARCA', 3230.48).

The answer rises from 1,494.2 to 1,873.0 kilograms per hectare, a difference of almost 380. The reason is in the last line. Colquemarca, in the province of Chumbivilcas, harvested 525 of the region's 2,469 hectares, more than a fifth, at a yield of 3,230 kilograms per hectare. In Version 1 it was one district in 84; in Version 2 it carries the weight of its land. Neither number is a lie. They answer different questions: "what does a typical district get?" and "what does a typical hectare give?"

The sums and ratios are ours, from the 2023-2024 quinoa rows. The file is Latin-1 encoded with semicolons between fields, so it must be opened with encoding="latin-1" and delimiter=";".

There is a second lesson in the same file, and it is the reason we only used 2023-2024. Yield should equal production divided by area, so a careful student checks it on every row before trusting the column.

**A check: does the yield column equal production divided by area?** (Python)
```python
import csv

checked = {}
wrong = {}
with open("DataSet_Estadistica_Agricola_2023_2024_2025.csv", encoding="latin-1") as fh:
    for line in csv.DictReader(fh, delimiter=";"):
        ha = float(line["Cosechada"] or 0)
        if ha == 0:
            continue
        season = line["Campania_Agricola"]
        worked_out = 1000 * float(line["Produccion"] or 0) / ha
        written = float(line["Rendimiento"] or 0)
        checked[season] = checked.get(season, 0) + 1
        if abs(worked_out - written) > 0.01 * max(written, 1):
            wrong[season] = wrong.get(season, 0) + 1

for season in sorted(checked):
    print(season, "rows checked:", checked[season], "yield disagrees:", wrong.get(season, 0))
```
It prints 2023-2024 rows checked: 1778 yield disagrees: 0 and 2024-2025 rows checked: 1770 yield disagrees: 1770.

In 2023-2024 the columns agree on every harvested row. In 2024-2025 they disagree on every single one, by more than 1%. We do not know why, and the page does not guess: the data dictionary may describe a different unit, or the columns may have shifted. Until the publisher explains it, the 2024-2025 yields are left out of anything we teach as a result. Checking that the numbers agree with each other is part of the job, not an extra.

1. **Under 9, with beans.** One bag holds 2 handfuls of beans from 1 small pot; another holds 30 handfuls from 10 pots. What is the "average per pot" if you average the bags, and if you count all the pots together?
2. **Scratch, around 9 to 11.** Store three districts' hectares and tonnes in two lists, then show both averages side by side on the stage.
3. **First Python, around 11 to 13.** Run Version 1 and Version 2 and write one sentence for each saying what question it answers.
4. **Ages 13 and over.** Repeat both versions for potatoes or maize amiláceo in 2023-2024. Which crop shows the biggest gap between the two averages, and why?
5. **Going further.** Read the GERAGRI data dictionary and propose an explanation for the 2024-2025 yields, then test it against the rows before believing it.

## When could a Cusco learner have classes?

At any hour that suits the family. Cusco runs on Peru time, five hours behind UTC throughout the year, and the mentor adjusts.

**Sample conversions from Cusco to the mentor's clock in India (a conversion aid, not a timetable)**

| Cusco (Peru time) | Mentor (India time) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 07:30 | 18:00 | Evening in India |
| 09:30 | 20:00 | Evening in India |
| 12:00 | 22:30 | Late evening in India |
| 15:30 | 02:00 | India is already on the next day |
| 19:00 | 05:30 | India is already on the next day |

Peru: UTC minus 5, no daylight saving since 1994 (tz database, America/Lima). India: UTC plus 5:30, no daylight saving. The difference is 10 hours 30 minutes every day of the year.

Because the gap never shifts, a time chosen once stays the same through the Peruvian summer break and the school year that follows. You book in Cusco time; the mentor sees the India time on their side. Mornings suit families whose connection is busier in the evening; afternoons suit teenagers after colegio.
