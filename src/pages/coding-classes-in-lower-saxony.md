## When do pupils in Lower Saxony start Informatik at school?

All pupils in grade 9; some much earlier, depending on school type and choices. The detail below is from the Informatik-Monitor 2026/27 of the Gesellschaft für Informatik, which reads the Land's ordinances. We are independent of both the GI and the Kultusministerium.

Since 2024/25, Informatik has been a compulsory subject in grades 9 and 10 at all general school types in Niedersachsen. Lower Saxony has five school types in the Sekundarstufe I, after four years of Grundschule: Hauptschule, Realschule, Oberschule, Gesamtschule (integrated or cooperative) and Gymnasium. Each offers earlier Informatik in its own way.

Put the table together and a pattern appears. A pupil at a Gymnasium that runs only the standard timetable, who does not pick the Wahlfach, can reach grade 9 without a timetabled Informatik lesson.

### Few take it further

The monitor's national comparison names Niedersachsen, with Baden-Württemberg and Bremen, among the Länder where only about one Oberstufe pupil in twelve takes Informatik. On its Niedersachsen page it gives 9 percent of Sekundarstufe II pupils in 2024/25.

In 2025/26 the Land had 544 qualified Informatik teachers, about 1 percent of its teachers.

## How is the Informatik Abitur set in Lower Saxony, and when is it in 2027?

Centrally, on paper, with almost nothing on the desk. The Kultusministerium has also published the full Abitur calendar for 2027.

### Routes through the Oberstufe

Niedersachsen's Oberstufe is nine-year: grade 11 is the Einführungsphase, grades 12 and 13 the Qualifikationsphase, at the Gymnasium and the Gesamtschule. In grade 11 Informatik can be a two-hour elective.

In the Qualifikationsphase, within the maths and science Schwerpunkt, it can run for five hours at raised level as the second Schwerpunktfach, or three hours at basic level as an Ergänzungsfach.

### What the exam allows

The monitor states that written Abitur tasks in Informatik are set centrally and that no task may require a computer. A calculator and a formula collection are not allowed either.

The one aid is a centrally issued, printed information sheet that summarises, for example, operations on abstract data types and the notation for database queries. Pupils have to know everything else.

1. **Grade 11.** Take Informatik as the two-hour elective in the Einführungsphase, if the school offers it.
2. **Choose the Schwerpunkt.** In the maths and science Schwerpunkt, Informatik can be the second Schwerpunktfach for five hours, examined in writing.
3. **Or the Ergänzungsfach.** Taken for three hours from the Einführungsphase on, it can be the fourth, written exam subject or the fifth, oral one.
4. **Prepare for paper.** No computer and no calculator: practise reading and writing code by hand, with only the information sheet beside you.

## How many Hannover streets are a "Weg"? A Python project on street names

The Land capital publishes its official street directory as open data. Our pupils use it to learn why "contains" and "ends with" are different questions, and why a count is only finished when you have looked at the cases it got wrong.

### The data

The Landeshauptstadt Hannover's Bereich Geoinformation publishes the Straßenverzeichnis, the complete list of the city's streets, paths and squares with their street keys, under Creative Commons Namensnennung 4.0. We downloaded the January 2026 edition on 3 November 2026.

The spreadsheet has one row per stretch of street, so long streets appear more than once. We exported the main sheet to a file called strassen_2026.tsv and keep one name per street key, STRNR.

### The question

German street names end with what they are: -straße, -weg, -platz, -hof, -kamp. How many of Hannover's streets are a Weg?

The quick program asks whether the letters "weg" appear in the name. It runs, and it gives a number. The second program asks whether the name ends in "weg", and gets a smaller one. The difference is the lesson.

**Version 1: one name per street, then "contains"** (Python)
```python
import csv

streets = {}
with open("strassen_2026.tsv", encoding="utf-8") as src:
    for row in csv.DictReader(src, delimiter="\t"):
        if row["STRNR"]:
            streets[row["STRNR"]] = row["STRASSENNAME"].strip()

print("Streets:", len(streets))
print("Containing 'weg':", sum("weg" in n.lower() for n in streets.values()))
```
It prints: Streets: 3650 and Containing 'weg': 877.

**Version 2 (continues Version 1): "ends with", and the leftovers** (Python)
```python
names = list(streets.values())
ends = [n for n in names if n.lower().endswith("weg")]
inside = [n for n in names if "weg" in n.lower() and not n.lower().endswith("weg")]

print("Ending in 'weg':", len(ends))
print("False matches:", len(inside), inside[:4])
```
It prints: Ending in 'weg': 860 and False matches: 17 ['Am Graswege', 'August-Holweg-Platz', 'Diesterwegstraße', 'Frenswegengang'].

Seventeen streets contain the letters without being a Weg. Diesterwegstraße is a street named after a person; August-Holweg-Platz is a square; Am Graswege has the old dative ending. None of them is wrong data. The first program simply asked a looser question than the one we meant.

**Version 3 (continues Version 2): five endings compared** (Python)
```python
for word in ["straße", "weg", "hof", "kamp", "feld"]:
    anywhere = sum(word in n.lower() for n in names)
    at_end = sum(n.lower().endswith(word) for n in names)
    print(word, anywhere, at_end)
```
It prints: straße 1531 1522, weg 877 860, hof 204 164, kamp 83 50 and feld 96 28.

**Hannover street names, January 2026 edition: "contains" against "ends with" (our count)**

| Word | Names containing it | Names ending in it | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| straße | 1,531 | 1,522 | 9 |
| weg | 877 | 860 | 17 |
| hof | 204 | 164 | 40 |
| kamp | 83 | 50 | 33 |
| feld | 96 | 28 | 68 |

Data: Landeshauptstadt Hannover, Bereich Geoinformation, Straßenverzeichnis, January 2026 edition, CC BY 4.0 (Datenquelle: Landeshauptstadt Hannover), downloaded 3 November 2026. One name per street key; all counts are ours.

"feld" is the striking row: only 28 of the 96 names that contain it end with it. Many of the other 68 are a -straße or a -weg with a field name in front, such as Lerchenfeldstraße or Klaterfeldweg, and a good number use the old form -felde, as in Im Roggenfelde. A pupil who reports 96 fields has counted something real, just not fields.

1. **Grades 5 and 6, no computer.** Write ten street names from your own town. Sort them by their last word or ending. Which endings could fool someone who only looks for letters?
2. **Grades 7 and 8, Python.** Run Version 1 and Version 2, then print all seventeen false matches and sort them into groups: person, place, other.
3. **Grades 9 and 10.** "Straße der Nationen" contains "straße" but does not end with it. Write a test list of awkward names and check that your function treats each one as you intend.
4. **Oberstufe.** Rewrite the check as a regular expression that matches "weg" only at the end of a word, and compare its count with Version 2.
