# Coding classes in Barranco, Lima

Barranco is small. INEI's 2025 census puts it among the smallest districts of Lima, with only a couple of thousand children of primary age and a couple of thousand teenagers. That is wonderful for a neighbourhood and awkward for anyone looking for a specialist class taught in English: there are rarely enough learners of one age and level nearby to fill one. ## Why should someone who draws, writes or makes music learn to code?

Because code is a material, like paint or clay, with properties you can only learn by working it. Once you can write a few lines, a computer stops being a tool you use and becomes something you can shape.

### What it opens up

An illustrator can generate a thousand variations of a pattern and keep the few that please the eye; a photographer can rename, resize and sort a whole shoot in seconds; a writer can build an interactive story; a musician can program a rhythm that changes every bar.

None of this needs a computer science degree. It needs the foundations, taught properly: variables, loops, conditions, lists and functions, then a library for the medium the learner cares about.

### Why learn it live

Creative coding is full of small, puzzling failures: a shape drawn off the edge, a colour that never changes, an animation that freezes. A mentor watching your screen spots the cause in seconds and, more usefully, asks the question that lets you spot it yourself.

And because the class is in English, the learner meets the vocabulary of the documentation, the forums and the libraries in the language they are written in.

We teach learners to think through a problem rather than copy an answer, from a tutorial or from an AI tool. Adults in our classes use AI assistants and learn to check what they produce; children stay off adult chatbot accounts, since Claude sets 18 as its minimum age and ChatGPT asks for a parent's consent below 18.

## What does a lesson look like? How a paint bucket decides what to fill

Every drawing program has a paint-bucket tool: click inside a shape and the colour floods to its edges. We teach how it works to learners of about 12 and up, and it turns on a question an artist would ask: do two squares that touch only at a corner belong to the same shape?

### The picture

Our picture, drawn for the lesson, is a small grid of squares: a 2 by 2 block in the top-left corner, a diagonal line of three squares running down from the top-right, a second 2 by 2 block lower down that the diagonal touches at one corner, and a single square on its own at the bottom right.

An artist looking at it sees three shapes: the first block, the diagonal joined to the second block, and the lone square. The program has to decide for itself.

### How the fill works

The program finds a filled square it has not seen, then keeps a stack of squares to visit. It takes one from the stack, counts it, and pushes every filled neighbour it has not seen yet. When the stack is empty, one shape is finished, and it records the size.

The seen set is essential: without it the fill would visit the same squares back and forth for ever. Marking a square as seen when it is pushed, not when it is popped, also stops any square being counted twice.

**Version 1: a fill that moves up, down, left and right** (Python)
```python
picture = [
    "##....#",
    "##...#.",
    "....#..",
    "..##...",
    "..##..#",
]
STEPS = [(-1, 0), (1, 0), (0, -1), (0, 1)]

def count_shapes(rows, steps):
    seen = set()
    shapes = []
    for r in range(len(rows)):
        for c in range(len(rows[0])):
            if rows[r][c] == "#" and (r, c) not in seen:
                size = 0
                stack = [(r, c)]
                seen.add((r, c))
                while stack:
                    y, x = stack.pop()
                    size += 1
                    for dy, dx in steps:
                        ny, nx = y + dy, x + dx
                        if (0 <= ny < len(rows) and 0 <= nx < len(rows[0])
                                and rows[ny][nx] == "#" and (ny, nx) not in seen):
                            seen.add((ny, nx))
                            stack.append((ny, nx))
                shapes.append(size)
    return shapes

print("Four directions:", count_shapes(picture, STEPS))
```
It prints Four directions: [4, 1, 1, 1, 4, 1].

Six shapes, not three. The fill can only move up, down, left or right, so the diagonal line falls apart into three single squares, and none of them can reach the lower block. Nothing is broken in the code; it answered a different question from the one the artist asked. The fix is to say what **touching** means.

**Version 2: let the paint pass through corners** (Python)
```python
# picture, STEPS and count_shapes() as in Version 1
DIAGONALS = STEPS + [(-1, -1), (-1, 1), (1, -1), (1, 1)]
print("Four directions:", count_shapes(picture, STEPS))
print("Eight directions:", count_shapes(picture, DIAGONALS))
print("Squares painted:", sum(count_shapes(picture, STEPS)), sum(count_shapes(picture, DIAGONALS)))
```
With Version 1's picture, STEPS and function above it, it prints Four directions: [4, 1, 1, 1, 4, 1], Eight directions: [4, 7, 1] and Squares painted: 12 12.

With eight directions, corners count as touching. The diagonal joins up and links to the lower block, giving one shape of seven squares, and the count matches the artist's three. The last line is the check every learner should write: whichever rule we use, all 12 filled squares are painted exactly once. Only the grouping changes.

Real paint programs choose deliberately. Many fill with four directions, because otherwise colour leaks through a diagonal line meant as a border; the same choice appears in maps, games and medical images. There is no universally right answer, only a rule you have to state. That is the lesson: before you count shapes, decide what makes two pieces one.

1. **Ages 6 to 8, with crayons.** Colour some squares on squared paper. Ask a friend how many shapes there are. Do you agree about the squares that only touch at a corner?
2. **Ages 9 to 11, in Scratch.** Draw a maze with the pen, and make a sprite that can only move up, down, left and right. Can it squeeze between two walls that meet at a corner?
3. **Ages 12 to 14, in Python.** Change the picture so that four directions and eight directions give the same count. What did you have to remove?
4. **Ages 15 and over.** Rewrite the fill as a recursive function, then try it on a 200 by 200 picture that is entirely filled. Explain, in English, why Python complains, and why the stack version does not.

## Why learn online from Barranco rather than at a centre elsewhere in Lima?

For a small district, the alternative to an online class is usually a journey to a bigger one. A live class at home keeps the time for learning and costs no travel at all.

### The trip you skip

We do not publish traffic estimates without a source, and Barranco residents hardly need them. Whatever the route, an hour-long class elsewhere means an afternoon organised around getting there and back.

At home, the learner sits down, logs in and starts. A parent working nearby can glance over; nobody needs to drive.

### What you need

A laptop or desktop computer with a keyboard, camera and microphone, and a steady connection. INEI's household survey for the second quarter of 2026 reported internet in 84.7% of homes in Lima Metropolitana, which for INEI includes Callao, and at least one computer in 53.3%.

For creative work a mouse helps, and headphones help everyone. A phone is fine for following a class but not for writing code.

Plenty of Lima adults use English daily, and EF's English Proficiency Index 2025 rates the city of Lima "High" at 555, against 519 for Peru as a whole. ## Coding classes in Barranco: questions and answers

### Are the classes in Spanish?

No. Every class is live, online and taught only in English. Mentors speak clearly, adapt to the learner's pace and accent, and show each step on screen, so children and adults whose English is still growing can follow. Code is written in English keywords anyway, so class and code share a language.

### Is Barranco too small for a proper class?

Not online. Groups are formed from learners of the same age and level in many places, so a Barranco learner joins a full group of fifteen to twenty from the start, or chooses one-to-one lessons.

### What age can my child start?

From 6, with Scratch, where blocks snap together and little typing is needed. From about 9, most children can move to typed Python. Teenagers and adults start directly with Python, JavaScript or web building.

### When are classes in Barranco time?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India is 10 hours 30 minutes ahead of Peru on every date, so 09:30 in Barranco is 20:00 in India.

### I am an adult designer with no coding background. Where do I start?

Usually with HTML and CSS if you want a website, or creative coding if you want to make images and motion. Both start from zero. If you want to automate work, start with Python. The free first class helps you choose.

### Do you have a studio or centre in Barranco?

No. We have no premises in Barranco or anywhere in Peru. Every class is live over video with a mentor based in India, which is how a small district gets the same classes as a large one.

### Do children use AI tools in your classes?

Only what suits their age, and never before they have reasoned the problem out themselves. Claude is limited to users aged 18 or older, and ChatGPT requires parental consent for minors, so we keep children off adult chatbot accounts.

### Are you connected with the municipality or any school?

No. We are an independent online school, not connected with the Municipalidad de Barranco, MINEDU or any school, and we do not award Peruvian qualifications. Learners who complete a course receive our own certificate of completion.

### How are fees paid?

Monthly, by card, in US dollars. If the card is in soles, your bank converts each charge, so the amount can vary slightly between months. The first class is free and needs no card.
