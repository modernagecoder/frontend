Oxford MAT preparation in 2026: the MAT is now the TMUA

Anyone searching for Oxford MAT preparation this year needs one fact before anything else: the Mathematics Admissions Test has finished. Oxford ran it from 2007 to 2025, and the Mathematical Institute now states that from 2026 the MAT will not take place and every applicant for Mathematics, Computer Science or a joint honours course sits the TMUA instead. The old papers have not become useless. Oxford itself says the topics are very similar and the spirit of problem solving is shared. But the paper on the day is different in shape: forty multiple-choice questions over two papers, with no long written answers. This page sets out what changed, the 2026 dates Oxford publishes, how to use nineteen years of MAT papers sensibly, and what follows the test.

The Oxford MAT (Mathematics Admissions Test) no longer exists: the Mathematical Institute used it from 2007 to 2025 and says that from 2026 all applicants for Mathematics, Computer Science and joint honours courses take the TMUA, administered by UAT-UK, in the 12 to 16 October 2026 sitting. The TMUA is two 75-minute papers of 20 multiple-choice questions each, with no calculator. Oxford says MAT topics are very similar, so MAT papers remain useful practice. What changed

Is the Oxford MAT still running in 2026?

No. The Mathematical Institute says the MAT will not take place in 2026, and Oxford applicants for Mathematics, Computer Science and the joint honours courses sit the TMUA instead. The notice sits at the top of the MAT page itself.

The wording is direct: "From 2026, Oxford will use the TMUA. The MAT will not take place, and all applicants for Mathematics or Computer Science (including joint honours courses) will take the TMUA instead." The Department of Computer Science says the same on its own how-to-apply page, and adds that its students "take the same test as those applying for Mathematics". So there is no separate Oxford route that keeps the MAT alive for one course.

What has not changed

The purpose. Oxford describes the TMUA in almost exactly the words it once used for the MAT: a test of "the depth of mathematical understanding of a student in the fourth term of their A-levels (or equivalent) rather than a breadth of knowledge", set to be approachable without Further Maths and from other school systems.

The use. Oxford still cannot interview everyone. It shortlists "around three applicants per place", and the test score is read together with the UCAS application and information about school background. There is no pass mark under either test.

What has changed

The written answer has gone. The MAT closed with two long questions worth 15 marks each, typed out, marked by a team in Oxford with partial credit. Thirty of a hundred marks rewarded explaining yourself. The TMUA rewards only the choice you click.

The timing per question has changed with it. Our arithmetic: the MAT gave 150 minutes for 100 marks, roughly a minute and a half a mark. The TMUA gives 75 minutes for 20 questions, which is three minutes and forty-five seconds a question, every question counting the same.

Sources: the Mathematical Institute Maths Admissions Test page and TMUA page, and the Department of Computer Science how to apply page, all read on 31 October 2026. The minutes per mark and per question are our own division. Modern Age Coders is not connected with the University of Oxford, UAT-UK or Pearson VUE.

Nineteen years of papers

Are MAT past papers still worth doing for the TMUA?

Yes, used the right way. Oxford says applicants who have looked at MAT papers "will find that the mathematical topics covered on the TMUA are very similar", and it still hosts every paper with solutions from 2007 onwards. What needs care is the long questions and the marking.

The archive also carries something no other admissions test publishes in one place: three averages for every year. Oxford labels them μ1, the mean MAT score of all applicants for Mathematics, Mathematics and Statistics and Mathematics and Philosophy; μ2, the mean of those shortlisted for interview; and μ3, the mean of those who were made offers. What the averages say

Across these five years, the shortlisted group averaged around seventeen marks more than the whole field, while the offer group averaged only four to seven marks more than the shortlisted group. The differences are ours, computed from Oxford's figures; Oxford draws no conclusion from them on the page.

Read cautiously, the pattern fits what Oxford says about the process: the test does most of its work at the shortlisting stage, and after that the interview and the rest of the application carry the decision. None of these are pass marks, and they belong to the MAT, not the TMUA, which reports on a different scale.

How to use the archive now

Treat the multiple-choice sections of MAT papers as close practice. They test the same A Level content with the same expectation of thought rather than routine, and Oxford updated the MAT syllabus for the 2018 test, so papers from 2018 onwards match current school content more closely.

Treat the long questions as interview practice rather than test practice. They are exactly the kind of extended problem a tutor might set in a December interview, and Oxford's own interview page suggests working a past MAT question aloud in front of a teacher.

Worked example, ours

One problem worked as a MAT long question, then as a TMUA item

The difference between the two tests is easiest to see on a single idea. This problem is our own, written for this page in the style of A Level calculus with a twist, and is not taken from any Oxford or UAT-UK paper.

Our problem. Let f(x) = x3 − 3x + c, where c is a real constant. For which values of c does the equation f(x) = 0 have three distinct real roots?

Step 1: where it turns

f′(x) = 3x2 − 3, which is zero at x = −1 and x = 1. So the cubic has a local maximum at x = −1 and a local minimum at x = 1, and nowhere else changes direction.

Step 2: the two heights

f(−1) = −1 + 3 + c = c + 2, and f(1) = 1 − 3 + c = c − 2. Three crossings need the maximum above the axis and the minimum below it: c + 2 > 0 and c − 2 < 0.

Step 3: the answer

Together, −2 < c < 2. At c = 2 or c = −2 a turning point touches the axis and two roots merge, so there are only two distinct roots. Outside the interval there is one.

As a long question

On the old MAT, the marks would have lived in the reasoning: why the two turning points decide everything, why touching the axis counts as a repeated root rather than two roots, and why a cubic with a maximum below the axis can cross only once. A student who reached the interval with no explanation would have lost marks; one who explained clearly and slipped on an endpoint would have kept most of them.

That is also the shape of a good interview answer. Say what you are looking for (the turning points), say why it matters (a continuous cubic must cross between a positive maximum and a negative minimum), and only then compute.

As a multiple-choice item

Now ask it the TMUA way: how many integer values of c give f(x) = 0 three distinct real roots? (A) 1 (B) 2 (C) 3 (D) 4 (E) 5. The integers strictly between −2 and 2 are −1, 0 and 1, so the answer is (C). We checked all nine integers from −4 to 4 numerically: exactly those three give three roots.

The trap is (E), which counts the endpoints. The same reasoning is needed, compressed into one sketch and one decision about strict inequalities. That compression is the skill to practise.

After the test

What happens after the TMUA for Oxford applicants?

Shortlisting in November, interviews online in December, decisions in January. Oxford's pages are unusually candid about what the interview is for and what it is not.

Oxford on special training

The Mathematical Institute's interview page says something every family should read before paying anyone, us included: "We very much don't want applicants to feel they need special training for our admissions." It continues: the main thing is to do mathematics, lots of it, and to find it interesting, working "lots of problems, on and near your syllabus".

We agree, and it shapes what we teach. We do not drill a test. We teach the mathematics on and near the syllabus properly, set problems that make a student stop and think, and give them a teacher who listens while they think aloud. If a school already offers that, a student may need nothing more.

Further Maths and the offer

Oxford asks for A Level Further Mathematics where a school offers it, and says that candidates without it may be made an offer only in exceptional circumstances: "In recent years we have admitted fewer than three such students per year." For a student at a school without Further Maths, that sentence matters more than any test score.

The offer is conditional and made in January on predicted grades. A student who has been offered a place still has the summer exams to meet, so interview preparation and A Level work have to run side by side through Year 13.

Sources: the Mathematical Institute interviews page and our offer page, and the Department of Computer Science how to apply page, read 31 October 2026. We make no promise of a shortlist, an interview or an offer.

If the test is weeks away

Sit the free practice papers under strict timing, then spend twice as long reviewing as sitting. Look for the questions you got right slowly, not only those you got wrong.

Practise the decision to move on. Three minutes forty-five seconds a question means a stuck question has to be abandoned, guessed and returned to, never stared at.

If the test is a year away

Do not start with test papers. Start with the A Level content done deeply, and with problems that have no obvious method, from MAT archives, STEP support material or olympiad sets.

Students who enjoy that kind of problem often enjoy the British Mathematical Olympiad, which uses the same muscles over three and a half hours.

Oxford MAT questions

What Oxford applicants and parents ask

Is the Oxford MAT still running?

No. The Mathematical Institute used the MAT from 2007 to 2025 and says that from 2026 the MAT will not take place. All applicants for Mathematics, Computer Science and joint honours courses take the TMUA instead.

What test do I take for Oxford maths in 2026?

The TMUA, the Test of Mathematics for University Admission, administered by UAT-UK. Oxford applicants must sit the October window, which in 2026 ran from 12 to 16 October.

Does Oxford Computer Science still use the MAT?

No. The Department of Computer Science says applicants register for the TMUA through UAT-UK, and that its students take the same test as Mathematics applicants. The MAT is not set for any Oxford course in 2026.

Are MAT past papers still useful?

Yes. Oxford says the TMUA covers mathematical topics very similar to the MAT and shares its spirit of problem solving, and it still publishes MAT papers with solutions. The long MAT questions are now better used as interview practice, because the TMUA has no written answers.

How is the TMUA different from the MAT?

The 2025 MAT had 27 questions, 25 multiple-choice and two long questions worth 15 marks each, marked out of 100. The TMUA has two 75-minute papers of 20 multiple-choice questions each, with no calculator and no written working.

What MAT score did you need for an Oxford interview?

There was never a pass mark. Oxford published averages instead: in 2025, all Mathematics applicants averaged 49.6, those shortlisted 66.7 and those offered places 74.0. These figures describe the MAT and do not carry over to the TMUA scale.

Do I need Further Maths to apply to Oxford for maths?

Oxford says Further Maths is highly recommended and expected where a school offers it. Applicants without it may be made an offer only in exceptional circumstances, and Oxford says it has admitted fewer than three such students per year recently.

What is an Oxford maths interview like?

Online by video, about 25 minutes each, with interviews at the first-choice college and at least one other. Tutors may ask about mathematics you have studied, a point of technique, a problem to talk through or a new definition to explore.

Do I need a course to prepare for Oxford?

Oxford says it does not want applicants to feel they need special training, and that the main thing is to do lots of mathematics and enjoy it. A class helps a student who lacks problems, a teacher to think aloud with, or Further Maths at school.

When can classes take place?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

Are you connected with the University of Oxford?

No. Modern Age Coders is an independent online school with no connection to the University of Oxford, UAT-UK or Pearson VUE. Applicants register and book the TMUA themselves through UAT-UK.
