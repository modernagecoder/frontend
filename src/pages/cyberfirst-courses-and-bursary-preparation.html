CyberFirst courses and bursary in 2026: what still runs, and how to prepare

Families searching for the CyberFirst bursary in 2026 meet a moved programme. The National Cyber Security Centre says CyberFirst "has now transitioned over to TechFirst", the government's wider tech skills programme, and its old CyberFirst pages now redirect or return errors. Some free NCSC courses survive under the CyberFirst name, the girls' competition has been renamed, and the bursary's place is taken by a TechFirst Undergraduate Scholarship whose application includes a technical aptitude assessment and an interview. This page sets out what the NCSC and GOV.UK pages said on 31 October 2026, and how a student can prepare without pretending to know an assessment nobody has published.

In short

CyberFirst, the National Cyber Security Centre's programme for young people since 2016, has transitioned into TechFirst, the UK government's tech skills programme. The NCSC still lists free CyberFirst courses, Defenders for ages 14 to 15 and Futures for 15 to 16 marked coming soon, while the old bursary is succeeded by the TechFirst Undergraduate Scholarship, which opens in November 2026 with an aptitude assessment and an interview. Modern Age Coders teaches cyber security and computing live online, in a group of 15 to 20 or 1-on-1.

From CyberFirst to TechFirst

Does CyberFirst still exist in 2026?

In part. The NCSC says the programme has transitioned to TechFirst under the leadership of DCMS, the government department named on the TechFirst guidance, and that its own focus is moving to Key Stage 5 students heading into cyber study or work. The CyberFirst name survives on some free courses and on international pilots.

Why searches still say CyberFirst

The NCSC says it has supported thousands of young people through CyberFirst since 2016, and it published a CyberFirst annual report and social value report for 2025 to 2026. Teachers, parents and older siblings still know the name, so the searches will continue for a while.

The useful question is not whether a page says CyberFirst but whether it is current. Any bursary or course page dated before the move should be checked against GOV.UK.

What we could not find

None of the pages we read on 31 October 2026 describes a CyberFirst Advanced course, a residential CyberFirst summer course, or a CyberFirst bursary application for 2027 entry. We are not saying they will never return; we are saying the official pages do not offer them today.

If a school or provider advertises one, ask which official page it comes from. The NCSC and GOV.UK pages are the authority.

Free and official

Several, and all are free. The NCSC lists the CyberFirst Defenders course for 14 to 15 year olds, signed up through IBM SkillsBuild, plus younger resources and a set of short browser activities. Through schools, TechFirst offers regional activities, Ada Computer Science and national challenges.

Defenders, in the NCSC's words

The NCSC calls Defenders "a valuable introduction on how to build and protect small networks and personal devices". Its listed topics are identifying common threats, first-line defences, constructing and securing a home network, and managing a digital footprint.

That is a sensible first course for any 14 year old, whatever they do next, and it costs nothing. A student who finishes it and wants more is ready for structured classes.

Through school, by region

GOV.UK says TechFirst reaches schools through regional delivery partners in every English region and in Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and that schools can apply for Bronze, Silver or Gold TechFirst School recognition.

A parent whose child's school is not involved can pass the regional contact to a computing teacher. Most TechFirst activity reaches students that way, not by individual sign-up.

What replaced the bursary

What is the TechFirst Undergraduate Scholarship, and who can apply?

It is the government's new funding scheme for students heading into tech: yearly financial support at a UK university for up to four years, summer placements and mentoring. School students can apply if they expect at least 120 UCAS tariff points and plan to start a full-time degree in September 2027. Applications open in November 2026.

  1. Check eligibility. Home fee status, a full-time UK degree starting September 2027, and a realistic 120 UCAS tariff points.
  2. Register interest now. GOV.UK says registering brings news of opening dates and support sessions, but "Registering your interest is not an application."
  3. Apply in full when applications open in November 2026. The full application is separate from UCAS.
  4. Complete the interactive learning activities. GOV.UK has not yet published what they involve.
  5. Sit the technical aptitude assessment. Its content is also unpublished; GOV.UK says more information will follow.
  6. Interview. The final stage named in the guidance.

Who it says it welcomes

GOV.UK says applicants do not need previous industry experience or extra technology qualifications, and that it particularly welcomes applicants from groups underrepresented in tech, including students from lower socio-economic backgrounds.

That makes it reachable for a student who has never been near a cyber club. Curiosity and the ability to reason are what the stages appear built to find.

Two names for one idea

The NCSC's page announces "A new TechGrad University Scholarship" launching in November 2026, for up to 1150 domestic students a year, and mentions summer placements through the "TechGrad Scholarship scheme". GOV.UK calls its scheme the TechFirst Undergraduate Scholarship.

Both describe yearly university funding with placements, opening in November 2026. Until the application site is live, treat the GOV.UK guidance as the reference for rules.

Preparing honestly

How do you prepare for the TechFirst technical aptitude assessment?

Broadly, because nobody outside the scheme knows its content yet. GOV.UK says further information will be available soon. The honest preparation is fluency in the ideas any technical aptitude test is likely to touch: number bases, logic, simple cryptography, networks and counting. The worked example and warm-ups below are ours.

Our worked example: why length beats variety. A password of 8 lowercase letters has 268 = 208,827,064,576 possibilities. Allowing upper case and digits as well (62 symbols) gives 628 = 218,340,105,584,896, about a thousand times more. But simply using 12 lowercase letters gives 2612 = 95,428,956,661,682,176, over four hundred times more than the 8-character mixed password.

The reasoning, not the numbers

Each extra character multiplies the possibilities by the size of the alphabet, so length grows the search space exponentially. Adding symbol types only enlarges the base. Four more lowercase letters multiply by 264 = 456,976; changing 26 symbols to 62 multiplies by (62/26)8, which is only about 1,046.

That is the kind of step an aptitude assessment rewards: turning a vague idea, "stronger password", into a count that can be compared.

What this does not mean

Real attackers rarely try every combination; they try common words and leaked passwords first, so a long password made of predictable words can still fall quickly. Counting shows the ceiling, not the whole story.

Saying that out loud in an interview, that a model has limits, is worth as much as the arithmetic.

Legal practice only

Every exercise we set runs on platforms built for practice or on the student's own machine. Trying techniques on systems you do not own or have permission to test is an offence under the Computer Misuse Act 1990, and we teach that before anything else.

Capture-the-flag competitions such as picoCTF exist so that students can learn attack techniques legally, inside a deliberately vulnerable sandbox.

Not only a cyber career

The scholarship covers any degree in any subject, and TechFirst spans AI, semiconductors and quantum as well as cyber. A student who discovers at 16 that they prefer machine learning has lost nothing.

What carries across all of these is the same: careful reasoning, comfort with numbers and logic, and the ability to explain both.

CyberFirst questions

What families ask about CyberFirst and TechFirst

Is the CyberFirst bursary still running?

We found no CyberFirst bursary on the official pages on 31 October 2026: the NCSC bursary page returns not found and the NCSC says CyberFirst has transitioned to TechFirst. The scheme for school leavers is now the TechFirst Undergraduate Scholarship.

What is TechFirst?

GOV.UK calls it the government's flagship tech skills programme, with strands for schools, apprenticeships, undergraduate scholarships, doctoral support, local employers and women. The NCSC remains its lead for the cyber pathway.

When do TechFirst scholarship applications open?

GOV.UK says applications open in November, for degrees starting in September 2027. Registering interest beforehand brings updates but does not count as an application.

What is in the TechFirst application?

GOV.UK lists interactive learning activities, a technical aptitude assessment and an interview. It has not yet published the content of the assessment and says more information will follow.

Who can apply for the TechFirst Undergraduate Scholarship?

UK Home Students applying for, or already on, a full-time degree at a UK university in any subject. School applicants must expect at least 120 UCAS tariff points over at most two years.

Are CyberFirst courses still available?

The NCSC lists CyberFirst Defenders, a free course for 14 to 15 year olds signed up through IBM SkillsBuild, and CyberFirst Futures for 15 to 16 year olds as coming soon. We found no CyberFirst Advanced course listed.

What happened to the CyberFirst Girls Competition?

GOV.UK says it ran from 2016 to 2025 and is now the TechFirst Girls Competition, for girls aged 12 to 13. The 2026 competition runs from 11 to 20 November 2026.

Do I need coding experience to apply?

GOV.UK says previous industry experience and extra technology qualifications are not required. Being comfortable with logic, numbers and basic computing will still help with an aptitude assessment.

What days are your classes on?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

Are you part of CyberFirst or TechFirst?

No. Modern Age Coders is an independent online school, not connected with the NCSC, GCHQ, DCMS, TechFirst, IBM or any delivery partner. We promise no scholarship or place.
