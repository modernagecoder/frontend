## The age map, from the providers' own terms

Under 13: no mainstream AI chat accounts exist, full stop, so a legitimate class for this age needs the child to hold no AI account at all. From 13 to 17: ChatGPT with parental permission, Google Gemini in most countries, Microsoft Copilot at 13 in the UK and US but 18 in India. From 18: everything opens, including Claude and Replit, which require adulthood everywhere. The full quoted audit is at /ai-tools-age-guide.

The single most useful question to put to any provider: which accounts will my child need, in whose name, and at what published minimum age? A crisp answer means they did the work. "Do not worry about that" means no.

An under-13 loses nothing real by the account rule: the describe, run, read, steer instinct transfers completely from Scratch and teacher-operated tools, which is how the Modern Age Coders kids course is built.

## What learning looks like from behind the chair

Working: the child reads the reply before running it, pauses when something is off, opens the code, mutters the cause, types one precise sentence, then tries to break their own app on purpose. The pauses are the learning.

Hollow: instant runs, longer and louder re-prompts, whole errors pasted blind, the project deleted, a bigger project started nightly. That child is not hopeless; they are unsupported.

Three dinner-table questions need no screen: what does your project do (real learners answer in specifics), what broke this week and how did you find it (debugging stories cannot be faked), and what would you build with one more week (real learners have a backlog).

## The four household rules

1. Nothing personal goes in a prompt: names, addresses, school, photos, passwords. Prompts may be stored and reviewed; once sent, never unsent.
2. Publishing needs a second pair of eyes: not "no publishing", but someone looks before a link goes public.
3. Accounts are created together and listed: which service, whose email, what age it requires. No account should exist that a parent learns about in a crisis.
4. Three failed prompts means stop and read: the rule that protects learning itself.

## The ten-minute monthly check

Watch the current project run, driven by the child. Ask for this month's best bug, the question that cannot be faked. Glance at the account list together. Ask what is public and open it on your own phone. End with the backlog question, on time, even mid-enthusiasm.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is this just more screen time?** Judge it by output: hours that leave finished things behind are working hours; hours that leave only fatigue are scrolling.

**Do I need to know code to supervise?** No. The three no-code questions above verify learning better than looking at the code would.

**What are the real risks, ranked?** Personal data in prompts, private things in published projects, thinking-dependence, and far behind those, the headline scenarios. The first three have boring, effective controls.

**My child only wants games.** Games are the best on-ramp there is: state, logic, input, scoring and unexpected players are everything serious software needs, attached to real motivation.

**Hidden costs?** Beginner and teen tracks run on free tool tiers. Advanced agent-tool courses that require paid subscriptions say so on their course pages before enrolment.

**Homework?** Two lines: school work follows school AI rules, and class projects are deliberately things no school assigns, so laundering does not arise.
