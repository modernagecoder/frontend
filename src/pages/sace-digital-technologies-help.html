In short

SACE Stage 2 Digital Technologies is a 20-credit subject (code 2DGT20) with four focus areas: computational thinking, design and programming, data analytics, and iterative project development. Project Skills counts 50 per cent and the Collaborative Project 20 per cent, both school assessed. The Individual Digital Solution, worth 30 per cent, is assessed externally.

Inside the subject outline

Four focus areas that are taught together

Everything here comes from the SACE Board Stage 2 Digital Technologies Subject Outline, read on 7 October 2026. The outline says students study all four focus areas, and that the areas are not intended to be taught independently.

Stage 2 Digital Technologies focus areas, as the outline describes them
Focus areaWhat the outline emphasisesWhere it shows up
1. Computational thinkingDecomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction and algorithm design, with algorithms written in pseudocode or drawn as a structure chart or flow chartEvery task, and the algorithm design required in the individual solution
2. Design and programmingProgram design, pseudocode, simple constructs, array processing and modularisation in a general-purpose language, with comments that explain the codeProgramming skills tasks and both digital solutions
3. Data analyticsCollecting, extracting, interpreting and modelling data sets to identify trends and make predictionsAt least one project skills task
4. Iterative project developmentBuilding a product, prototype or proof of concept in stages, following agile practices, with testing at each stageThe collaborative project and the individual solution

The outline frames the subject around problems of interest to the student, and it repeats that phrase often enough to be a design principle. A student who chooses a problem they genuinely care about writes a stronger designer's statement, because the statement asks what makes the solution effective and what could count as innovative.

Programming is assessed in a general-purpose programming language. The outline does not prescribe one, so schools choose. Python is common in South Australian classrooms, and the skills transfer: arrays, functions, files and a little object-oriented design if the student wants to explore it.

Data analytics is the area students most often underestimate. The outline asks for simple and complex data sets, patterns and trends, conclusions and predictions, which is statistics done in code rather than in a spreadsheet alone.

The subject is also part of the SACE Board's subject renewal program. Its renewal page lists Digital Technologies as a group 3 subject working through the Develop phase in 2026, so details may change for later cohorts. Students should always use the outline their school gives them for their own year.

Sources: SACE Board, Digital Technologies overview (credits, codes and weightings); Stage 2 Digital Technologies Subject Outline (docx) linked from that page; subject renewal page. Read on 7 October 2026.

How the result is built

Three assessment types, five or six pieces of evidence

The outline asks for five to six assessments in total, including the external one. Each limit below is copied from the outline, because these limits shape how a project has to be planned.

Stage 2 Digital Technologies assessment, from the subject outline
Assessment typeWeightWhat the student submitsLimits
AT1 Project Skills (school)50 per centThree or four tasks applying computational thinking, data analysis, design and programming, and iterative developmentTogether a maximum of 20 minutes in multimodal form
AT2 Collaborative Project (school)20 per centAn individual Project Solution Walkthrough and an individual Technical Walkthrough about a group solution built for a clientUp to 5 minutes and up to 3 minutes; recordings no more than 1GB
AT3 Individual Digital Solution (external)30 per centA solution or prototype with source code, design comments, algorithm design and a user interface or instructions, plus a designer's statementSolution no more than 1 GB and 15 minutes; statement up to 3 minutes oral or 500 words written

Inside Project Skills, the outline sets four floors: at least one task involves collaboration, at least one analyses data sets, at least one assesses programming skills, and in at least one the student discusses the ethics of data use or digital solutions. Teachers decide how those floors are met, which is why tasks differ between schools.

The Collaborative Project needs an identifiable client outside the student group, consulted throughout development. The group solution itself is presented and discussed but not submitted to the SACE Board. Each student's walkthroughs are what the teacher assesses.

The assessment design criteria have three groups. Computational thinking runs from CT1 to CT4, development and evaluation from DE1 to DE4, and research and ethics is RE1. The individual solution is assessed against CT1, CT2 and CT4, and DE1, DE2 and DE3.

Performance standards describe five levels of achievement, A to E. The outline also expects a broad definition of collaboration: face to face or online, with students, teachers, relatives or community members.

Weights, limits and criteria: Stage 2 Digital Technologies Subject Outline, sections on evidence of learning, assessment design criteria and the three assessment types, read on 7 October 2026. External assessment summary: SACE Board, Digital Technologies external assessment.

A worked lesson

Scoping a client project with MoSCoW

Iterative development only works if the first version is small enough to finish. MoSCoW is a common way to sort a client's wish list into Must have, Should have, Could have and Won't have this time. The client, requirements and hour estimates below are invented for teaching; the arithmetic is ours.

The client

A canteen manager wants students to pre-order lunch. The group has about 36 working hours left before walkthroughs are due, shared across the team.

The wish list

Ten requirements arrive in the first meeting. Every one sounds reasonable, and together they need 41 hours, which is more than the group has.

The decision

Sorting by MoSCoW keeps 34 hours of work, leaves a 2 hour buffer for testing, and tells the client plainly what waits for a later version.

An invented canteen pre-order project, sorted with MoSCoW (estimates in hours)
PriorityRequirementEstimateKept in version 1?
MustShow today's menu4Yes
MustOrder form with input validation6Yes
MustOrder list for canteen staff5Yes
MustSave orders to a file between sessions5Yes
ShouldSearch the menu3Yes
ShouldFilter out items with a chosen allergen5Yes
ShouldDaily totals for the kitchen4Yes
CouldColour themes2Yes, fits the time left
CouldOrder history for each student7No, deferred
Won't (this time)Online paymentNot estimatedNo, out of scope
The same project as four iterations (our plan, 36 hours in total)
IterationContentsHoursWhat the client sees afterwards
1Menu display and order form10A working form that rejects a blank name or a quantity of zero
2Staff order list and saving to file10Orders that survive closing the program
3Search, allergen filter, daily totals12The features that make it useful at 12.30 pm
4Colour themes, then testing and fixes4A tested version and a list of what comes next

The four Musts add to 20 hours and the three Shoulds to 12, so together they use 32 of the 36 hours. Of the two Coulds, only the 2 hour colour themes fit, leaving 2 hours of buffer. The 7 hour order history is deferred, and online payment is ruled out for this version because it raises security and privacy questions the group cannot answer properly in the time available.

Writing the Won't list down matters as much as the Must list. It is evidence of client consultation, it stops scope creep in week four, and it gives every student something honest to say in a Project Solution Walkthrough about decisions and trade-offs.

The same habit protects the individual solution. A student working alone has no teammates to absorb an overrun, and the outline caps the submission at 15 minutes and 1 GB. A solution with three finished features beats one with seven half-built ones, both for the student and for an external assessor watching a walkthrough.

In lessons we practise the sorting on invented briefs, then ask the student to defend each priority aloud, as a client would challenge it. That rehearses the explanation without anyone touching the student's real project.

MoSCoW is a general prioritisation technique; the outline itself asks for iterative development, client consultation and evaluation. The project, requirements, estimates and plan are invented for teaching and checked by our own arithmetic on 7 October 2026.

The external 30 per cent

What the individual digital solution has to contain

The outline lists what the solution or prototype should include, and the integrity rule that applies to everything a student submits. Both are quoted or closely paraphrased from the outline below.

AT3 Individual Digital Solution, from the subject outline
ElementWhat the outline asks for
CodeOriginal source code and/or adapted code displaying selection, repetition, and sequencing, accompanied by design comments
DesignAlgorithm design
UseA graphical user interface and/or instructions for use
StatementA designer's statement discussing the effectiveness of the solution and a feature or features that could be considered innovative
Size and lengthNo more than 1 GB, with a maximum time limit of 15 minutes; the statement up to 3 minutes oral or 500 words written
Starting pointMay build on an aspect of a problem from AT1 or AT2, but must be the student's own independent work

The SACE Board states that, to ensure academic integrity, assessment must be based on the work students have produced without undue assistance from other people or sources. We take that literally. We teach selection, repetition and sequencing, design comments, algorithm notation and evaluation with problems we invent, and we never write, debug or review a student's assessed code, recordings or statement.

Source: Stage 2 Digital Technologies Subject Outline, Assessment Type 3; integrity wording from the SACE Board school assessment page, read on 7 October 2026.

Getting ready for Stage 2

Four rungs from Year 10 to a finished solution

Most students start the subject able to code a little. The gap is usually design and finishing, not syntax.

From first programs to a Stage 2 digital solution
WhenRungThe sign it is secure
Year 101. ConstructsWrites loops and conditions that work first time on small problems
Stage 12. StructureSplits a program into functions and explains what each one is for
Early Stage 23. DataLoads a data set, cleans it and states one trend it shows
Later Stage 24. IterationShips a small version, gathers feedback and plans the next one

If programming is the worry

Short daily practice beats long weekend sessions. A small problem solved and explained every day builds the fluency that a programming skills task assumes.

Reading other people's code aloud, line by line, is the fastest way to learn what clean code looks like.

If finishing is the worry

Plan in iterations and protect the Musts. Our understand the code, don't copy-paste page explains why every line in a solution must be one the student can explain.

Questions about Stage 2

Digital Technologies questions we are asked

What is SACE Stage 2 Digital Technologies?

It is a 20-credit Stage 2 subject, code 2DGT20, in which students solve problems of interest through computational thinking, design and programming, data analytics and iterative project development. School assessment counts 70 per cent and external assessment 30 per cent.

What are the three assessment types?

Project Skills (50 per cent, three or four tasks), the Collaborative Project (20 per cent, assessed through individual walkthroughs) and the Individual Digital Solution (30 per cent, assessed externally).

How long can the walkthroughs be?

The outline sets the Project Solution Walkthrough at up to 5 minutes and the Technical Walkthrough at up to 3 minutes, with recordings no larger than 1GB.

What must the individual digital solution include?

Source code showing selection, repetition and sequencing with design comments, algorithm design, a graphical user interface or instructions for use, and a designer's statement of up to 3 minutes oral or 500 words written. The solution may be no more than 1 GB and 15 minutes.

Which programming language should I use?

The outline asks for a general-purpose programming language without naming one, so schools decide. We teach Python by default and Java for students whose school uses it.

What is MoSCoW prioritisation?

A way of sorting requirements into Must have, Should have, Could have and Won't have this time, so the first version of a product is small enough to finish and the client knows what is deferred.

Is Digital Technologies changing?

The SACE Board lists it as a group 3 subject in its subject renewal program, working through the Develop phase in 2026. Always follow the outline your school provides for your year.

Will you help with my project?

We teach the skills with invented projects. We never write, debug or review your assessed code, recordings or designer's statement, because the SACE Board requires assessed work to be produced without undue assistance.

Do I need to know how to code before Stage 2?

It helps a great deal. Students who can already write loops, conditions and functions spend Stage 2 learning design and data rather than syntax.

What time are lessons in Adelaide?

Late afternoon in Adelaide suits most students and falls within our tutors' day. The weekly time is agreed after the free lesson.
