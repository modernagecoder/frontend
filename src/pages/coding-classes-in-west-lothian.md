## Forty thousand grains, five tipping points

A grid, a slide rule for grains, and shapes nobody programmed.

The heap is imaginary. The ground is a grid of 161 by 161 squares, each holding a column of grains. A grain is dropped at a tipping point and then obeys one rule: if a neighbouring square is at least two grains lower, it slides to the lowest such neighbour, and keeps going until no step down is that steep. Real spoil settles at a natural slope called the angle of repose; the rule builds the same idea into the grid. The learner drops 40,000 grains, first all at one point and then shared among five points in a row, and prints the heights.

| Tipping plan | What forms | Peak height | Low point between peaks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One point | A single cone, 61 squares wide | 62 | Not applicable |
| Five points, 30 squares apart | Five separate lobes | 36 each | 6 |
| Five points, 10 squares apart | One long ridge | 39 to 44 | 32 to 34 |

The results match the idea in the record. Tipping from one place builds one cone whose sides settle at exactly the slope the rule allows, about 2 grains per square. Tipping from five places far enough apart builds five lobes, each about 36 grains high with shallow saddles between. Bring the points closer and the lobes merge into a single ridge with only slight bumps. The shape of the heap is written in the tipping plan, even though no line of code mentions lobes.

Then the learner looks harder and finds a surprise. The single heap reaches 30 squares from its centre along the grid lines but only about 21 squares along the diagonals: it is shaped like a diamond, not a circle. A real heap tipped from one point is roughly round. The diamond comes from the model letting grains slide only north, south, east or west, a grid artefact, and fixing it means adding diagonal moves with the right distances. The learner also finds that late grains travel much further, about 20 squares on average against 4 or 5 at the start, because they must run down a much larger slope.

## A heap that remembers an industry

What the scheduled monument records say.

| Record detail | What it says |
| --- | --- |
| What a bing is | A waste heap of residue from the destructive distillation of shale to extract oil |
| Five Sisters form | Rather unusual, with five distinct lobes formed by the method of tipping |
| Five Sisters importance | The most widely known reminder of the pioneering West Lothian shale oil industry |
| Greendykes size | A scheduled area about 920 metres by 580 metres |
| Greendykes operator | The Broxburn Oil Company, active from the 1860s to about 1940 |
| Pioneer | James Young, whose refining techniques are still used in the oil industry |

Simulations built from tiny local rules explain landslides, snowdrifts, sand dunes, grain silos and the way powders flow in factories. They also teach a lasting lesson about models: every model has artefacts, like the diamond-shaped heap, and good modellers look for them before trusting a picture. A West Lothian pupil who has caught one has a skill that carries into any simulation.

We have no connection with Historic Environment Scotland or West Lothian Council. The records' facts are theirs; the heap simulation and any errors are ours.

## An AI can write a heap simulation in seconds. Will it notice the heap is a diamond?

Model artefacts hide in plain sight.

Ask a chatbot for a sand-pile simulation and it will happily produce a grid and a rule, and the pictures will look like piles. Whether the heaps come out diamond-shaped because of the grid, and whether that matters for your question, is something it rarely raises.

A West Lothian learner who has measured a heap along the axis and the diagonal knows to test a model for artefacts before believing it. That is the kind of checking AI-built models need.

A model that looks right can still be wrong in shape, and catching that is a reason for West Lothian teenagers to keep learning to code in 2026. The longer argument is in [why teenagers should still learn to code in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).

### What is the Five Sisters project?

Learners simulate 40,000 grains tipped from one or five points on a grid in Python, see five separate lobes form when the points are far apart, and discover that their model makes diamond-shaped heaps.

### What is the angle of repose?

The steepest slope at which loose material such as sand or spoil stays put. Add more and it slides until the slope is back to that angle.

### What is a shale bing?

Historic Environment Scotland describes it as a waste heap of residue from the destructive distillation of shale to extract oil.
