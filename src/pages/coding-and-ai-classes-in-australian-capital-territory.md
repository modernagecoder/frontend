## Which ACT suburbs really have the shadiest playgrounds?

The ACT Government's open dataset ACTGOV Playground Assets, ranked two ways. Our calculation, 7 October 2026.

| Suburb | GOOD of rated playgrounds | Raw share | Bayesian average, 5 imaginary playgrounds |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fadden | 4 of 5 | 80.0% | 0.528 |
| Forde | 4 of 5 | 80.0% | 0.528 |
| Giralang | 4 of 6 | 66.7% | 0.480 |
| Oaks Estate | 2 of 2 | 100% | 0.469 |
| City | 1 of 1 | 100% | 0.380 |
| Kambah | 6 of 37 | 16.2% | 0.173 |

### 1. Rank the obvious way

Divide GOOD by rated playgrounds in each suburb. Nine suburbs score a perfect 100%, and six of those have just one playground. Thirty-six suburbs score zero. The extremes are mostly small samples.

### 2. Add a little evidence

Across the territory, 131 of 511 rated playgrounds are GOOD, about 25.6%. A Bayesian average adds five imaginary playgrounds at that rate to every suburb before dividing, which pulls tiny samples toward the middle.

### 3. Check it is stable

With 5 imaginary playgrounds, Fadden and Forde lead. With 2, 10 or 20 they still lead. A ranking that survives a change of assumption deserves more trust than one that does not.

### The same trick runs half the internet

Product ratings, film scores and app store charts all face the same problem: a single five-star review should not beat four hundred four-and-a-half-star ones. Learners who build it on ACT playground data recognise it the next time a top ten list looks suspicious.

Shade ratings come from the ACT Government's asset records, which may not reflect every tree or structure today. The rankings, shares and averages are our calculations. Records with a blank or unusual shade entry were left out, which is why 511 of 537 records are used.

## Five habits for any program that produces a league table

Learned on playgrounds, then used on school surveys, sales reports, review scores and any list with a winner.

| Habit | In practice | What it prevents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Show the sample size | Print 4 of 5, not just 80% | Readers trusting a ranking built on one item |
| Smooth small samples | Add imaginary items at the overall rate | Tiny groups dominating the top and bottom |
| Test other assumptions | Re-rank with different smoothing strengths | A winner that only exists under one choice |
| Say what was excluded | Count and report records left out | Hidden gaps in the data |
| Describe the data honestly | Note when records may be out of date | Treating an asset register as a site survey |

The first habit fixes more than it seems. A table that shows "1 of 1" beside "100%" lets every reader judge the evidence for themselves, even before any smoothing is applied.

## An AI tool will rank anything you give it. Why should an ACT student learn to question the ranking?

Because a list sorted by raw percentages can crown a winner on the strength of a single data point.

Hand an assistant the playground records and ask which suburbs have the most shade and it may sort by raw share and present the result as a confident top five, led by suburbs with one playground each. Ask it to write the ranking code and it may do the same, with no column for sample size. The answer looks precise. It is precise about the wrong thing.

A student who has smoothed the ACT data and tested the ranking under several assumptions knows what to ask of any generated league table: how many items sit behind each score, what happens to the order if the method changes, and what was left out. Those questions apply to every ranking a model produces, from product recommendations to search results.

So the reason for an ACT student to learn to code in 2026 is not to compete with an AI tool. It is to know when its ranking can be trusted. The longer argument is in [why teenagers should still learn to code in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).

## ACT coding class questions

Questions ACT families most often send us.

### What is the population of the ACT?

The 2021 Census counted 454,499 people in the Australian Capital Territory, with a median age of 35, three years below the national median.

### What is a Bayesian average?

A Bayesian average is a way of ranking items with very different amounts of evidence. Before averaging, it adds a few imaginary data points at the overall rate, which stops an item with one perfect score from outranking items with many good ones.

### What is the ACT playground project?

Learners rank ACT suburbs by the share of playgrounds rated GOOD for shade in the ACT Government's asset records, first raw and then with a Bayesian average. Fadden and Forde, each 4 of 5, come first however the smoothing is set.
