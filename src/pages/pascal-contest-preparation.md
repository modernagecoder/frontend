# Pascal Contest preparation for Grade 9

The Pascal Contest is the Grade 9 paper in the family of math contests that the University of Waterloo's Centre for Education in Mathematics and Computing (CEMC) sets every year. It is a one-hour, 25-question multiple-choice paper, and for many Canadian students it is the first math contest of high school. This page is built from CEMC's 2027 Pascal page, the front cover of the 2025 Pascal booklet and CEMC's 2026 results booklet. It explains the paper, the scoring rule that makes a blank worth more than a wild guess, what the questions draw on, seven practice problems we wrote with full solutions, and a plan that ends on contest day in February 2027.

At a glance: Organiser: CEMC, University of Waterloo; Who writes it: Grade 9 (and keen younger students); 2027 dates: Tuesday 23 or Wednesday 24 February 2027 (Americas); Paper: 25 multiple-choice questions, 60 minutes; Score: Out of 150; Entry: Through a school only; Classes: Weekdays or weekends, flexible; Start: A free first class. ### What "Grade 9 or below" means

CEMC counts a Pascal writer as official when the contest "may be written by any participant in Grade 9 or below." A keen Grade 8 student can therefore write Pascal and still count.

Grade is what matters, not the math course. CEMC gives the example of a Grade 9 student already taking a Grade 10 math course and says that student "is eligible to write the Pascal Contest." A student working well ahead may choose a higher contest instead, but never a lower one.

### Which calculators are allowed

CEMC's calculator page allows calculating devices without "internet access", "the ability to communicate with other devices", stored notes or programs, "a computer algebra system" or "dynamic geometry software".

It names three examples that are not allowed: "the Casio ClassPad 300 series, HP Prime and TI-Nspire CAS." A plain scientific calculator is the safe choice. In our classes most Pascal questions are worked without one, because the ideas, not the arithmetic, take the time.

## How Pascal is scored, and when a blank beats a guess

The front cover of every recent Pascal booklet carries a short scoring rule, and it changes how a student should treat the last ten minutes. The rule quoted here is from the 2025 Pascal booklet, the latest one we read; check the cover of the 2027 paper on the day.

**Pascal scoring, from the front cover of CEMC's 2025 Pascal Contest booklet; the total in the last row is our arithmetic**

| Rule | What the 2025 booklet says |
|---|---|
| Marks for a right answer | "Each correct answer is worth 5 in Part A, 6 in Part B, and 8 in Part C." |
| Wrong answers | "There is no penalty for an incorrect answer." |
| Blank answers | "Each unanswered question is worth 2, to a maximum of 10 unanswered questions." |
| Part C answers | An "integer from 0 to 99, inclusive"; "A one-digit answer (such as "7") must be coded with a leading zero ("07")." |
| Where the parts start | Part B begins at question 11 and Part C at question 21 in the 2025 booklet. |
| Our check of the total | 10 questions x 5 + 10 x 6 + 5 x 8 = 50 + 60 + 40 = 150, which matches CEMC's "Score out of 150". |

Read the blank rule slowly. A wrong answer costs nothing, but it also earns nothing, while a blank earns 2 points, for up to ten blanks. So a guess is only worth making when it is likely enough to beat 2 points. The arithmetic below is ours, not CEMC's: it divides the marks for a right answer by the number of options still standing.

**Blank or guess · our arithmetic** (text)
```text
Our expected-value check, per question (not CEMC's)

Leave it blank            2 points (up to 10 blanks)
Blind guess, Part A       5 x 1/5   = 1.0 point
Blind guess, Part B       6 x 1/5   = 1.2 points
Blind guess, Part C       8 x 1/100 = 0.08 points

Part A, three options crossed out:  5 x 1/2 = 2.5  -> guess
Part B, two options crossed out:    6 x 1/3 = 2.0  -> a tie
Part B, three options crossed out:  6 x 1/2 = 3.0  -> guess
```
Our reading: a blind guess never beats a blank while blanks still count. In Part A, guess only after crossing out three of the five options. In Part B, two crossed out is a tie and three is a clear guess. In Part C, with a hundred possible answers, a guess without working is close to worthless.

### The eleventh blank

The rule stops at ten. Once a student has left ten questions blank, an eleventh blank earns nothing, so from that point any guess is better than leaving the circle empty.

In practice very few Grade 9 students leave more than ten blanks with time to spare. The rule matters most for the student who finishes Part B with five minutes left and has to decide what to do with Part C.

### What we teach from it

Students keep a tally in the margin: how many questions left blank so far. It takes two seconds and it turns the last five minutes into a calm decision instead of a scramble.

We also teach elimination as a skill in its own right. Crossing out two options in a Part B question by estimation is often faster than solving it, and a third crossed out turns a blank worth 2 into a guess worth 3.

## Entering Pascal: the school orders, the student writes there

There is no form a family can fill in. On its How contests work page CEMC puts it plainly: "Students cannot register for contests on their own. They must be registered by their school."

1. Ask the math department in the autumn. Ask the math teacher whether the school will order the 2027 contest and whether it writes on paper or online.
2. If the school has no CEMC account, CEMC asks the "Principal or Head of Math" to send in a new school application, and warns the manual review can last as long as three weeks. Starting that in January 2027 leaves little room before the Tuesday, February 9, 2027 deadline for schools in Canada.
3. If your own school says no, CEMC suggests asking a different school nearby whether the student may write with its group. Make those calls in December, not February.
4. Write on the school's chosen day. The school runs the contest within the two-day window, in a classroom with a teacher present, and CEMC adds that "a contest can never be written before the official contest date."
5. Online writers: try the practice contest. CEMC says "The PCF Online Practice Contest will be available in January" and strongly encourages online writers to complete it.

Our classes are online, and an online class can never be the place where Pascal is written: that is always the ordering school. Source: [CEMC, How contests work](https://cemc.uwaterloo.ca/contests/how-contests-work), read 22 October 2026.

## The Grade 9 math a Pascal paper leans on

CEMC describes the content in one sentence: curriculum common to all provinces, used to test logical thinking more than content. The breakdown below is ours, from teaching the paper, and it is not CEMC's list.

**Topics behind Pascal questions (our analysis, not a CEMC syllabus)**

| Area | How it tends to appear | What we drill |
|---|---|---|
| Number sense | Fractions, percentages, powers and order of operations in the opening questions | Exact arithmetic without a calculator, and spotting the shortcut before computing |
| Algebra | A word problem that becomes one or two linear equations | Naming the unknown first, then writing the equation from the words |
| Ratio and rate | Mixtures, speeds, shares that change when something moves | The "k" method: write each quantity as a multiple of one unknown |
| Geometry | Angles, areas of composite shapes, the Pythagorean theorem | Drawing the figure larger and labelling every length that can be found |
| Counting | How many numbers, paths or arrangements satisfy a rule | Listing in a fixed order so nothing is counted twice or missed |
| Number theory | Digits, divisibility, remainders and last digits, often in Part C | Patterns in last digits and factor pairs, checked on small cases |

### Where the Ontario Grade 9 course fits

In Ontario, Grade 9 math is MTH1W, the de-streamed course issued in 2021. Its algebra, linear relations and geometry cover most of what the early Pascal questions use.

Ontario Grade 9 students also meet the EQAO Grade 9 Assessment of Mathematics, which EQAO lists for January 12 to February 5, 2027. That window ends just before Pascal, so we keep January classes lighter for Ontario students.

### The jump at question 16 or so

The first ten questions reward accuracy. The second ten ask for one idea that a textbook exercise would have handed over: an auxiliary line, a clever unknown, a pattern in the digits.

Part C asks for a number, not a letter, so elimination is gone. A student who reaches question 21 with fifteen minutes left has done well; we train students to look at all five Part C questions before choosing one.

## Seven original Pascal-style problems with worked solutions

Each problem below is our practice problem, not a CEMC problem: we wrote all seven for our Grade 9 students, and none is copied or adapted from a past contest. They climb like the paper, two at Part A level, three at Part B level and two Part C questions with whole-number answers. Every answer was checked by brute force in Python before publishing.

Problem 1 (Part A level; our practice problem, not a CEMC problem). A curling team played 12 games. It won twice as many games as it lost, and 3 games were ties. How many games did it win? (A) 3 (B) 4 (C) 6 (D) 8 (E) 9

Solution. Let the team lose L games, so it won 2L. Wins, losses and ties make 12 games: 2L + L + 3 = 12, so 3L = 9 and L = 3. The team won 2 x 3 = 6 games, answer (C). Check: 6 + 3 + 3 = 12. The trap is answer (E), 9, which is the number of games that were not ties.

Problem 2 (Part A level; our practice problem, not a CEMC problem). A water tank is three-fifths full. After 12 litres are drained, it is one-third full. How many litres does the full tank hold? (A) 30 (B) 36 (C) 45 (D) 48 (E) 60

Solution. The 12 litres are the difference between three-fifths and one-third of the tank. 3/5 - 1/3 = 9/15 - 5/15 = 4/15, so 4/15 of the tank is 12 litres, one-fifteenth is 3 litres, and the whole tank is 45 litres, answer (C). Check: 3/5 of 45 is 27, and 27 - 12 = 15, which is one-third of 45.

Problem 3 (Part B level; our practice problem, not a CEMC problem). The two shorter sides of a right-angled triangle are in the ratio 3 : 4, and its longest side is 25 cm. What is its area, in square centimetres? (A) 120 (B) 150 (C) 175 (D) 200 (E) 300

Solution. Write the shorter sides as 3k and 4k. By the Pythagorean theorem, (3k)2 + (4k)2 = 252, so 25k2 = 625, k2 = 25 and k = 5. The sides are 15 cm and 20 cm, and the area is (15 x 20) / 2 = 150, answer (B). Answer (E), 300, is what a student gets by forgetting to halve.

Problem 4 (Part B level; our practice problem, not a CEMC problem). How many positive integers less than 100 have digits that add up to 9? (A) 8 (B) 9 (C) 10 (D) 11 (E) 18

Solution. Count by the number of digits. One digit: only 9. Two digits: the tens digit t runs from 1 to 9 and the units digit is 9 - t, giving 18, 27, 36, 45, 54, 63, 72, 81 and 90, which is nine numbers. The total is 1 + 9 = 10, answer (C). Two numbers are easy to lose: the single-digit 9, and 90, whose units digit is 0.

Problem 5 (Part B level; our practice problem, not a CEMC problem). In a class, the ratio of students who walk to school to students who take the bus is 3 : 5. Then 4 students switch from the bus to walking, and the ratio becomes 1 : 1. How many students are in the class? (A) 24 (B) 28 (C) 32 (D) 36 (E) 40

Solution. Let 3k students walk and 5k take the bus. After the switch, 3k + 4 walk and 5k - 4 ride, and these are equal: 3k + 4 = 5k - 4, so 2k = 8 and k = 4. The class has 3k + 5k = 8k = 32 students, answer (C). Check: 12 walkers and 20 riders become 16 and 16. The class size never changes, which is a quick way to test each option: it must be a multiple of 8, and of the five only 24, 32 and 40 are.

Problem 6 (Part C level; our practice problem, not a CEMC problem). How many ordered pairs of positive integers (a, b) satisfy 1/a + 1/b = 1/6? Answer with a whole number from 0 to 99.

Solution. Multiply both sides by 6ab: 6b + 6a = ab. Move everything to one side and add 36: ab - 6a - 6b + 36 = 36, which factors as (a - 6)(b - 6) = 36. Both a and b must be greater than 6 (if a were 6 or less, 1/a alone would already be at least 1/6), so a - 6 and b - 6 are a positive factor pair of 36. Thirty-six has nine positive factors (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36), and each gives one ordered pair, from (7, 42) to (42, 7). The answer is 9, coded as 09 on the response form.

Problem 7 (Part C level; our practice problem, not a CEMC problem). What are the last two digits of 72027? Give the integer they form, from 0 to 99.

**Problem 7 · our working** (text)
```text
7^1 =   7      last two digits 07
7^2 =  49      last two digits 49
7^3 = 343      last two digits 43
7^4 = 2401     last two digits 01   <- the cycle restarts

2027 = 4 x 506 + 3
7^2027 = (7^4)^506 x 7^3
       ends in 01 x 43, so it ends in 43
```
The answer is 43. The idea is that only the last two digits ever matter when multiplying, and 7 to the fourth power ends in 01, so the pattern of last two digits repeats every four powers. A student who sees the cycle needs no calculator; one who reaches for it will find 72027 far too large to display.

### How to use the seven

Give yourself 14 minutes for all seven, the pace of a real paper, and mark your answers before reading any solution. Note which ones you would have left blank under the scoring rule.

Then read each solution and write down, in one line, the idea you were missing. That line is worth more than the score.

### What links them

Problems 1, 2, 3 and 5 are all the same move: name one unknown well, and the rest follows. Problem 4 is careful listing. Problems 6 and 7 are Part C ideas, factoring a clever expression and finding a repeating pattern.

Every solution ends with a check. On a multiple-choice paper the check is cheap, and it is the habit that catches a wrong letter before it costs 5 or 6 points.

## A plan from November 2026 to the February 2027 Pascal

This is our own plan, for a Grade 9 student who starts in early November 2026 and sits the paper on Tuesday 23 or Wednesday 24 February 2027. It is built on two live classes a week with roughly forty minutes of practice in between.

1. Phase 1, 2 to 22 November 2026: speed and accuracy in Part A. Fractions, percentages, powers and order of operations, worked exactly. The target by the end of the phase: ten Part A questions in twenty minutes with no careless slips. Ask the school now whether it will order the 2027 Pascal.
2. Phase 2, 23 November to 20 December 2026: algebra and ratio. Turning words into one or two equations; the k method for ratios; our problems 1, 2 and 5. First Part B questions from past papers in CEMC's archive.
3. Phase 3, 21 December 2026 to 3 January 2027: the holiday fortnight. No new topics. One problem a day from CEMC's Problem Set Generator, chosen at Part B level, with the solution read only after a real attempt.
4. Phase 4, 4 to 31 January 2027: geometry, counting and Part C. Angles, areas and the Pythagorean theorem, careful listing, digits and remainders; our problems 3, 4, 6 and 7. One full 60-minute paper at the end of January. Ontario students sitting the EQAO Grade 9 window keep this phase to one class a week.
5. Phase 5, 1 to 21 February 2027: timed papers and the blank rule. Two more past papers in exact contest conditions, each marked with the 5, 6, 8 and blank-2 rule, then reviewed line by line. Online writers try the PCF Online Practice Contest. Confirm the school's order is in before Tuesday, February 9, 2027.
6. Contest week, 22 to 24 February 2027. A light review of the wrong-answer list, a good night's sleep, and the paper at school. The 2025 booklet asks writers not to discuss the problems or solutions online for the next 48 hours; we ask the same.

## Pascal Contest questions, answered

### What is the Pascal Contest?

It is the Grade 9 math contest that CEMC, part of the University of Waterloo, sets and marks each year. It has 25 multiple-choice questions, takes 60 minutes and is scored out of 150. CEMC says most of its problems test logical thinking and problem solving rather than content.

### When is the Pascal Contest in 2027?

For schools in the Americas, CEMC gives a two-day window, Tuesday, February 23, 2027 and Wednesday, February 24, 2027, and each school runs it on one of those days. The ordering deadline for schools in Canada is Tuesday, February 9, 2027.

### Can a Grade 8 student write Pascal?

Yes. CEMC welcomes motivated students in lower grades, and says the Pascal Contest may be written by any participant in Grade 9 or below. A student can write only one of Pascal, Cayley and Fermat in a school year.

### How is the Pascal Contest scored?

The 2025 booklet gives 5 points for each right answer in Part A, 6 in Part B and 8 in Part C. There is no penalty for a wrong answer, and each unanswered question is worth 2, up to a maximum of 10 unanswered questions. Check the 2027 booklet cover on the day.

### Should my child guess or leave a Pascal question blank?

By our arithmetic, a blind guess is worth less than the 2 points a blank earns, as long as fewer than ten questions are blank. Guessing pays in Part A after three options are ruled out, and in Part B after two or three. That is our analysis of the scoring rule, not CEMC advice.

### Can my child register for Pascal on their own?

No. CEMC says students cannot register on their own and must be registered by their school. Where a school does not take part, CEMC points families to other schools nearby that may let the student write with them.

### Are calculators allowed on the Pascal Contest?

CEMC says some calculators are permitted. Devices with internet access, communication with other devices, stored notes or programs, a computer algebra system or dynamic geometry software are not, and CEMC names the Casio ClassPad 300 series, HP Prime and TI-Nspire CAS as examples.

### Where can we find past Pascal papers?

Ten years of old Pascal papers, with official solutions and results, sit free on CEMC's website, and its Problem Set Generator builds new sets from past Pascal questions. Our own problems are written from scratch, and CEMC's papers stay on CEMC's site.

### What was a good Pascal score in 2026?

CEMC lists the 2026 Pascal average as 93.1 out of 150, the Certificate of Distinction cutoff as 110 and the School Medal Champion cutoff as 93. CEMC gives the certificate to the top 25 percent of competitors, so the cutoff changes each year.
