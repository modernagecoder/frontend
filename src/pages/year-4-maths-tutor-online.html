Year 4 maths tutor online

Most parents know Year 4 as the times tables year, and with reason: by the summer every child in a state school in England is expected to know the tables up to 12 × 12, and a short on-screen check in June asks them to prove it. Year 4 also brings four-digit numbers, counting below zero, hundredths and decimals, Roman numerals to 100, the area of a shape and the first coordinate grid. This page covers the whole of Year 4 maths from the Department for Education's programme of study, what the 2027 check involves in brief, and how we tutor the year, with four examples of our own.

In short

Year 4 maths is the last year of lower Key Stage 2 in England's national curriculum, for children aged 8 to 9. Its best-known requirement is recall of every times table up to 12 × 12, checked in a statutory on-screen multiplication tables check held from 7 to 18 June 2027. The programme also adds four-digit place value, negative numbers, hundredths, Roman numerals to C, area by counting squares and coordinates. Modern Age Coders tutors Year 4 maths live online through its Mental Maths for Kids and Elementary Maths courses, one to one or in a class of 15 to 20. Your first class is free. Year 4 content

What is taught in Year 4 maths?

Seven strands, of which times tables are only one. The table lists the statutory Year 4 requirements in the DfE programme of study, shortened and grouped.

The lower Key Stage 2 introduction closes with the sentence that makes Year 4 famous: "By the end of year 4, pupils should have memorised their multiplication tables up to and including the 12 multiplication table and show precision and fluency in their work." The second half of that sentence matters as much as the first. Precision and fluency are asked for across all of Year 4, not just in the tables.

Two notes in the guidance show how the year is meant to hang together. One says "Pupils are taught throughout that decimals and fractions are different ways of expressing numbers and proportions." The other gives the first algebra a child meets in primary school: "Perimeter can be expressed algebraically as 2(a + b) where a and b are the dimensions in the same unit." Year 4 quietly starts both the decimal system and the idea of a formula.

The June check

When is the 2027 multiplication tables check, and how much preparation does it need?

The 2027 check runs from Monday 7 June to Friday 18 June 2027 in state-funded schools. The STA tells parents no extra preparation is needed beyond the times tables practice teachers already set.

The 2027 check, from the STA's 2027 key stage 2 assessment and reporting arrangements and its information for parents
Date in 2027What happens
Monday 26 AprilSchools can sign in to the MTC service and use the try it out area with pupils
Monday 7 June to Friday 18 JuneMTC administration period
Monday 21 June to Friday 25 JuneAbsent pupils, or those delayed by technical problems, take the check
Monday 28 JuneResults available to schools on the MTC service
After thatYour child's teacher shares the score with you; there is no pass mark

The check itself is short: the STA's leaflet for parents describes "25 times table questions", with "3 practice questions" first. Our sibling pages explain the timing and which tables carry the most weight: see the Year 4 multiplication tables check explained and our practice guide for the check. Here we only add the parts that matter for planning a Year 4 year.

What the STA tells parents

Asked whether parents need to prepare a child, the leaflet answers: "No, you do not need to do anything additional to prepare your child for the check."

It adds that teachers may ask you to practise times tables at home as part of usual practice. That is the right scale of help: a few minutes, often.

Independent schools

The 2027 arrangements state plainly: "Independent schools cannot participate in the MTC."

A Year 4 child at an independent school, or educated at home, will not sit the check, though the expectation in the curriculum is the same goal to aim for.

Is the check changing? The Curriculum and Assessment Review, published in November 2025, recommended keeping it: "We recommend retaining the Multiplication Tables Check (MTC) in Year 4 as an important indicator of multiplicative competence." The government's response agreed that "The timed nature of the multiplication tables check is important to its purpose of assessing fluent recall of times tables", while promising to work with the STA to reduce any unintended effects. The DfE says arrangements for 2026 to 2027 are unchanged.

Sources: STA, 2027 key stage 2 assessment and reporting arrangements; STA, Multiplication tables check: information for parents; Curriculum and Assessment Review final report and government response.

Beyond tables

What else in Year 4 maths do children find hard?

In our experience three things: decimals and hundredths, negative numbers, and telling area from perimeter. Each is new in Year 4, and none is touched by the June check.

Hundredths and decimals

Year 4 asks children to "recognise and write decimal equivalents of any number of tenths or hundredths" and to see what happens when a one- or two-digit number is divided by 10 or 100.

The hard part is that 0.4 is larger than 0.35, even though 35 is larger than 4. Children who understand tenths and hundredths as places, not as digits after a dot, get this right.

Below zero

The requirement to "count backwards through zero to include negative numbers" is a child's first meeting with numbers less than nothing.

We use a thermometer and a number line before any rule. A temperature that falls 7 degrees from 4 degrees lands at minus 3, which a child can see happen.

Area is not perimeter

Year 4 adds "find the area of rectilinear shapes by counting squares" alongside perimeter. Many children add the sides when asked for area, or count squares when asked for the distance round.

We ask one question every time: are we covering the shape, or walking round it?

Roman numerals to C

The curriculum asks pupils to "read Roman numerals to 100 (I to C) and know that over time, the numeral system changed to include the concept of zero and place value".

That second clause is the interesting one. Writing 94 as XCIV shows a child why a system with place value and a zero is such an advance.

The written method of the year deserves a mention too. Year 4 asks children to "multiply two-digit and three-digit numbers by a one-digit number using formal written layout", and the notes connect it to the distributive law with the example 39 × 7 = 30 × 7 + 9 × 7. A child who sees short multiplication as splitting a number into hundreds, tens and ones and multiplying each part is ready for long multiplication in Year 5.

Worked examples

Four Year 4 questions we wrote, with every step shown

All four use statutory Year 4 content and are our own questions, not items from the check or a school scheme.

1. Short multiplication with carrying

Question (ours). 247 × 6.

Ones. 7 × 6 = 42. Write 2, carry 4 tens.

Tens. 4 × 6 = 24 tens, plus the 4 carried, is 28 tens. Write 8, carry 2 hundreds.

Hundreds. 2 × 6 = 12 hundreds, plus 2, is 14 hundreds. The answer is 1482. Check by splitting: 1200 + 240 + 42 = 1482.

2. Dividing by 10 and 100

Question (ours). What is 47 ÷ 10, and 47 ÷ 100? Name the value of the 7 each time.

Divide by 10. Every digit moves one place to the right: 47 becomes 4.7, and the 7 is now 7 tenths.

Divide by 100. Two places: 47 becomes 0.47, and the 7 is 7 hundredths.

Why it helps. A child who says the digits move, not the decimal point, will not be confused when Year 5 brings thousandths.

3. Reading a Roman numeral

Question (ours). Write XCIV as a number.

Split into pairs and singles. XC and IV, because a smaller symbol before a larger one is taken away.

Value each part. XC is 100 take away 10, which is 90. IV is 5 take away 1, which is 4.

Add. 90 + 4 = 94. Now ask how to write 104 with no symbol for zero, and the reason the curriculum mentions zero becomes clear.

4. Same perimeter, different area

Question (ours). A rectangle is 7 cm by 3 cm. A square has sides of 5 cm. Compare their perimeters and areas.

Perimeters. Using 2(a + b): 2 × (7 + 3) = 20 cm, and 2 × (5 + 5) = 20 cm. They are equal.

Areas. Counting centimetre squares: 7 rows of 3 is 21, and 5 rows of 5 is 25.

The point. The same walk round can enclose different amounts of space. This is a stretch question that looks ahead to Year 6.

Practice set (ours, answers below). (1) 1000 more than 6482. (2) Round 3651 to the nearest 100. (3) 315 × 4. (4) Start at 6 and count back 10. (5) Write 63 hundredths as a decimal. (6) Write three quarters as a decimal. (7) Move the point (2, 5) three squares right and one square down. (8) 9 × 12 and 108 ÷ 9. Answers: (1) 7482; (2) 3700; (3) 1260; (4) minus 4; (5) 0.63; (6) 0.75; (7) (5, 4); (8) 108 and 12.

Our tutoring

How do we tutor Year 4 maths through the school year?

Tables early and steadily, so they are secure well before June, and the rest of the programme taught properly around them. The order below is ours and changes after the first class.

  1. A free first class. The tutor checks which tables are instant and which are counted, reads a four-digit number with your child and tries one short multiplication.
  2. Tables from autumn, not May. Two or three tables at a time, with their division facts, revisited every lesson. Factor pairs and commutativity halve the facts a child really has to learn.
  3. Place value past 1000 and below 0. Number lines that run in both directions, rounding to 10, 100 and 1000, and Roman numerals as a history lesson.
  4. Short multiplication as splitting. The written layout taught alongside the distributive law, so every answer can be checked a second way.
  5. Decimals as places. Tenths and hundredths on a place value chart, with lengths and capacities as real examples.
  6. Shape, area and coordinates. Classifying triangles and quadrilaterals, counting squares for area and plotting points in the first quadrant.

Group or 1-on-1 in Year 4?

A group of 15 to 20 Year 4 children works well for steady tables practice and a child who likes company.

1-on-1 is better for a child who freezes under a timer, has gaps from Year 3, or is ready to move faster.

Year 4 questions

What parents ask about Year 4 maths

What maths do children learn in Year 4?

All the times tables up to 12 × 12, numbers to four digits, negative numbers, rounding, short multiplication, hundredths and decimals, Roman numerals up to C, perimeter and area, angles, symmetry, coordinates and bar charts.

Do Year 4 children have to know all their times tables?

Yes. The national curriculum says that by the end of Year 4 pupils should have memorised the tables up to and including 12, with the related division facts.

When is the multiplication tables check in 2027?

The STA's 2027 arrangements set the administration period from Monday 7 June to Friday 18 June 2027, with a catch-up week for absent pupils from Monday 21 June.

Do I need to prepare my child for the multiplication tables check?

The STA's leaflet for parents says you do not need to do anything additional, though teachers may ask you to practise tables at home. Short, regular practice is enough.

Is there a pass mark for the Year 4 times tables check?

No. The teacher shares your child's score with you, and the school uses the results to see which children need extra support.

Does my child sit the check at an independent school?

No. The 2027 arrangements say independent schools cannot participate in the check, so it is taken only in state-funded schools.

Why does my child find decimals hard in Year 4?

Usually because they read the digits after the point as a whole number, so 0.35 looks bigger than 0.4. Teaching tenths and hundredths as places on a chart, with measurements as examples, clears this up.

What comes after Year 4 maths?

Year 5 extends place value to a million and adds long multiplication, thousandths and percentages. A child in Year 4 during 2026 to 2027 sits the KS2 tests in May 2029, listed by the DfE for 14 to 17 May.

What time are Year 4 lessons?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Times are always given in UK time.

Are you part of the STA or my child's school?

No. Modern Age Coders is a separate, independent online tutoring school. We follow the published curriculum but are not connected with the STA, the DfE or any school.
