In short

Modern Age Coders teaches AI and machine learning live online across Canada, to learners aged 6 to 67, either in a group of five to eight or privately. What is taught is model building. Python comes first, then the probability and matrix work the subject genuinely needs, then training and testing models against published Canadian data. Lessons are in English. The teaching staff work from India, which is why the timetable sits in Canadian mornings and weekends and not on weekday evenings. A group seat costs USD 40 a month, CAD 56.37 at the Bank of Canada rate for 24 July 2026, and nothing is charged for the first class.

What you actually build

Fewer fires, more of the country gone: modelling a Canadian fire season

Each country we teach into gets its opening project cut from its own public measurements. Canada gets wildfire. The 2025 season reported fewer fires than 2024 and burned far more land, which is the fastest way we know to destroy a beginner's first instinct that more fires must mean more damage. The figures below come from the published season summaries, which trace back to situation reports from the Canadian Interagency Forest Fire Centre, and each one carries the date it was reported on.

The fourth column is not decoration. These are snapshots taken at three different points in three different seasons, and treating them as a clean certified year on year series is the second trap in this project.

Run the arithmetic before touching a model. 184,961 km2 multiplied by 100 hectares per square kilometre gives 18,496,100 hectares, which matches the 18.496 million hectare figure. A student who cannot reproduce that has no business fitting anything, and checking units is the first hour of the course rather than an afterthought.

Now the comparison the whole project hangs on. From 2024 to 2025 the number of fires fell by about 6 per cent while the area burned rose by roughly 63 per cent. So what actually decides how much of Canada burns in a season is not how many ignitions occur. It is a small number of extreme fire weather days and how large a few megafires are allowed to get.

The target is hectares burned in one province in one season, modelled as log1p of hectares. One row is one province or territory in one fire season, so thirteen jurisdictions across N seasons. The features are assembled by the learner rather than downloaded ready made: seasonal mean Fire Weather Index and Drought Code, the May to June precipitation anomaly, lightning strike count, the number of fires that overwintered from the previous season, and the number of days the national preparedness level sat at 4 or 5.

Three of these rows have no published fire count. The cell says so rather than carrying a zero, because a zero is a claim and a blank is an absence, and a model cannot tell the difference unless the student does it first.

  1. Assemble the panel by hand, with a date column

    One row per province or territory per fire season, built from the situation reports rather than lifted from a tidy download. Record the as-of date of every area figure in its own column. This is the step students want to skip and the one that saves the project, because the date is what later lets you prove the trend is real.

  2. Normalise the snapshots before comparing anything

    Keep only end of season figures, or pick one calendar cutoff and apply it to every row. Compare the three national rows above and the problem is visible: 6 October, 20 November and 16 September. Scraping the latest reported total for each year silently sets a mid-September number against a late-November one and manufactures a decline that is really just a publishing schedule.

  3. Engineer the fire weather features, and mark which are knowable in advance

    Fire Weather Index and Drought Code averages, the May to June precipitation anomaly, lightning counts, overwintered fires and days at national preparedness level 4 or 5. Then write down, for each one, whether it is known at the moment you would want a forecast. Fire count is not: it is only final once the season has ended, and as 2024 against 2025 shows, it barely tracks the target anyway.

  4. Plot the target, then plot it again

    Hectares burned is violently right skewed. Apply log1p and plot it a second time. Write one sentence saying why you did that, and keep the sentence, because on raw hectares a single season owns nearly all the squared error and the model quietly optimises for one year at the expense of every other.

  5. Build the baseline that every later model has to beat

    Predict each province's own historical median hectares. That is the floor. Any model that fails to beat it is not a model, it is a plot, and students are asked to report the baseline score in the same table as everything else so the comparison cannot be quietly dropped later.

  6. Fit under GroupKFold, grouped by fire season

    Gradient boosting, with groups set to the season year. Report mean absolute error on the log scale and back transformed into hectares, because a parent can argue with hectares and nobody can argue with a decimal on a log axis.

  7. Rerun it wrong on purpose, and write up the gap

    Do step six again with a plain random split and record the inflated score. Canadian fire seasons are correlated across the continent: 2023 was catastrophic in British Columbia at 2,840,104 hectares and in Alberta at 2,222,900 hectares simultaneously, driven by the same drought and heat. Under a random split, British Columbia 2023 sits in training while Alberta 2023 sits in test, so the model never learns anything about fire weather. It only learns to recognise a row from 2023, which it can read straight off the training set. The grouped score is lower and it is the honest one. When rows share a common shock, split on the shock.

The distinction

Prompting a model and training one are separate trades

Both are taught here, in that sequence, and we are direct about which one holds its value. One is a set of habits attached to a product that will be redesigned and repriced. The other is arithmetic, and arithmetic does not get deprecated.

Building AI

  • Open a public file, count the rows, and find out what the columns actually mean
  • Notice that one bad season hit two provinces at once, and split the data on that
  • Choose a model knowing which assumption it is making on your behalf
  • Report the error in hectares, so a reader with no statistics can argue with it
  • Put it in front of a season it has never seen, and accept the lower score as the true one
  • Underneath sit probability, matrices and code, and none of the three expire

Canada makes the argument easy, because the building side of it is domestic. A language model company with roughly $7 billion of valuation as of September 2025 runs from Toronto, three federally funded research institutes sit in Montreal, Toronto and Edmonton, and the co-op system means employers here routinely take on students who have not graduated yet.

Why now, in Canada

A minister with AI in the job title, and three institutes that have been hiring since 2017

Canadian AI policy runs on two named tracks, and they are separated by roughly eight years. The first is the Pan-Canadian Artificial Intelligence Strategy of 2017, supported by federal funding of Can $125 million and delivered by CIFAR. Its cornerstone is the Canada CIFAR AI Chairs Program, which benefits from funding of Can$86.5 million over five years. A second phase of the strategy launched in June 2022.

The second track is administrative rather than academic. In November 2024 the federal government announced the creation of the Canadian Artificial Intelligence Safety Institute as part of a 2.4 billion CAD federal AI investment package. Then, on 13 May 2025, Evan Solomon assumed office as Minister of Artificial Intelligence and Digital Innovation under Prime Minister Mark Carney, in a post recorded as newly created. Very few countries put AI in a cabinet title at all.

The three institutes funded under the 2017 strategy are the part a student can actually walk into. Mila, the Quebec AI Institute in Montreal, was founded in 1993 by Universite de Montreal professor Yoshua Bengio and had approximately 1000 students and researchers and 100 faculty members as of 2022. The Vector Institute in Toronto, at the Schwartz Reisman Innovation Campus, was founded in March 2017 with a combined total of $200 million CAD from private and public sectors, established by Brendan Frey, Geoffrey Hinton and Raquel Urtasun, with Hinton as Chief Scientific Advisor; it received $60 million CAD in 2021 from the Government of Canada. Amii began in Edmonton in 2002 as the Alberta Ingenuity Centre for Machine Learning, a joint effort between the Government of Alberta and the University of Alberta.

None of that would matter to a fifteen year old if the employers were somewhere else. They are not. Cohere is headquartered in Toronto, with further offices in Montreal, New York City, San Francisco, London, Paris and Seoul. It was founded in 2019 by Aidan Gomez, Ivan Zhang and Nick Frosst; all three attended the University of Toronto, Gomez co-authored the transformer paper at Google Brain, and Frosst was a researcher there too. The funding history reads $40M Series A in 2021, $125M Series B in 2022, $270M Series C in 2023 at a $2.2B valuation, $500M in July 2024 at $5.5B, $500M in August 2025 at $6.8B and a further $100M in September 2025 taking it to roughly $7 billion. CNBC reported revenue of $240 million in February 2026, against 450+ employees as of 2025.

Read that paragraph as a parent rather than as an investor. The people who built a multi-billion dollar language model company came out of a Canadian undergraduate programme, not out of an American graduate school, and they built it here.

The second employer to know is Shopify, headquartered in Ottawa, founded in 2006, with 7,600 employees and US$11.6 billion of revenue in 2025. In January 2026 it announced the Universal Commerce Protocol, an open standard designed to allow artificial intelligence agents to connect and transact with merchants, alongside native shopping in Google's AI Mode and embedded checkout in Microsoft Copilot. Agent facing commerce infrastructure, designed in Canada, is a live hiring area rather than a forecast.

Vector's founding sponsors make the same point from the industry side. The companies committing funds included Uber, Google and Shopify, with 30 companies committing a combined $80 million over 10 years; Ontario cut $24 million CAD in funding in 2019 and the federal government put in $60 million CAD in 2021. Around all of it sits the co-op system, which rotates undergraduates into paid work every four months.

School and university

No national curriculum, no federal ministry, and two funnels that read more than an average

Ontario, where computing has course codes

Education in Canada is within provincial jurisdiction and the curriculum is overseen by the province, so a live online school is selling into thirteen systems rather than one. Ontario is the system with the tidiest labels. Its computer studies sequence runs ICS2O, Introduction to Computer Studies, Grade 10, Open; ICS3U, Introduction to Computer Science, Grade 11, University Preparation; ICS3C, Introduction to Computer Programming, Grade 11, College Preparation; ICS4U, Computer Science, Grade 12, University Preparation; and ICS4C, Computer Programming, Grade 12, College Preparation.

The prerequisite chain matters more than the list. ICS4U requires ICS3U, so a student who did not take Grade 11 computer science cannot simply arrive in Grade 12 and take the university preparation course. That single line is why an out of school track exists at all.

The diploma itself is 30 credits, of which 18 are compulsory, including 3 credits in Mathematics with at least one credit in Grade 11 or 12, plus 40 hours of community service and the Ontario Secondary School Literacy Test. The OSSLT is administered by the Education Quality and Accountability Office, sat in Grade 10, and is written every year in either October to December or March to May. Students who do not pass can take the Ontario Secondary School Literacy Course, OLC 4O, in Grade 12 instead.

Quebec, which is on a different ladder entirely

This is the part providers outside Canada get wrong. Grade 12 is the final grade in every provincial secondary curriculum except Quebec, whose secondary schools end after Secondary V, which is Grade 11, and Quebec is currently the only province that treats Grade 12 as part of the tertiary level of education. After Secondary V students go to CEGEP, where academic programmes are typically two years in duration and professional programmes typically three. A Diploma of College Studies is required for university admission in Quebec.

The practical consequence is a placement rule rather than a piece of trivia. A 17 year old in Montreal is a college student, and putting that person in a teen batch alongside a Grade 11 student in Mississauga is a mistake we would rather not make on the first call.

British Columbia, where computing sits inside design

In British Columbia computing lives within Applied Design, Skills and Technologies, a K to 12 curriculum area, and Computer Studies 10 is an ADST Grade 10 course. Its Big Ideas are stated as user needs and interests drive the design process; social, ethical, and sustainability issues are influenced by design; and complex tasks require different technologies and tools at different stages. That is a design and ethics framing rather than a list of language features, which is a reasonable thing for a school to teach and leaves the modelling to somebody else.

The contest that crosses every provincial line

The Canadian Computing Competition is organised by the Centre for Education in Mathematics and Computing at the University of Waterloo, with a Junior division for grades 9 and 10 and a Senior division for grades 11 and 12. The top 20 Senior competitors advance to the Canadian Computing Olympiad at Waterloo, and the top four are selected for the Canadian IOI team. It is national in a country where almost nothing about schooling is.

Waterloo, where the form matters as much as the average

Computer Science at the University of Waterloo sits inside the Faculty of Mathematics. The requirement is six Grade 12 U and M courses including Advanced Functions, Calculus and Vectors, any Grade 12 U English and one other 4U course, with Grade 11 U Introduction to Computer Science recommended. Admission is by individual selection from the low to mid-90s, and the Admission Information Form is required.

Waterloo also strongly encourages the Canadian Computing Competition, the Canadian Senior Mathematics Contest or the Euclid Mathematics Contest, and to qualify for Faculty of Mathematics entrance scholarships an applicant has to write either the CSMC or the Euclid. The programme is available as a co-op, where students alternate between school and work every four months across four possible work-study sequences and graduate with up to two years of paid experience.

Toronto, where St. George asks for a supplemental application

Computer Science at the University of Toronto is offered at all three campuses and requires an Ontario Secondary School Diploma with six subjects at the 4U or M level including English. The named courses are ENG4U or EAE4U, MCV4U for Calculus and Vectors and MHF4U for Advanced Functions, with MDM4U, Mathematics of Data Management, recommended. The degree is the HBSc, available as Major, Minor or Specialist, with work-integrated learning at all three campuses. The campus codes are TAD for St. George in the Faculty of Arts and Science, TMX for Mississauga and TXC for Scarborough, and St. George lists a supplemental application as required.

What a parent should take from those two paragraphs

Both flagship programmes want Grade 12 calculus and advanced functions, so the mathematics is not optional and cannot be repaired in the final term. Both also attach a non-transcript component: the Admission Information Form at Waterloo, the supplemental application at U of T St. George. An average is a number the school produces about a student. A trained model with a written up validation scheme is something the student produced, and it is the only one of the two that a form can be filled with.

One more detail is worth reading twice. U of T recommends MDM4U, which is the statistics and probability course, and statistics and probability are precisely what a machine learning class spends its time on. A student who takes our teens track is not doing something adjacent to that recommendation. They are doing the same subject with a dataset attached.

How the classes run

Canadian mornings and weekends, because the after-school hour lands in the Indian night

Canada spans six time zones, while India sits at UTC plus 5 hours 30 minutes in every month of the year and keeps no daylight saving, so the gap between the two moves twice a year rather than staying put. In July 2026, Eastern Daylight Time in Toronto, Ottawa and Montreal is 9 hours 30 minutes behind India; Winnipeg is 10 hours 30 minutes behind, Calgary and Edmonton 11 hours 30 minutes, Vancouver 12 hours 30 minutes, Halifax 8 hours 30 minutes and St. John's 8 hours. In winter, Toronto is 10 hours 30 minutes behind and Vancouver 13 hours 30 minutes. Saskatchewan stays on Central Standard Time all year and Yukon is permanently on UTC minus 7, so Regina, Saskatoon and Whitehorse never move at all.

What a session is

An hour and a half of live teaching with the same person every week, who arrives already knowing where the student stopped last time. Screens are shared in both directions, so an error gets caught in the second it is typed instead of turning up in a marked file on Wednesday. Groups are five to eight students, and one to one is available for anyone who wants the syllabus bent around a specific goal.

Homework is a thing to build, never a worksheet to fill in, and a question at nine in the evening reaches the teacher who set the work rather than a shared support queue. We will not sell a Canadian weekday evening, and the reason is arithmetic rather than preference: 7:00 PM in Toronto is 4:30 the next morning in India, and 7:00 PM in Vancouver is 7:30 the next morning. Nobody teaches well at either hour. Enrolment stays open all year, so a learner joins in whichever week suits the family.

The Canadian year, which is thirteen calendars

There is no national school calendar to plan against, because education is provincial and in many places the term dates are set board by board. We do not publish a fake one. What is consistent is that the school year ends in June, and student ages are usually described by how old a child is when the year ends. Everything else, term start and end dates, break weeks, and provincial examination sessions, we confirm with each family rather than assume, because a break week in one province is an ordinary teaching week in the next.

Two scheduling facts we do work to. Ontario's OSSLT is written every year in either October to December or March to May, so we avoid stacking assessment-heavy work onto Grade 10 Ontario students inside those windows. And a Waterloo co-op student alternates between school and work every four months, which means a college-age Canadian lead may be in Waterloo, Toronto or San Francisco depending on the term, so we set the timetable per term rather than per year. Cohorts open after Labour Day and again after New Year, and every recurring booking is re-cut when the clocks move in November and again in March.

Questions from Canada

What families from Toronto to Vancouver ask first

Does this count toward my child's OSSD?

No, and we will not imply otherwise. This is not an Ontario Ministry of Education credit course, it carries no ministry course code, and it will not appear on a transcript. What it produces is evidence: a model trained on public Canadian data, validated honestly and written up. That is the sort of thing a student can put into Waterloo's Admission Information Form, or into the supplemental application that U of T St. George lists as required. Neither of those forms is a transcript, and neither can be filled in with an average.

How does this sit next to ICS3U and ICS4U?

It runs in parallel and depends on neither. The stated prerequisite for ICS4U is Introduction to Computer Science, Grade 11, University Preparation, which is ICS3U, so an Ontario student who did not take Grade 11 computer science has no in-school route into the Grade 12 course. Our teens track has no such gate. It also covers ground the Ontario computer studies sequence does not reach: training a model, choosing a validation scheme, and reporting an error in units somebody else can check.

My child is in Secondaire V or at CEGEP in Quebec. Which class is the right one?

The college and adult cohort, not a teen one. Quebec secondary school ends after Secondary V, which is Grade 11, and Quebec is the only province that treats Grade 12 as part of the tertiary level of education. A 17 year old in Montreal is therefore usually already at CEGEP, on an academic programme of about two years or a professional one of about three, working toward the Diploma of College Studies that Quebec universities require for admission. We place that student with university students and working professionals, and pitch the mathematics accordingly.

What time are classes in Vancouver, Toronto and Regina, and does the time change?

We sell Canadian mornings and weekends and we do not sell Canadian weekday evenings, because our teachers are in India and 7:00 PM in Toronto is 4:30 the following morning there. Three slots work cleanly: Saturday 09:00 to 10:30 Eastern, Sunday 10:30 to 12:00 Eastern, and Saturday 08:00 to 09:30 Pacific. India holds at UTC plus 5 hours 30 minutes in every month and never moves its clocks, so the gap changes when Canada changes, once in November and once in March, and we rebuild the timetable on both dates. Saskatchewan and Yukon never shift at all, so the offset from Regina, Saskatoon or Whitehorse is the same in every month of the year.

Will this help with the Canadian Computing Competition or the Euclid?

Partly, and we would rather draw the line than blur it. The Canadian Computing Competition is organised by the Centre for Education in Mathematics and Computing at the University of Waterloo, with a Junior division for grades 9 and 10 and a Senior division for grades 11 and 12; the top 20 Senior competitors advance to the Canadian Computing Olympiad and the top four are selected for the Canadian IOI team. Those are algorithm and mathematics contests, not machine learning contests, so the course that overlaps is Data Structures and Algorithms rather than the machine learning track. The contests are worth naming anyway, because to qualify for Faculty of Mathematics entrance scholarships Waterloo asks applicants to write either the Canadian Senior Mathematics Contest or the Euclid.

Both convert at 1 US dollar to 1.4093 Canadian dollars, the Bank of Canada daily rate for 24 July 2026 in series FXUSDCAD. That is a daily indicative rate rather than a transaction rate, so a card billed in dollars settles slightly away from it. Billing is monthly, the first class costs nothing, and no card is needed to book it.

Is there a route from this to Cohere, Mila, Vector or Amii?

There is, and it starts at a Canadian university rather than an American one. Cohere is headquartered in Toronto and was founded in 2019 by Aidan Gomez, Ivan Zhang and Nick Frosst, all three of whom attended the University of Toronto. Mila in Montreal was founded in 1993 by Yoshua Bengio and had approximately 1000 students and researchers and 100 faculty members as of 2022. Vector was established in Toronto in March 2017 with a combined total of $200 million CAD from private and public sectors, and Geoffrey Hinton serves as its Chief Scientific Advisor. Amii began in Edmonton in 2002 as the Alberta Ingenuity Centre for Machine Learning. Waterloo co-op students alternate between school and work every four months, so employers here are structurally set up to take students and not only graduates. What we add is the portfolio that makes a student worth taking.

Are the classes taught in French?

No. Teaching, materials, code and written feedback are in English, and we would rather say that plainly than accept a booking from a francophone family expecting otherwise. Machine learning is documented in English, so the vocabulary a student leaves with is the vocabulary the field uses, and that is genuinely useful. It is still English, and a family in Quebec or in a francophone community elsewhere in Canada should weigh that before booking rather than after.
