Coding classes for kids in Frankfurt

In Hessen, no school type has to teach Informatik, and the Gesellschaft für Informatik knows of no plan to change that. Hessian schools

Do Frankfurt schools teach coding, and from which grade?

Only if a school chooses to and a child signs up. Every rule in this section is taken from the Informatik-Monitor and from the Hessian Ministry for Education.

No compulsory Informatik in Hessen

Hessen's entry in the Informatik-Monitor 2026/27 puts it in one sentence: "Schulartübergreifend findet kein verbindlicher Informatikunterricht statt." No school type, from Hauptschule to Gymnasium, has a compulsory Informatik lesson.

Hessen is one of six Länder in that position, alongside Berlin, Brandenburg, Bremen, Rheinland-Pfalz and Sachsen-Anhalt. For Hessen, as for Berlin and Brandenburg, the Monitor is not aware of any firm plan to make the subject compulsory. Bremen and Rheinland-Pfalz, by contrast, have dates.

What a school may offer

At a Gymnasium, Informatik can be offered as a Wahlunterricht course, one lesson a week, from grade 7. In the Hauptschule and Realschule tracks a school may set up Informatik as an elective in the Wahlpflichtunterricht, but the Monitor notes there is no curriculum of its own for those tracks.

Grundschule in Hessen runs from grade 1 to grade 4, as in most of Germany, so the whole primary stage passes without any computing requirement, and the first optional lesson can come three years after it ends.

A family moving to Frankfurt from Nordrhein-Westfalen, where Informatik is compulsory in grades 5 and 6, or from Bayern, often expects the same here and is surprised. The practical point for parents is simple: whatever a Frankfurt child learns about programming before grade 7 comes from outside school, and even after grade 7 it depends on what the school offers and what the child picks.

Taught in English

Can a young child learn to code in English?

Every lesson is live and in English, with a mentor who slows down for your child rather than asking your child to keep up.

English, and only English

We do not teach in German, and we say so before the first class rather than after it. Children at one of the international or bilingual schools in and around Frankfurt hear English all day anyway; children at a German Grundschule pick up the words of coding in English, which is where those words come from.

A mentor who adjusts

Mentors speak at a calm speed, check that each step landed, and rephrase in shorter words when a child looks lost. They learn your child's accent quickly, and a six year old who mixes in a German word is simply handed the English one and carries on.

Less talk, more screen

Most of a lesson is doing, not listening: dragging a block, running a program, seeing a sprite jump. The child copies, changes and then invents, so the amount of English needed in the first weeks is small and grows with confidence.

Our mentors live in India and teach over video, which is why Frankfurt afternoons line up with their evenings. The same mentor teaches your child every week. Within a month the mentor can tell whether your child enjoys a hard challenge, needs quiet thinking time, or learns fastest by breaking a working program and fixing it again.

A real Frankfurt project

Frankfurt's skyline in data: is every high-rise really over 100 metres?

The city of Frankfurt publishes a register of its high-rise buildings that anyone may reuse. In class, children of ten and over use it to learn a habit that matters in maths, science and coding alike: before you compare two measurements, ask where each one starts from.

The register

The Stadtvermessungsamt, the city's survey office, publishes WFS Hochhäuser Frankfurt am Main under the Datenlizenz Deutschland Namensnennung 2.0. When we downloaded it on 2 November 2026 it had 439 rows. A tall tower can appear several times, once for each part of its outline, so the 439 rows belong to 116 different first addresses.

Each row has an address, a number of storeys above ground and two columns of heights. The file does not explain the height columns. One runs from 152.8 to 360.0, the other from 90.8 to 134.7.

The question

How many of Frankfurt's high-rises are taller than 100 metres? Children get a small file, hochhaeuser.csv, made from the register with four columns: adresse, geschosse, boden and spitze (address, storeys, ground and top). The first program simply counts the rows whose top is over 100.

A ten year old usually predicts "about half". The program says something else.

If every building were over 100 metres, the shortest would be at least 152.8 metres tall, taller than most church spires. The clue is the second column. Its numbers sit between about 91 and 135, and they barely change from row to row. Our reading, and it is our reading because the file gives no definition, is that both columns are heights above sea level: the smaller one is the ground the building stands on, the larger one is its top. Frankfurt itself is not at sea level, so a building's own height is the top minus the ground.

That is far more believable: 210 of the 439 rows, at 44 addresses, are over 100 metres, and the smallest height anywhere in the register is exactly 60.0 metres. Children can test our reading of the columns themselves. A storey in an office tower is usually a few metres high, so divide each height by the number of storeys and see which version gives sensible numbers.

For each address we took its tallest row. The reading of the two height columns as ground and top above sea level, all heights, the storey ratios and the counts are our own work, not the city's.

One pair in the file makes the whole lesson in two lines. A building at Mailänder Straße 1 in Sachsenhausen has a top of 224.6 and a building at Neue Mainzer Straße 46-50 in the city centre has a top of 224.5, almost the same number. But the first stands on ground at 133.7 and the second at 99.0, so the first is 90.9 metres tall and the second 125.5 metres, nearly 35 metres taller. Same top, different start.

  1. Ages 6 to 8, no screen. Stand one toy on the floor and one on a book. Measure both from the floor. Then measure each from where it stands. Which toy is taller now?
  2. Ages 8 to 10, Scratch. Put five tops and five grounds in two lists, make a sprite say each building's height, and colour it red if it is over 100.
  3. 10 to 12, in Python. Start with Version 1, explain why 439 out of 439 cannot be right, then write Version 2 before you look at ours.
  4. Going further. Print the metres per storey for every address and find the two that look strangest. Is it the data, or a building with very tall floors?

A free puzzle contest

Can a Frankfurt child take part in the Informatik-Biber?

Yes, if the school signs up, and the 2026 contest runs right now. No coding is needed: it is a short quiz of logic puzzles.

What the Biber is

Bundesweite Informatikwettbewerbe, the organiser, describes the Informatik-Biber as the largest informatics competition for school pupils in Germany. It is free, online, open to grades 3 to 13 and needs no prior knowledge. Over 580,000 pupils entered in 2025.

It fits in one lesson: half an hour and nine puzzles in grades 3 and 4, twelve puzzles and 35 minutes in grades 5 and 6, then fifteen puzzles and 40 minutes from grade 7. Children may work alone or with a partner.

When, and how to enter

This year's Biberwochen are 9 to 20 November 2026. Entry is through the school, which registers its pupils and runs the quiz in class, so the first step is to ask the class teacher.

Results come out in December 2026, and certificates and small prizes follow in January 2027. Since 14 September 2026 the Schnupper-Biber practice round has been online for anyone.

Biber puzzles ask children to find a hidden rule, put things in order, follow a route or crack a simple code. That is the same thinking our classes build, but we never copy or reuse the real tasks: in class the mentor writes original puzzles in a similar spirit. A Frankfurt Grundschule that does not take part this year can still be asked about 2027, and the practice puzzles are free for anyone.

Coding classes for kids in Frankfurt: common questions

Are the classes in German?

No. We teach in English only, and every class is live. The mentor keeps a gentle pace, tunes in to your child's accent and says things again in simpler words, which lets a child with only some English keep up. The code itself, in Scratch or Python, looks the same in any language.

Is Informatik compulsory in Hessen schools?

No. The Gesellschaft für Informatik says no school type in Hessen has compulsory Informatik, and it knows of no binding plan to introduce it. A Gymnasium may offer it as an optional one-lesson course from grade 7, and other school types may offer it as an elective.

From what age can my child start?

From 6. Children of six or seven begin with Scratch blocks that snap together, so they do not need to type or read long instructions. Most children are ready for typed Python at about 9, which is when our Python and AI for Kids course begins.

My child is in grade 1 at a German Grundschule and speaks little English. Will it work?

Often, yes. Young beginners need only a handful of English words at first, such as move, repeat and when clicked, and they see every step on the screen. The free first class shows quickly whether your child is comfortable, and the mentor will tell you honestly.

When are the classes in Frankfurt time?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. A common choice is a slot between the end of school and about 18:00, or a weekend morning.

Can my child enter the Informatik-Biber in Frankfurt?

Yes, through the school. The 2026 Biber runs from 9 to 20 November 2026 for grades 3 to 13, needs no coding and is free. Ask the class teacher whether the school is registered; the practice Schnupper-Biber is open to anyone.

Do classes stop in the Hessen school holidays?

No. Our mentors do not follow the Hessian calendar, so classes run through the Herbst, Christmas, Easter and summer breaks. Many families pause for a trip in the summer holidays, which in 2027 run from 28 June to 6 August, and restart afterwards.

Where are the teachers?

Our mentors teach from India, over live video. That is why Frankfurt afternoons work well: 16:40 in Frankfurt is 21:10 in India in winter and 20:10 in summer. Your child keeps the same mentor from week to week.

What are the fees, and what currency is charged?

Fees are monthly. This page shows them in euro using the European Central Bank reference rate, but the charge is made in US dollars, so your bank converts it and your statement may differ by a little. There is no charge and no card for the first class.

What does a child actually make?

Under 9, mostly Scratch games, animated stories and quizzes. From about 9, Python programs: drawings with code, small games, and, from about 10, real data projects such as the Frankfurt high-rise register on this page, where the first answer turns out to be wrong.
