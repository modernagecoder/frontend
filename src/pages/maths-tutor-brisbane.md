## How much does the Brisbane River wind?

Sinuosity is the length of a river's channel divided by the straight-line distance between its ends. A perfectly straight river scores 1; the more it winds, the higher the number.

We downloaded the centre line of the Brisbane River from OpenStreetMap wherever it crosses a rectangle around the city, then followed the channel from its western end to its eastern end near the bay. The channel runs 83.92 kilometres; the straight line between the two ends is 39.0 kilometres. The sinuosity of the whole stretch is therefore 2.15. We then cut the channel into five sections of equal length and measured each one.

**Five equal sections of the mapped channel, west to east (our calculation)**

| Section | Channel (km) | Straight line (km) | Sinuosity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Section 1 | 16.78 | 7.69 | 2.18 |
| Section 2 | 16.78 | 8.72 | 1.92 |
| Section 3 | 16.78 | 8.00 | 2.10 |
| Section 4 | 16.78 | 6.41 | 2.62 |
| Section 5 | 16.78 | 13.22 | 1.27 |

**Measuring the same channel with rulers of different lengths (our calculation)**

| Ruler step | Measured length (km) |
|---|---|
| 100 metres | 83.64 |
| 1 kilometre | 78.79 |
| 5 kilometres | 56.44 |
| 10 kilometres | 47.81 |

### Where it winds most

Section 4 scores 2.62: its water travels more than two and a half times as far as a straight line would. Section 5, nearest the bay, is the straightest at 1.27.

### Longer rulers cut corners

A 10 kilometre ruler skips every bend and measures only 47.81 kilometres, little more than half the channel. Every measured length depends on the step size.

### When the simple ruler is enough

A 100 metre ruler gives 83.64 kilometres, within half a per cent of the full mapped line. Past that point, extra detail changes nothing worth knowing.

### What students practise

Ratios, distance between points, adding many short lengths, and how a measurement depends on the instrument. Senior students connect it to arc length and limits.

Primary learners can trace the river with string on a printed map and compare it with a ruler.

### Honest limits

The result depends on our rectangle and on how the map draws the river, so it describes this mapped stretch, not an official river length. We make no claim about the river's total length.

Map data: OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL.

The ruler table is the heart of the lesson for older students. It shows that "the length of a river" is not one fixed number but depends on how closely you follow it. The idea leads naturally to limits in senior calculus: as the steps get shorter, the measured length settles towards a value, and the question becomes how short is short enough. For a wandering river on a map, 100 metres already is.

**How the same idea is taught at different ages**

| Year level | Task | Big idea |
|---|---|---|
| Years 3 to 5 | Lay string along the river on a printed map, then straighten it against a ruler | Curved and straight distance differ |
| Years 6 to 8 | Divide channel length by straight distance for three sections | Ratios compare shapes, not sizes |
| Years 9 and 10 | Add Pythagoras distances between map points | Length of a path from coordinates |
| Years 11 and 12 | Shrink the step and watch the total settle | Limits and arc length |

Data: [OpenStreetMap](https://www.openstreetmap.org/copyright), Brisbane River waterway ways, one Overpass query on 13 October 2026. Lengths, sections and ratios are our calculation.
