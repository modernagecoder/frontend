The quick answer

Modern Age Coders teaches coding to teenagers of 12 to 17 in Peru. Every class is live, online and taught in English by a mentor based in India, who works through real code with the student on a shared screen. Teens learn Python first, then choose a direction: building apps, games and websites; contest problem solving in C++ or Python for events like Peru's Informatics Olympiad; or a recognised Python certificate. Classes run in a group of fifteen to twenty or one to one, at any hour that suits the family in Peru time, and the first class is free.

Who joins

Who are these coding classes for teens for?

Teenagers in Peru who can follow spoken English, or want to get there, and who are ready to type real code. Three groups write to us most often.

Bilingual, IB and Cambridge pupils

Their maths and science already come in English, and many will meet computer science as an exam subject. Learning code in the same language avoids translating every term twice before an IGCSE or Diploma paper.

Teens who moved to Peru

Arriving at 13 or 14 is hard enough without a coding club in a language you are still learning. Here the teaching is in English, and the student keeps one mentor who knows their level.

Peruvian teens with plans

University reading, programming documentation and most help forums are in English. A teenager who learns to code in English is practising both at once, every week, with a real reason to listen.

Parents who prefer Spanish will find a page written for them: Leer en español: clases de programación para adolescentes. It describes the same classes in Spanish; the lessons themselves are always taught in English.

Three routes

What should a teenager in Peru learn to code: build, compete or certify?

All three start with the same foundations. After about four months, most teenagers lean towards one of them, and the choice shapes which language and which course comes next.

Build

Apps, websites, games and tools that someone else can use. This suits the teen who wants to see a result and show it off. Languages: Python, then JavaScript, C# or Java. Proof of progress: a portfolio of finished projects.

Compete

Short, hard problems solved against the clock, as in Peru's Informatics Olympiad. This suits the teen who enjoys puzzles and maths. Languages: Python to start, C++ for speed. Proof: contest results and solved problem counts.

Certify

An exam with a clear syllabus, such as the Python Institute's entry certificate, the PCEP. This suits the teen who likes a defined target. Language: Python. Proof: the certificate itself, from the body that sets the exam.

Why not all three at once?

Because depth beats breadth at this age. A fourteen year old who has built one complete game understands variables, loops and functions far better than one who has started five tutorials. We keep a student on one route long enough to finish something real.

Routes are not fixed. Contest training makes a builder faster and more careful; building makes a contest coder better at reading long programs. Many of our teenagers switch after a year.

How the first months look

Month one: typed Python, variables, conditions and loops, with a small program finished every week. Month two: lists, functions and files. Months three and four: a first real project, and a first timed problem set.

By then the student, the mentor and the parent can usually see which route fits. The mentor writes a short note suggesting the next course, and the family decides.

Which language

Should a teenager learn Python or C++ first?

Python first for almost everyone, then C++ for those heading to contests. The Peruvian contests and MINEDU's own pilot point the same way.

What Peru's own programmes use

The Peruvian Informatics Olympiad, the OPI, is organised by UTEC and is free for every secundaria pupil in the country. Its 2025 rules allowed C++ or Python in the first round, which was held online, and only C++ in the in-person final.

MINEDU's Fábrica de Programadores pilot, launched in July 2025 for 1,000 pupils in 4th and 5th of secundaria in Lima Norte, chose Python and offered its graduates the chance to sit the PCEP exam. Python is where the state chose to start.

What we recommend

Start with Python. It lets a beginner think about the problem rather than the punctuation, and every idea learnt in it, from loops to recursion, carries straight into C++ later.

Move to C++ when timed problems start to matter. A student aiming at the OPI final needs C++ by then, and its standard library, with ready-made sorting and data structures, is a big part of contest work. That switch usually takes a few weeks, not months.

For the certificate route, the Python Institute describes the PCEP-30-03 as an entry-level exam with no prerequisites: 35 questions in 50 minutes, a pass mark of 70%, and a credential valid for five years. Our certification course prepares students for it; the Python Institute, not us, sets and awards the exam. For a 5th-year student weighing it up, our Spanish page on programación para 5to de secundaria goes through it in detail.

How a student gets there

In 2025, UTEC's rules set the route: an online first round, an in-person final at UTEC, and the top four of the final chosen for the IOI. Students had to be no older than 20 on 1 July 2025 and either enrolled at a Peruvian school in 2025 or out of school since 2024. For 2026, registration closed on 18 March and the final was held on 11 April.

UTEC runs two sister contests from the same site: OPEIA for artificial intelligence, held on 19 March 2026, and OPECIA for cybersecurity. All three are free for secundaria pupils.

What the table tells a young coder

That the gap is real and can be closed. Peru's best finish came in its very first year, and every year since, its students have placed in the middle or lower half of a very strong field. Countries that do well at the IOI usually train students for years before the contest.

That training starts with fundamentals, not tricks: careful reading, choosing the right data structure, and testing edge cases. We teach that kind of thinking with problems we write ourselves. We are not connected with UTEC, the OPI or the IOI and we never reproduce their problems.

Contest training is not for everyone, and it does not have to be. A teenager who builds an app that a family member uses every day has learnt just as much. The table matters because it shows what serious practice can lead to, not because every student should aim at it.

Secundaria

Does secundaria in Peru teach programming?

Not as its own subject. In MINEDU's national curriculum, the CNEB, none of the secundaria areas is computing or programming, though some private schools add it.

The national timetable

Under the regular school day (Jornada Escolar Regular), secundaria has 35 teaching periods a week, with Matemática at 4 in every grade. The nearest area to computing is Educación para el Trabajo, at 2 periods a week, or 3 under the longer full school day of 45 periods; its competency is about running enterprise projects, not writing code.

MINEDU places pupils by their age on 31 March: about 12 in 1st of secundaria and 16 in 5th. Our groups follow age and level, so a 13 year old who has coded for years is not held back.

Where coding fits instead

For many Peruvian teens, the closest thing to a school computing contest is mathematical. The national maths olympiad, ONEM, drew more than 1.7 million secundaria students in 2026, according to El Peruano. Coding and contest maths reward the same habit: reading a problem slowly and proving the answer.

Teenagers are online in any case. INEI reports that 92.5% of Peruvians aged 12 to 18 used the internet in the second quarter of 2026. The question is whether they only consume, or also build.

Our classes run beside school, never in its place. We are not connected with MINEDU, UTEC or any school, we do not award Peruvian grades, and a student who finishes a course receives our own certificate of completion.

A real lesson

What does a lesson look like? Ranking a contest scoreboard in Python

This is a lesson we teach to teenagers from about 13, early in contest training. The contestants and scores are invented by us. The rule is the usual one for timed contests: the higher score wins, and on equal scores, the faster contestant wins.

The task

Five contestants, each with a score and the minutes they took. Sort them into a ranking. Python's sorted accepts a key, and a key can be a tuple, so the natural idea is to sort by score and then by time.

Because the highest score should come first, almost everyone adds reverse=True. Version 1 runs cleanly and puts the two 300-point contestants at the top. It looks right at a glance.

The bug

Look at the tie. Diego and Bruno both scored 300, but Diego took 120 minutes and Bruno took 140. Diego should be first. Version 1 puts Bruno first, and does the same to Carla and Ana lower down.

The reason: reverse=True reverses every field in the key, not just the score. Larger times now come first too, so the tie-break works backwards. The fix is to reverse only the score, by sorting on its negative.

Then we show a second fix that older students like. Python's sort is stable: items that compare equal keep the order they arrived in. So you can sort twice, least important key first. Sort by time; then sort that result by score with reverse=True. When we ran it, it printed ['Diego', 'Bruno', 'Carla', 'Ana', 'Elena'], the same ranking as Version 2. Negating works only for numbers; the two-pass method works for names and dates as well.

The lesson behind the lesson: a program that runs without an error can still be wrong, and the place to look is the tie, the edge, the case where two things are equal. Contest problems are built around exactly those cases. So are real systems that rank anything from search results to exam marks.

  1. Warm-up. Before running Version 1, write down the ranking you expect. Compare it with the output and find the first line that differs.
  2. Your fix. Write Version 2 without looking, then add a sixth contestant who ties with Diego on both score and time. Where should she go, and why does Python put her there?
  3. Two passes. Rank the contestants by score, highest first, and then by name in alphabetical order on ties, using two calls to sorted.
  4. Explain it. In three English sentences, tell your mentor why reverse=True broke the tie and why negating the score does not.

AI and teenagers

Can teenagers use AI tools for coding?

Some, within each company's age terms and only after the student can solve the problem alone. We follow the terms the companies publish, not the ones people assume.

What the terms say

OpenAI requires ChatGPT users to be at least 13, with a parent or guardian's permission under 18. Anthropic requires Claude users to be at least 18, so no teenager uses Claude in our classes. GitHub sets 13 as the floor for Copilot.

What contests say

UTEC's 2025 OPI rules forbid generative AI for solving the problems. A teenager who leans on AI during practice will find that crutch removed on the day that matters.

What we do

Students solve first, alone, and explain their reasoning. Older teens then use permitted AI tools to review their code and question it, the way a professional uses a second opinion, never to write the answer.

Teenagers curious about how AI itself works can go further on our Spanish-language page, curso de IA para adolescentes, which covers building simple AI models rather than only using them.

Teenagers and parents ask

What teenagers and parents in Peru ask about coding classes

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. Every class is live, online and in English only. Mentors speak clearly, slow down when needed and match each student's pace and accent, and every step also appears on the shared screen. A Spanish-language page describes the classes for parents, but the lessons are always in English.

Which language should a teenager learn first?

Python, in almost every case. It lets a beginner concentrate on the problem, and the ideas transfer directly to C++, Java or JavaScript later. Teenagers aiming at the final of Peru's Informatics Olympiad move to C++, the only language its 2025 final allowed.

Can a complete beginner of 15 join?

Yes. Most of our teenage students start from zero. The first course assumes no previous code at all, and the free class places the student in a group of a similar level or in one-to-one lessons.

Do you prepare students for the OPI?

We teach the problem-solving skills the OPI and similar contests reward, with problems we write ourselves, in Python and C++. We are not connected with UTEC or the OPI and never reproduce their problems. Students register through UTEC's own site, where entry is free for secundaria pupils.

When are classes in Peru time?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India is ten and a half hours ahead of Peru all year, so 18:00 in Peru is 04:30 the next morning in India.

Can teenagers use ChatGPT or Claude in class?

Not Claude: Anthropic's terms require users to be 18 or older. ChatGPT needs a user to be at least 13 and, under 18, to have a parent's permission. In class, students solve problems on their own first and use only tools whose terms allow their age.

Is the PCEP certificate worth it for a teenager?

It can be a useful target. The Python Institute describes it as an entry-level exam with no prerequisites: 35 questions, 50 minutes, a 70% pass mark. It proves basic Python, not the ability to build large programs, so we pair it with a real project.

Will this help with IB or IGCSE computer science?

Yes, as a foundation, and we also teach IB Diploma Computer Science and IGCSE Computer Science 0478 directly. The school and the exam board set and mark every assessed piece of work; we help the student understand the material deeply.

Are you connected with MINEDU or a school in Peru?

No. Modern Age Coders is an independent online school whose mentors work from India. We have no connection with MINEDU, UTEC or any school in Peru, and award no Peruvian grades. Students who complete a course get our own certificate of completion.

What does a teenager need at home?

A laptop or desktop computer, a steady connection and a headset with a microphone. A phone is not enough for typing code. Classes use an ordinary video call, and any software needed later is free and installed with the mentor's help.
