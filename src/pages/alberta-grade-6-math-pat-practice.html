Alberta Grade 6 math PAT: a parent’s guide, with practice

In June, Alberta’s Grade 6 students write a Provincial Achievement Test in mathematics, in two parts and without a calculator in either. It is built on the new Kindergarten to Grade 6 math curriculum, so its sections use that curriculum’s language: integers, decimals and powers; fractions, rates and ratios. Below, we walk through Alberta’s 2026 to 2027 bulletin, solve two problems the way a Grade 6 teacher would, and gives practice we wrote ourselves, aimed at the math rather than at the test.

The two parts

What the Grade 6 math PAT contains

Alberta’s 2026 to 2027 Grade 6 Mathematics Subject Bulletin sets out the test. The first thing families notice is the calculator rule: “The use of a calculator is not permitted in Part A or Part B.” Everything is done by hand, with standard methods.

Tools that are allowed

Manipulatives are welcome in both parts. The bulletin’s list includes “a protractor, a ruler, tracing paper, pattern blocks, tiles and cubes, geoboards, tangrams, counters, spinners, unlabeled number lines, graph paper”, with one condition: the tool “cannot perform the mental conversion or provide the solution to a problem.”

A 12 by 12 multiplication grid may be used in both parts as a regular accommodation for children who use one in class, recorded on the platform. And the bulletin is firm on calculators: “If a student requires the use of a calculator to complete Part A and Part B, the student should be excused from the provincial achievement test.”

What Alberta expects at each standard

At the acceptable standard, the bulletin says students can typically “apply appropriate strategies and standard algorithms to solve routine one-step problems”. At the standard of excellence they can “apply appropriate and efficient strategies and standard algorithms fluently and accurately to solve multi-step novel problems”.

The difference between those two sentences is a good map for practice: first one-step accuracy with the standard methods, then several steps chained together, then problems the child has not seen in that form before.

June 2027

Who writes it, and how

Alberta’s Grade 6 Parent Guide says “All students registered in grades 6 and 9 or those in their sixth or ninth year of schooling are expected to write the tests for their grades.” Under each schedule Alberta adds that every student may have up to double the official time.

Grade 6 children now sit the PATs on a screen through Alberta’s digital assessment platform, the same place Alberta keeps its practice tests, which offer read-aloud, coloured backgrounds and enlarged text.

Results

Parents hear two raw scores, one per part, never added together: Alberta’s bulletin forbids combining them into one total. A fuller report, the Individual Student Profile, is due in late September 2027 under the bulletin’s timetable of significant dates.

The same parent guide asks parents to keep the test in perspective: children’s feelings about a test “are very much influenced by what adults close to them say about those tests.” A calm week before the test is worth more than an anxious one.

A note on the new curriculum. Alberta’s renewal timeline records that “New grades 4 to 6 mathematics” was implemented across the province in the 2023-24 school year. A child in Grade 6 in 2026 to 2027 has therefore been taught from the new curriculum since Grade 3 or Grade 4, and the PAT now uses its organizing ideas. Alberta’s summary of Grade 6 math includes “Multiply fractions by natural numbers.”, “Calculate area and volume.”, “Solve algebraic equations.” and “Collect, graph and interpret data.”

Learning, not rehearsing

How we help with the Grade 6 PAT without coaching to it

One line in Alberta’s Grade 6 Parent Guide shapes how we work: the province “discourages test rehearsal and coaching.” We take that at face value. A Grade 6 child who understands the math does not need rehearsal; one who does not understand it is not helped by it.

Standard methods, understood

Column addition, long multiplication and division, done by hand and explained aloud. The new curriculum names these “standard procedures (algorithms)”, and the PAT allows no calculator.

Fractions as numbers

Placing fractions on a number line, comparing them and multiplying them by whole numbers, so that 3 × 2/7 is a picture before it is a rule.

Reading the question

Alberta notes that many questions are “placed in real-life contexts”. We practise turning a short paragraph into a calculation, and writing the answer back in words.

What we leave out

No released test items, no question-spotting and no multiple-choice tricks. Every question on this page was written by us for the Grade 6 content, and none is copied or adapted from an Alberta test.

And no promises about results. The PAT is one picture of one week; Alberta itself says it is part of a larger picture that teachers build all year.

Where computational thinking comes in

The Grade 6 science PAT, written the Friday before the math test, has six reporting categories, and one of them is “Computational Thinking”. Patterns, step-by-step instructions and checking a rule are where math and early coding meet.

The short program in the worked section shows the idea: a growing pattern produced by a loop, then checked against a rule. Children who build the pattern themselves remember the rule.

Worked problems, ours

Two Grade 6 problems, solved without a calculator

Our own problems, not Alberta Education questions. The first is a one-step fraction problem like Part A; the second is a multi-step measurement problem like Part B. Both are done by hand, as the test requires.

Problem 1. A recipe uses 3/4 of a cup of oats for one tray of granola. How many cups are needed for 6 trays?

  1. Choose the operation. Six trays of 3/4 each means 6 × 3/4, a fraction multiplied by a natural number, which is on Alberta’s Grade 6 list.
  2. Multiply the numerator only. 6 × 3 = 18, so the product is 18/4. The denominator stays 4 because the pieces are still quarters.
  3. Simplify. 18/4 = 9/2 = 4 1/2.
  4. Check with a picture. Two trays use 3/4 + 3/4 = 1 1/2 cups, so six trays use three times that: 4 1/2 cups.
  5. Answer in words. Six trays need 4 1/2 cups of oats.

Problem 2. A garden bed is 3 m long and 2 m wide, and it will be filled with soil 0.5 m deep. Soil comes in bags of 0.25 cubic metres. How many bags are needed?

  1. Find the volume. Volume = length × width × depth = 3 × 2 × 0.5. Work it as 6 × 0.5 = 3, so the bed holds 3 cubic metres of soil.
  2. Decide the next operation. The question asks how many bags of 0.25 fit into 3, which is a division: 3 ÷ 0.25.
  3. Divide without a calculator. 0.25 is one quarter, and there are 4 quarters in 1, so there are 4 × 3 = 12 quarters in 3.
  4. Check. 12 bags × 0.25 = 3 cubic metres, exactly the volume of the bed.
  5. Answer in words. 12 bags of soil are needed. A real gardener might buy one more for settling; on the test, the answer is 12.

Number practice, ours

Ten Part A style questions: number, no calculator

Our own questions on the two Part A categories: integers, decimals and powers; fractions, rates and ratios. They are not Alberta Education questions. Give it a quarter of an hour, pencil only.

  1. Evaluate −7 + 12.
  2. Evaluate 3 − 9.
  3. Add 4.6 + 2.75.
  4. Multiply 5.2 × 3.
  5. Evaluate 24.
  6. Write 103 as a whole number.
  7. Add 2/5 + 1/5.
  8. Multiply 3 × 2/7.
  9. A bag holds 12 red counters and 18 blue counters. Write the ratio of red to blue in lowest terms.
  10. A train travels 150 km in 3 hours at a steady speed. What is its rate in kilometres per hour?
  1. 5. On a number line, start at −7 and move 12 to the right.
  2. −6. Start at 3 and move 9 to the left, passing zero.
  3. 7.35. Line up the decimal points: 4.60 + 2.75.
  4. 15.6. 5 × 3 = 15 and 0.2 × 3 = 0.6.
  5. 16. 2 × 2 × 2 × 2. A power means repeated multiplication, not 2 × 4.
  6. 1000. 10 × 10 × 10.
  7. 3/5. Same denominator, so add the numerators.
  8. 6/7. 3 × 2 = 6 sevenths.
  9. 2:3. 12:18, then divide both by 6.
  10. 50 km per hour. 150 ÷ 3 = 50.

Wider practice, ours

Eight questions in the spirit of Part B

Also ours and not from Alberta: algebra and patterns, geometry and coordinates, measurement and data, still with no calculator. Allow about 25 minutes.

  1. Solve n + 17 = 41.
  2. Solve 4m = 36.
  3. A pattern starts 3, 7, 11, 15, ... Describe the rule and find the 10th term.
  4. Points A(2, 3), B(6, 3) and C(6, 7) are three corners of a square. What are the coordinates of the fourth corner?
  5. A triangle has a base of 10 cm and a height of 6 cm. What is its area?
  6. A box measures 5 cm by 4 cm by 3 cm. What is its volume?
  7. Find the mean of 4, 7, 9, 10 and 10.
  8. A rectangle has a perimeter of 30 cm and a length of 9 cm. Find its width and its area.
  1. n = 24. Subtract 17 from both sides: 41 − 17 = 24. Check: 24 + 17 = 41.
  2. m = 9. Divide both sides by 4.
  3. Start at 3 and add 4 each time; the 10th term is 39. From the 1st term to the 10th there are 9 steps of 4: 3 + 36 = 39. The program above prints the same value.
  4. (2, 7). AB and BC are each 4 units long, so the fourth corner is 4 units above A.
  5. 30 cm2. Half of base times height: (10 × 6) ÷ 2.
  6. 60 cm3. 5 × 4 × 3.
  7. 8. The total is 40, and 40 ÷ 5 = 8.
  8. Width 6 cm, area 54 cm2. Two lengths make 18 cm, leaving 12 cm for two widths, so each width is 6 cm. Area = 9 × 6 = 54.

If the fraction or ratio questions caused trouble, that is the place to spend the next few weeks, because the two Number categories together carry a large part of both parts. If the word problems were the trouble, read each one aloud and draw a quick sketch before calculating.

Why short sessions work

Twenty focused minutes, twice a week, with a few questions from earlier weeks mixed in, does more for a Grade 6 child than a long session the weekend before. Mixing old and new is what makes the methods stick.

Each session ends with the child explaining one answer aloud. If they can teach it, they know it.

After Grade 6

The PAT is a checkpoint, not the finish. Grade 7 brings integer operations of every kind and equations with letters on both sides in Alberta’s draft junior high curriculum, and the fraction and decimal fluency built now carries straight into that.

Our classes keep going past June for the same reason: the summer after Grade 6 is a good time to make fractions and negative numbers automatic.

For parents

Ask for an explanation, not an answer. “How did you know?” tells you more than any worksheet score.

Keep June calm. Alberta’s own guide notes how much children pick up from the adults around them about a test.

For students

Estimate first. If 5.2 × 3 comes out as 156, the estimate of about 15 tells you something slipped.

Draw it. Fractions, areas and coordinates are all easier on paper than in your head.

Questions from Grade 6 parents

Grade 6 math PAT: common questions

What is the Grade 6 math PAT in Alberta?

Every June, Alberta's Grade 6 classes sit a provincial math test called a Provincial Achievement Test, or PAT. It has two parts: Part A, 15 questions on number, and Part B, 40 questions on the whole Grade 6 math curriculum.

When is the Grade 6 math PAT in 2027?

Part A is on Monday 14 June 2027 from 9 AM to 9:30 AM, and Part B on Wednesday 16 June 2027 from 9 AM to 10:15 AM. Every student may take up to double the official time.

Are calculators allowed on the Grade 6 math test?

No. Alberta's bulletin says a calculator is not permitted in Part A or Part B. Manipulatives such as a ruler, a protractor or graph paper are allowed.

What is on Part A?

Part A has 15 questions from the organizing idea Number, in two reporting categories: integers, decimals and powers, and fractions, rates and ratios. It is designed for 30 minutes, with up to 60 allowed.

What is on Part B?

Part B has 40 questions from all organizing ideas in Grade 6 math: number, algebra and patterns, geometry and coordinate geometry, and measurement and statistics. It is designed for 75 minutes, with up to 150 allowed.

Is the Grade 6 PAT based on the new curriculum?

Yes. Alberta implemented new Grades 4 to 6 math in the 2023-24 school year, and the 2026 to 2027 Grade 6 math bulletin describes the test in the new curriculum's terms of organizing ideas and reporting categories.

What is a sensible way to get ready for the Grade 6 PAT?

Know the Grade 6 math, done by hand. The province does not want PATs rehearsed or coached, so two short sessions a week on fractions, decimals, ratios and word problems beat any last-minute cramming.

Is there coding on the Grade 6 PATs?

Not on the math test. The Grade 6 science PAT reports on six categories, one of which is Computational Thinking.

When are lessons available for Alberta families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. All times are agreed in Alberta time.

What do Grade 6 math classes cost?

Nothing for the trial class. After that, eight live lessons a month are billed as one monthly fee, cancellable when a month closes; the Fees panel here lists the current amounts.
