Taught in English all day, often in the IB, IGCSE, A level or American programmes. They want help in the language of their own lessons.

## Why does a maths tutor need to speak the child's language?

Because German school maths is graded on words as well as numbers. The national standards say so in their own lists.

The KMK maths standards for the Klasse 9 and Klasse 10 certificates, in the version of 23 June 2022, name six process competences, and they are binding in every Land. The first is Mathematisch kommunizieren, communicating mathematically; the others are solving problems, modelling, representing, working with mathematical objects, and working with media. At Abitur level the first general competence is Mathematisch argumentieren (K1), arguing mathematically. A pupil can know every formula and still lose marks on a task that says "begründen Sie" if she cannot put the reason into a sentence.

Reasoning is easiest to learn in the language you think in. So we practise it in English: the pupil says the reason aloud, the mentor asks "why?" once more, and only then is the sentence written down. Here is a short example of the kind we use, our own.

### Claim, then reason

**Claim.** The sum of two odd numbers is always even.

**A first answer.** "3 + 5 = 8 and 7 + 9 = 16, so it is true." That is two examples, not a reason; a teacher would give little credit.

**A reason, said aloud.** "An odd number is one more than an even number. Two odd numbers are two even numbers plus 1 plus 1, and 1 + 1 = 2, so the total is even plus even plus 2, which is even."

### And in symbols

Write the two odd numbers as 2m + 1 and 2n + 1. Their sum is 2m + 2n + 2 = 2(m + n + 1), a multiple of 2.

The spoken reason and the algebra are the same argument. The spoken one comes first because a child can test it by saying it, and a sentence that sounds wrong usually is wrong.

In German the task would read "Begründen Sie, dass ...", and the pupil now has the argument ready to write in either language.

## Do German schools teach coding, and is English help useful there too?

Informatik is spreading Land by Land, but unevenly. The Gesellschaft für Informatik tracks it each year in its Informatik-Monitor.

For the school year 2026/27 the Informatik-Monitor counts six Länder where no Informatik lesson is required of every pupil. They are Berlin and Brandenburg, Bremen and Hessen, Rheinland-Pfalz and Sachsen-Anhalt. Two of the six have dates for a change: Bremen intends compulsory Informatik in all its schools from the school year 2027/28, Rheinland-Pfalz a year later, in 2028/29. In the Oberstufe, the monitor reports that about 15 % of pupils across Germany take Informatik in the Qualifikationsphase.

So a child in Berlin or Hessen may reach Klasse 10 with very little programming at school, while a child in a Land with compulsory Informatik meets it as a graded subject, taught in German. Both are reasons families come to us. For the first, we are the place where coding happens at all. For the second, we explain in English what the Informatik lesson covered, and then let the pupil build something with it.

There is also a plain practical reason. The keywords of Python and Java are English words, error messages are in English, and so is almost every guide a young programmer will search for. A learner who is taught to read for, while and return as ordinary English sentences reads code faster.

## What does explaining code aloud look like in a real class?

Programmers call it rubber-duck debugging: you explain your program, line by line, to someone (or something) that knows nothing, and the bug often shows itself while you talk. This is one of our own lessons for teenagers, built on a question every family in Germany working with India asks: what time is the class for the mentor?

**Version 1: add four and a half hours** (Python)
```python
from datetime import datetime, timedelta

def india_time(berlin):
    return berlin + timedelta(hours=4, minutes=30)

for day in ["2026-11-03 16:15", "2027-04-15 16:15"]:
    berlin = datetime.fromisoformat(day)
    print(day, "Berlin ->", india_time(berlin).strftime("%H:%M"), "India")
```
It prints 2026-11-03 16:15 Berlin -> 20:45 India and 2027-04-15 16:15 Berlin -> 20:45 India.

The program runs and gives two answers that look reasonable. The mentor asks the pupil to explain the function in English, one line at a time. "It takes the Berlin time and adds four hours and thirty minutes, because India is four and a half hours ahead." The mentor asks: always? And the pupil, saying the word "always" aloud, stops. In summer Germany moves its clocks forward and India does not. The second date, 15 April 2027, is in German summer time, when the gap is only three and a half hours.

**Version 2: let the time-zone database decide** (Python)
```python
from datetime import datetime
from zoneinfo import ZoneInfo

BERLIN = ZoneInfo("Europe/Berlin")
KOLKATA = ZoneInfo("Asia/Kolkata")

def india_time(berlin):
    return berlin.replace(tzinfo=BERLIN).astimezone(KOLKATA)

for day in ["2026-11-03 16:15", "2027-04-15 16:15"]:
    berlin = datetime.fromisoformat(day)
    print(day, "Berlin ->", india_time(berlin).strftime("%H:%M"), "India")
```
It prints 2026-11-03 16:15 Berlin -> 20:45 India and 2027-04-15 16:15 Berlin -> 19:45 India.

The fix is to stop doing the arithmetic by hand. Python's zoneinfo module knows the rules for Europe/Berlin and Asia/Kolkata, including the dates when Germany changes its clocks, so the second answer moves to 19:45. The bug was never in the syntax. It was in an assumption, "always four and a half hours", and assumptions hide most easily in code nobody has explained out loud.

**The same three lessons on the learner's clock and the mentor's clock, worked out with version 2**

| Learner in Germany | Mentor in India, until 27 March 2027 | Mentor in India, from 28 March 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Weekday 15:15 | 19:45 | 18:45 |
| Weekday 17:15 | 21:45 | 20:45 |
| Sunday 10:15 | 14:45 | 13:45 |

German clocks follow the Einheitenzeitgesetz: MEZ, one hour ahead of UTC, from 25 October 2026, and MESZ, two hours ahead, from 28 March 2027. Indian Standard Time is fixed at five and a half hours ahead of UTC with no summer change. We show the rows only to explain the conversion; they are not set batch times.

1. **Read the line aloud as a sentence.** "Return the Berlin time plus four hours thirty."
2. **Ask whether the sentence is always true.** Words like always, never and every are where hidden assumptions live.
3. **Test the case that would break it.** Here, one date in winter and one in summer.
4. **Fix the assumption, not the symptom.** Do not add a special case for April; use the rule that already exists.
