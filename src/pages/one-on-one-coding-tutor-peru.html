Who it suits

Who needs a one-on-one coding tutor rather than a class?

Most learners do well in a good group. Private lessons earn their place when one of these six situations applies. The list is ours, drawn from the learners who ask us for one to one.

A deadline

An exam, a coursework submission or a certification date is close, and there is no time to move at a group's pace. The tutor plans backwards from the date.

A specific syllabus

A university course, IB or IGCSE computer science, or PCEP. The tutor follows that syllabus, not ours, and works from the learner's own materials.

A shy or very young child

Some children of 6 to 9 will not speak in a group, especially in a second language. Alone with one patient adult, they talk, and talking through code is how they learn.

Far ahead, or far behind

A learner who would be bored or lost in any group. The tutor sets the pace weekly, faster or slower.

An adult with a goal

A professional automating reports, a parent learning alongside a child, someone over 50 starting fresh. Adults often prefer not to learn in front of others.

An unusual timetable

Shift work, travel, a sports schedule. One to one is booked around the learner, not the other way round.

Why it matters for private lessons

The youngest and the oldest learners are the ones who most often need a tutor's full attention: children because they cannot yet describe what went wrong, older adults because they want to go at their own pace without feeling watched.

Being online is not the same as being ready to code, though. Coding needs a laptop or desktop with a keyboard. A tutor's first job with a new learner is often to check the computer, the browser and the headset.

A private session

What happens in a one-on-one online coding session?

The learner drives and the tutor navigates. The learner's screen is shared, the learner types, and the tutor watches, asks and only occasionally points. Here is the shape of a typical session with us.

  1. Start from last time. The learner explains, in their own words, what they did since the previous session and what got stuck.
  2. Read the learner's own code. Not a model answer: their file, as it is. This is where private lessons find mistakes a group never sees.
  3. Predict, then run. Before each run the learner says what should happen. The gap between prediction and result sets the next ten minutes.
  4. One new idea. Introduced only when the learner needs it for their goal, and practised at once on their own project.
  5. A task to finish alone. Small enough to complete, hard enough to need thought. The next session starts by reading it.
  6. A note afterwards. What was covered, what is shaky, what comes next; for children, the note goes to a parent.

What a tutor does not do

Write the learner's assignment. Coursework, IB internal assessments and university projects must be the learner's own work, and a tutor who types it for them is doing harm, whatever the deadline.

Hand over answers to copy. A learner who cannot explain a line of their program does not yet understand it, and the next exam will find that out.

What the learner should leave with

A working piece of their own code, a clear idea of the one thing that was hardest, and the confidence to try the next step without the tutor.

Over weeks, the ability to read an error message and find its cause alone. That is the moment a private tutor starts making themselves unnecessary, which is the point.

A bug from a real-style session

What can a private tutor catch that a group class misses?

Mistakes in the learner's own code, the kind that never appear in the teacher's examples. Here is one we see often from learners writing their first games and scoring programs, rebuilt as a short example of our own.

The learner's program

The learner keeps a score in a variable outside the function and writes a function that adds points to it. It reads perfectly sensibly: take the score, add the points, give it back.

They run it and Python stops with an error they have never seen. In a group, the learner might quietly delete the function and copy the score line everywhere instead. In a private session the tutor sees the error appear and asks the learner to read it aloud.

What the error means

Python decides which names belong to a function before running it. Because the function assigns to score, Python treats score as the function's own local variable throughout, so the right-hand side tries to read a local that has no value yet.

The fix we teach is not to reach for the global keyword, which would make the code harder to test. It is to pass the current score in and return the new one, so the function depends only on what it is given.

The rule the learner writes down: if a function assigns to a name, that name is local to the whole function. It takes two minutes to explain and saves hours later, in Python and in other languages with similar rules. It is also the kind of discovery that only happens when someone is reading the learner's own code, line by line, which is what a private session is for.

  1. Ages 10 to 13. Predict whether the first program runs. Then rename the variable inside the function and see whether the error changes.
  2. Secundaria and university. Write a version with global score and one with parameters. Write one test for each, and say which was easier to test.
  3. Adults. Find a function in your own script that changes a value defined outside it. Rewrite it to take the value in and return the result.

Choosing a tutor

How do I choose a good online coding tutor?

Watch a session, not a profile. These are the checks we suggest, whether or not you choose us.

  1. Who types? In a good session the learner's hands are on the keyboard most of the time.
  2. Does the tutor ask before telling? Listen for "what do you think this line does?" before any explanation.
  3. Does the tutor read the learner's code, or switch to their own prepared example at the first problem?
  4. Is there a plan tied to your goal? For an exam or a university course, the tutor should ask for the syllabus or past papers early.
  5. What happens with coursework? A trustworthy tutor says plainly that they will not write it for the learner.
  6. Which AI tools does the tutor allow? For anyone under 18 the answer must respect each tool's own age terms: Claude requires users to be 18, and ChatGPT asks for a parent's permission under 18.
  7. Is it the same tutor every week? Continuity is most of the value of one to one.

Our tutors are mentors based in India who teach only live and only in English. We are not connected with MINEDU, any Peruvian school or university, or any certification body, and we do not issue Peruvian qualifications; learners who finish a course receive our own completion certificate.

Why one to one helps

Peru's reading score in the 2025 index (540) is well above its speaking score (480). Many learners understand written English, and code is written English, but rarely get to speak it.

In a private lesson the tutor asks the learner to explain each line aloud. That is speaking practice with a clear purpose and a patient listener, and the vocabulary is limited and repeated: loop, list, return, error.

How our tutors adapt

They slow down, repeat, write key words in the chat and on screen, and adjust to the learner's accent. Lima scored 555 in the same index, in EF's "high" band, but the tutor sets the pace for the learner in front of them, not for a city average.

We never switch to Spanish in a lesson. The point is that the English becomes usable, and it does so faster when there is no way round it.

Fitting the year

How does a private coding tutor fit around school or university?

Around the learner's calendar, not ours. For school students in Peru that calendar is set by MINEDU; for university students, by their faculty.

School students

MINEDU's resolution for 2026 (RM 501-2025-MINEDU) sets a school year of 36 teaching weeks and 8 management weeks. Private coding lessons can run through the term at a lighter rhythm and step up in the January to March summer break.

The national curriculum counts lessons in teaching hours of 45 minutes and has no computing subject of its own. MINEDU's Fábrica de Programadores pilot in 2025 trained 1,000 students of 4th and 5th of secundaria in Lima Norte in Python, with the option to sit the PCEP exam. Families who want something similar at home can do it privately; our coding classes for teens in Peru page describes the paths.

University students and adults

University learners usually book weekly sessions through the semester and extra ones before exams. The tutor works from the course's own syllabus, lab sheets and past assignments, always as a guide and never as the author.

Working adults often prefer early mornings or late evenings on Peru time. Because our mentors work from India, ten and a half hours ahead, those hours are easy to arrange.

Learners in Peru ask

One-on-one coding tutor: questions from Peru

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. Private lessons are live, online and in English only. Your tutor speaks slowly and clearly, adjusts to your pace and accent, and writes key words on screen. Our Spanish-language pages describe the lessons for readers who prefer Spanish, but the teaching itself is always in English.

Who is a private coding tutor right for?

Learners with a deadline or a specific syllabus (a university course, IB or IGCSE computer science, PCEP), very young or shy children, learners far ahead or behind any group, adults who prefer to learn privately, and anyone with an unusual timetable.

What ages do you tutor?

From 6 to 67. Children of 6 to 9 usually start with Scratch; from about 9 or 10, Python; teenagers and adults choose from Python, Java, web, data and AI, or an exam syllabus.

Will the tutor do my assignment or coursework?

No. Coursework, IB internal assessments and university projects must be your own work. The tutor explains, asks questions and reviews what you write, but never writes it for you.

Can a tutor help with a university programming course in Peru?

Yes, as a guide. Share the syllabus and past assignments and the tutor plans sessions around them. Courses at Peruvian universities may be taught in Spanish; our sessions are in English, and the code itself is the same.

What time can I have lessons?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India is ten and a half hours ahead of Peru all year, so a 06:30 lesson in Lima is 17:00 for the tutor.

Is one to one better than a group class?

Not always. Groups of fifteen to twenty are lively, and many children learn well from their peers. One to one is faster for a specific goal and better for learners who will not speak up in a group. You can switch either way.

Can my teenager use AI tools in lessons?

Only tools whose terms allow their age, and only after they can solve the problem themselves. Claude requires users to be 18, and ChatGPT requires a parent or guardian's permission for anyone under 18.

Do you prepare for PCEP?

Yes. Our PCEP and PCAP course can be taken one to one. MINEDU's Fábrica de Programadores pilot also led students to the PCEP exam; we have no connection with that programme or with the Python Institute.

Are you linked with MINEDU, a university or a school?

No. Modern Age Coders is an independent online school with no tie to MINEDU, SUNEDU, any Peruvian university or school, or any certification body. We give our own completion certificate only.
