Online coding and AI classes in Madre de Dios

Madre de Dios is Peru's south-eastern Amazon, with most of its people in and around Puerto Maldonado. It is a young region with school rolls that have grown fast, and nearly nine in ten residents aged six and over use the internet. What many homes lack is a computer. Our classes are live, online and in English. This page sets out the region in official figures, says plainly who is ready for English-taught lessons, and uses a research sensor at Puerto Maldonado's fish-farm ponds to teach a lesson every scientist learns: weak evidence should count for less.

Where people live

Tambopata district alone had 110,212 residents in 2025, Inambari 28,544, Las Piedras 21,348, the district of Madre de Dios 11,532, Laberinto 10,475, Huepetuhe 9,354 and Iberia 5,874.

An unusually young population

Of the 185,176 people enumerated in 2025, 22,414 were aged 6 to 11 and 22,092 were 12 to 17. Only 14,430 were 60 or older.

By our calculation from the same table, people aged 60 and over are 7.8 per cent of those enumerated in Madre de Dios, against 14.8 per cent across Peru. Children and teenagers make up a large share of the region.

A connected region

In 2025, 87.0 per cent of Madre de Dios residents aged six and over used the internet, more than the national 81.1 per cent. Over half of households had a home connection.

The regional government has also pursued connectivity: in June 2022 it reported winning national FIDT funding for pre-investment studies to bring an internet service to eight localities in the region.

The missing machine

Household computers peaked at 29.8 per cent in 2021 and fell to 22.8 per cent by 2025. So in most homes the internet arrives through phones.

For a live class that is half the answer. A phone can show the mentor's screen. It cannot comfortably hold an editor, a running program and its output while the learner types for an hour.

  1. Ages 6 to 9: a tablet or laptop for block coding, with an adult nearby.
  2. Ages 10 and up: a laptop or desktop with a keyboard; shared between siblings is fine.
  3. Any age: a headset if the home is busy, and the same quiet spot each week.
  4. Before paying: the free class runs on your real device, so you see whether it works.

Teachers have had help with devices: in July 2022 the regional government reported handing out 1,033 laptops, bought through its education directorate, to appointed teachers in public schools, in a programme covering 261 schools. That supports classroom teaching, though it does not put a computer in a pupil's home.

English and fit

Will a learner in Madre de Dios cope with classes in English?

English is the only language our mentors teach in, always live and online. Many teenagers and adults manage that well, and young children can too when the format is right.

Figures we can rely on

EF's English Proficiency Index 2025 places Peru at 519, a moderate level. EF publishes scores for sixteen Peruvian regions; Madre de Dios is not one of them, so there is no regional English figure.

MINEDU's 2025 census evaluation found 16.7 per cent of the region's fourth-graders at the satisfactory level in maths and 18.9 per cent in reading, against 30.0 and 32.7 nationally; in second year of secundaria, 6.0 and 8.0 per cent, against 12.7 and 18.5.

Who usually does well

Secundaria pupils curious about science, nature or games, who will put up with not knowing every English word at first. Students of engineering, forestry, education or business. Adults in tourism, trade or public service who already meet English and want programming or data skills.

For children under about ten we normally suggest private lessons, where the mentor can follow the child's speed and demonstrate rather than explain.

Talent is not the issue. In November 2021 the regional government reported that a pupil in 3° de secundaria from Iberia, in Tahuamanu province, had won second place nationally in ONEM, MINEDU's maths olympiad, in its Nivel II. A Madre de Dios data project

Is the fish-pond water at Puerto Maldonado warmer than the air?

IIAP, the Peruvian Amazon research institute, ran a temperature sensor at the water intake of Puerto Maldonado's fish-farm ponds in late 2016 and has published the daily values. The question is easy to ask. Answering it well means noticing that some days are built on far more measurements than others.

What IIAP published

IIAP releases "Temperatura del agua y del ambiente registrada en Puerto Maldonado [IIAP]" through Peru's open data portal, datosabiertos.gob.pe, with an ODC-By (Open Data Commons Attribution) licence. IIAP describes daily water and air temperatures recorded "entre el 10 de noviembre y el 31 de diciembre de 2016 por un nodo sensor", placed "en el estanque de captación de agua de las piscigranjas de Puerto Maldonado".

Our copy of the daily file, mediciones_diarias.csv, has 84 rows: 42 dates, each with a water value and an air value in degrees Celsius.

The column that changes everything

Each row also has n_lecturas, the number of readings that day, from 1 to 288. IIAP's own data dictionary labels this column "por confirmar", to be confirmed, and describes each value as a daily average, also "por confirmar". We keep that caution.

If 288 is a full day, as the maximum suggests, a day with 20 readings saw the sensor working for a small part of the day. Its average may describe one hot afternoon rather than the whole day.

Water was warmer on 40 days out of 42, by 3.13 degrees on average. Before believing it, look at the two exceptions and the extremes. The exceptions are 20 November 2016, built on 94 readings, and 1 December 2016, built on only 20. The air value of 14.975 degrees on 30 November rests on 40 readings. Several of the days that most affect the answer are the ones with least evidence behind them. Version 2 sets a minimum: a day must have at least 144 readings, half the maximum, to count.

On the 24 days with plenty of readings, the water was warmer every time, by 2.64 degrees on average. Both exceptions vanished, because both were thin days. But the program also threw away 18 days, and a fair report says so. The choice of 144 is ours, so the honest next step is to show how the answer moves as the minimum changes.

Two lessons come out of the table. From a minimum of 100 upwards, the water is warmer on every day kept, so that conclusion is solid. The size of the gap is not: it moves between about 2.6 and 4.0 degrees depending on the cut-off, and with only nine days left at 250 it rests on very little. A careful analyst reports the firm finding firmly and the shaky one with its range.

  1. Under 8, with cups. Measure a cup of water with a toy thermometer once, then five times. Which answer would you trust more?
  2. Blocks, around 8 to 10. Make two lists of five numbers. Count how many times the first list is bigger, position by position.
  3. First Python, around 10 to 12. Change MIN_READINGS to 100 in Version 2 and predict the output before running it.
  4. Ages 13 and over. Instead of dropping thin days, weight each day's gap by its number of readings. How does the weighted average compare?
  5. Going further. Find the dates missing between 10 November and 31 December 2016. Do the gaps cluster, and what might that say about the sensor?

Schools

Do schools in Madre de Dios teach programming?

Not as a subject of its own. The region's schools follow the national curriculum, in which digital skills belong to every area.

National rules

Under Ley 28044, the General Education Law, primaria runs for six years and secundaria for five, and MINEDU fixes a pupil's grade by age on 31 March. A child who is six on that date starts 1° de primaria.

The technology competency of the national curriculum, number 28, is listed among the competencies shared by all areas. The regular secundaria week has 35 hours, four of them for Matemática and three for Inglés, with none named programming.

Contests open to pupils here

ONEM, MINEDU's maths olympiad, starts with a round in every school. That round fell on 8 July 2026, and over 1.7 million secundaria pupils took part in the 2026 olympiad.

UTEC runs Peru's computing olympiads, including OPI, and they are free for secundaria pupils. We are not connected with MINEDU, UTEC, IIAP, the regional government or any contest; every practice problem we use is our own.

For pupils who enjoy contest maths, our page on maths olympiad coaching in Peru describes how we prepare learners with problems we write ourselves. Teenagers who prefer data and science can read about our data science course for Peru.

Online coding and AI classes in Madre de Dios: common questions

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. Lessons are only in English, live and online. Mentors keep their English slow and simple, adjust to how fast the learner goes and how they speak, and when a learner answers in Spanish they supply the English to try; they do not switch the lesson to Spanish.

We only have phones at home. Is that enough?

For watching a lesson, yes. For writing programs, no: from about ten a learner needs a keyboard and a larger screen. A shared laptop is enough, and young children can start block coding on a tablet.

Can learners in Mazuko, Huepetuhe or Iberia join?

Yes. Because lessons happen online, distance from Puerto Maldonado does not matter; a steady signal at your lesson time does. The free lesson tests it.

What if the connection drops during a lesson?

The learner rejoins and the mentor picks up where they stopped. If it happens often, we try audio with a shared screen, or a different hour of the day.

What time are the classes?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. Madre de Dios is 10 hours 30 minutes behind India all year; 08:00 in Puerto Maldonado is 18:30 for the mentor.

Do schools here teach programming?

No dedicated subject exists. Peru's curriculum asks every area to build digital skills, and no hour of the ordinary secundaria week is given to programming.

Are the classes useful for university students in Puerto Maldonado?

Yes. Python and data analysis support degrees in engineering, forestry, education and business. We teach alongside your studies; the university decides your grades.

Do you teach AI?

Yes. Older learners build and test simple models and see where they fail; children explore the ideas in games. Under-13s never use adult AI accounts in our lessons.

How do we pay?

By card, once a month, in US dollars; the bank converts the charge to soles. Visitors from India see rupee prices. The first lesson is free.

Is there a certificate?

Courses that include one finish with a completion certificate from us. It is not a Peruvian school or university qualification, and no Peruvian authority awards or recognises it.
