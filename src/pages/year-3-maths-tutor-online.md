# Year 3 maths tutor online

Year 3 opens Key Stage 2, and for many seven year olds it is the first time maths is written down in columns. Numbers stretch to 1000, the 3, 4 and 8 times tables arrive together, fractions stop being only halves and quarters of a cake and start sitting on a number line, and the clock face gains Roman numerals. None of this is tested nationally in Year 3, which is exactly why gaps can slip past unnoticed until the times tables check in Year 4. Below we set out what the Department for Education's programme of study asks of Year 3, how it grows out of Year 2 and into Year 4, and how we tutor it, with four examples we wrote ourselves.

## What do children learn in Year 3 maths?

Year 3 adds three-digit place value, written column methods, three new times tables, tenths and fractions as numbers. Everything in the table below is statutory in the DfE programme of study, summarised by area.

For the two years of lower Key Stage 2, Years 3 and 4, the curriculum sets one main aim: that pupils "become increasingly fluent with whole numbers and the four operations, including number facts and the concept of place value". It adds that they should "develop efficient written and mental methods and perform calculations accurately with increasingly large whole numbers." Year 3 is where the written part of that sentence starts.

**Year 3 maths in the national curriculum for England (statutory requirements, grouped and shortened)**

| Area | What a Year 3 child is taught |
|---|---|
| Place value | Read, write, compare and order numbers to 1000; the value of each digit in a three-digit number; 10 or 100 more or less; count in 4s, 8s, 50s and 100s |
| Adding and subtracting | Mental sums of a three-digit number and ones, tens or hundreds; columnar addition and subtraction with up to three digits; estimating and checking with the inverse |
| Multiplying and dividing | The 3, 4 and 8 tables; two-digit times one-digit, moving from mental to written methods; scaling and correspondence problems |
| Fractions | Counting in tenths; fractions of a set; fractions as numbers; equivalent fractions with small denominators shown in diagrams; adding and subtracting with the same denominator within one whole |
| Measurement | Lengths in m, cm and mm, mass and capacity; perimeter of simple shapes; change in pounds and pence; analogue clocks with Roman numerals I to XII; 12-hour and 24-hour clocks; days in each month and leap years |
| Geometry | Draw 2-D shapes and make 3-D ones; angles as a turn; right angles, half-turns and full turns; horizontal, vertical, perpendicular and parallel lines |
| Statistics | Bar charts, pictograms and tables; one-step and two-step questions such as how many more or fewer |

Two details in the non-statutory notes show the thinking behind the year. Place value is taught flexibly: the guidance writes 146 both as 100 + 40 and 6 and as 130 + 16, because a child who can see 146 as thirteen tens and six ones is ready for exchanging in column subtraction. And money is kept simple: pupils record pounds and pence separately, since decimal money waits until Year 4.

From the Year 3 programme of study and the lower Key Stage 2 introduction in the DfE's [mathematics programmes of study: key stages 1 and 2](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study), read on 31 October 2026.

## How is Year 3 maths different from Year 2?

Every strand takes one step up: one more digit, three more tables, written methods instead of objects, and fractions that are counted rather than only shaded. We put the three years side by side from the programme of study.

**One strand at a time, Year 2 to Year 4 (statements from the DfE programme of study; the side-by-side layout is ours)**

| Strand | Year 2 | Year 3 | Year 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Place value | Two-digit numbers, to at least 100 | Three-digit numbers, to 1000 | Four-digit numbers and beyond 1000 |
| Times tables | 2, 5 and 10 | 3, 4 and 8 | All tables up to 12 × 12 |
| Written methods | Objects, pictures and mental methods | Column addition and subtraction with up to three digits | Columnar methods with up to 4 digits; short multiplication |
| Fractions | One third, one quarter, two quarters, three quarters | Tenths; unit and non-unit fractions as numbers | Hundredths; decimal equivalents |
| Time | To five minutes | To the nearest minute; Roman numerals on the clock | Converting between analogue and digital 12- and 24-hour clocks |

Reading down the Year 3 column, the pattern is that Year 3 does the hard conceptual work while the numbers are still small. Exchanging a ten for ten ones is the same idea whether the number has three digits or six, and Year 3 is where a child either understands it or learns to copy the steps. The same is true of fractions: the Year 3 notes ask children to place fractions on a number line and to "go beyond the [0, 1] interval", which is the first sign that a fraction is a number in its own right.

### If Year 2 left gaps

The end of Year 2 expects number bonds to 20. A child still counting on for 8 + 7 will find column addition slow, because every column is a bond.

## How is column addition and subtraction taught in Year 3?

Year 3 is the year of the first formal written method. The statutory wording is to "add and subtract numbers with up to three digits, using formal written methods of columnar addition and subtraction", and the checking habit comes with it.

The programme of study pairs the method with a safeguard. Children are to "estimate the answer to a calculation and use inverse operations to check answers", so a subtraction is checked by adding back, and every answer is compared with a rough guess first. The DfE's Mathematics Appendix 1 shows examples of the column layouts, while saying the examples are not an exhaustive list and do not set out a fixed progression, which is why your child's school may lay the columns out a little differently from how you learned them.

### Where Year 3 columns go wrong

**Smaller from larger.** In 452 minus 178 a child writes 8 minus 2 in the ones column instead of exchanging. The answer comes out too large and they cannot see why.

**Exchanging across a zero.** 503 minus 178 needs a hundred broken into tens before a ten can be broken into ones. This two-step exchange is the single most common Year 3 stumble we see.

### What fixes it

**Place value before procedure.** If 503 can be read as 4 hundreds, 9 tens and 13 ones, the exchange is just a renaming, not a trick.

**Estimate first, add back after.** 500 minus 180 is about 320, so an answer of 435 is wrong before it is checked. The curriculum asks for both habits for this reason.

Mental methods do not stop when columns arrive. The statutory list keeps mental addition and subtraction of "a three-digit number and ones", "a three-digit number and tens" and "a three-digit number and hundreds" in the same year. We teach a child to ask first whether a sum needs a column at all: 347 + 200 does not, and 347 + 286 probably does.

Statutory wording from the Year 3 addition and subtraction requirements; layouts in Mathematics Appendix 1 of the same DfE document.

## Which times tables should a Year 3 child know?

The 3, 4 and 8 times tables, with their division facts, on top of the 2, 5 and 10 from Year 2. The requirement is to "recall and use multiplication and division facts for the 3, 4 and 8 multiplication tables".

The 4 and 8 tables arrive together for a reason the guidance states directly: "Through doubling, they connect the 2, 4 and 8 multiplication tables." Double a 2-table fact and you have a 4-table fact; double again and you have an 8-table fact. The 3 table stands apart and usually takes the longest. The notes also show children using the order of multiplication to make a sum easier, with the example 4 × 12 × 5 rearranged as 4 × 5 × 12, which is 20 × 12, which is 240.

### Why this matters for Year 4

The curriculum says that by the end of Year 4 pupils "should have memorised their multiplication tables up to and including the 12 multiplication table". Year 3 supplies three of the tables that check uses.

For a child in Year 3 during 2026 to 2027, the DfE lists the check "within the 2-week period from Monday 5 June 2028", when they will be in Year 4.

### Why it matters beyond the check

The Curriculum and Assessment Review, reporting in November 2025, wrote: "We believe the priority at Key Stage 2 should be ensuring all pupils build competence in multiplicative reasoning and problem-solving."

It described that reasoning as including "multiplication and division scaling, repeated addition, arrays, and whole number rates", which is the Year 3 multiplication strand almost word for word.

The review also led the government to plan a revised national curriculum, to be published in 2027 for first teaching in 2028. For the current school year the DfE says assessment arrangements "will remain unchanged", so a Year 3 child is taught today's programme of study. We will update this page when the revised one is published.

Sources: DfE [Primary assessments: future dates](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/primary-assessments-future-dates) (updated 12 October 2026) and the [Curriculum and Assessment Review final report](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/curriculum-and-assessment-review-final-report) and government response, November 2025.

## Four Year 3 problems we wrote, solved step by step

Each one uses statutory Year 3 content. The questions are ours; none comes from a school scheme or a test.

### 1. Column subtraction across a zero

**Question (ours).** 503 − 178.

**Ones first.** 3 take away 8 will not go, and the tens column holds 0, so there is no ten to borrow yet.

**Rename twice.** Change 5 hundreds into 4 hundreds and 10 tens, then change one of those tens into 10 ones. The top number now reads 4 hundreds, 9 tens and 13 ones.

**Subtract.** 13 − 8 = 5, 9 − 7 = 2, 4 − 1 = 3, giving 325. **Check with the inverse:** 325 + 178 = 503.

### 2. A fraction of a set

**Question (ours).** Mia collected 28 conkers and gave away three quarters of them. How many did she give away?

**One quarter first.** Quarters mean 4 equal groups, and 28 ÷ 4 = 7, a fact from the 4 table.

**Three of them.** 3 × 7 = 21.

**Answer.** 21 conkers, leaving 7. A fraction of a set is a division and then a multiplication, which is why Year 3 fractions and the 4 table are taught in the same year.

### 3. How many outfits?

**Question (ours).** A doll has 4 T-shirts and 3 pairs of shorts. How many different outfits can it wear?

**List one row.** The first T-shirt goes with each of the 3 pairs of shorts: 3 outfits.

**Repeat the row.** Each of the 4 T-shirts gives 3 outfits, so the list has 4 rows of 3.

**Answer.** 4 × 3 = 12 outfits. The curriculum calls this a correspondence problem, in which some objects are connected to others, and it is a child's first look at multiplication as counting combinations.

### 4. How long did it last?

**Question (ours).** A film starts at 2:45 pm and ends at 4:10 pm. How long is it?

**To the next hour.** 2:45 to 3:00 is 15 minutes.

**Whole hours.** 3:00 to 4:00 is 1 hour.

**The rest.** 4:00 to 4:10 is 10 minutes. Adding the pieces gives 1 hour and 25 minutes. Counting on in jumps is safer than subtracting times, because there are 60 minutes in an hour, not 100.

**Practice set (ours, answers below).** (1) What is 100 less than 692, and 10 more? (2) 8 × 6. (3) 264 + 359 in columns. (4) Two sevenths plus four sevenths. (5) The perimeter of a rectangle 9 cm long and 4 cm wide. (6) Which number does XI stand for on a clock? (7) 96 ÷ 8. **Answers:** (1) 592 and 702; (2) 48, which is double 8 × 3; (3) 623; (4) six sevenths; (5) 26 cm; (6) 11; (7) 12, because 8 × 12 = 96.

## How does a Year 3 maths tutor help, week by week?

By making place value solid before the columns, the doubling links clear before the 8 table, and fractions visible before they are written. The plan below is ours, adjusted after the first class.

1. **Free first class.** The tutor asks your child to read and build three-digit numbers, recall a few 2, 5 and 10 facts, and explain one subtraction aloud.
2. **Place value with objects on screen.** Hundreds, tens and ones drawn and regrouped on the shared whiteboard, so 146 really can be 130 + 16.
3. **Columns, slowly, with estimates.** Addition first, then subtraction, then exchanging across a zero, always with a rough answer written beside it.
4. **Tables as a family.** 2, then 4 by doubling, then 8 by doubling again; the 3 table practised separately with its own patterns.
5. **Fractions on a line.** Tenths counted forwards and back, unit fractions compared, and simple sums of sevenths or fifths that stay within one whole.
6. **Time and measure in real contexts.** Clock problems, change from a purchase and the perimeter of a drawn shape, because these are where Year 3 maths is used outside school.

## Questions parents ask about Year 3 maths

### What should my child know by the end of Year 3 in maths?

The programme of study expects numbers to 1000, column addition and subtraction with up to three digits, the 3, 4 and 8 times tables, tenths, simple fractions of amounts, perimeter, and telling the time to the minute, including Roman numerals on a clock face.

### Which times tables are taught in Year 3?

The 3, 4 and 8 tables, with their division facts. They build on the 2, 5 and 10 tables from Year 2, and the guidance links the 2, 4 and 8 tables through doubling.

### Is there a test at the end of Year 3?

No. Year 3 has no national test. Schools assess children in their own way, and the next national check is the multiplication tables check in June of Year 4.

### When will my Year 3 child take the multiplication tables check?

For a child in Year 3 during 2026 to 2027, the DfE lists the check in the two weeks from Monday 5 June 2028, when they will be in Year 4.

### Why is column subtraction so hard in Year 3?

Usually because of exchanging, especially across a zero, as in 503 take away 178. Children who can rename 503 as 4 hundreds, 9 tens and 13 ones find it straightforward; children who learned only the steps tend to get lost.

### What fractions do Year 3 children learn?

Tenths, unit fractions such as one fifth, non-unit fractions such as three fifths, equivalent fractions shown with diagrams, and adding or subtracting fractions with the same denominator as long as the answer stays within one whole.

### My child is behind in Year 3 maths. What should we do first?

Start with place value and the Year 2 number bonds and tables. Most Year 3 difficulties trace back to these, and fixing them makes columns and fractions much easier.

### How long should Year 3 maths practice take at home?

A few minutes most days is enough at seven or eight. Short, regular practice of tables and a single column sum does more than one long session at the weekend.
