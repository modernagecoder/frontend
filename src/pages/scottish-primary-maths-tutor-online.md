# Scottish primary maths tutor online

Parents in Scotland often go looking for P7 maths past papers and come back empty handed. That is not a gap in the internet. Primary pupils in Scotland sit no national maths exam: progress through P1 to P7 is judged by the class teacher against three Curriculum for Excellence levels, Early, First and Second, using a document Education Scotland calls the Benchmarks. The short online assessments taken in P1, P4 and P7 carry no pass mark and, in the government's words, need no revision. So the useful question is not which paper to practise but what a child at each level should be able to do. This page answers that from the Benchmarks themselves, then shows how our live lessons teach it.

## What CfE level should my child be at in primary school?

Early level by the end of P1, First level by the end of P4 and Second level by the end of P7: those are the stages at which the Scottish Government reports whether pupils have reached the expected level.

Curriculum for Excellence does not divide primary maths into seven year groups the way England does. It sets out Experiences and Outcomes at a handful of levels, and Education Scotland's Benchmarks, published in June 2017, describe what achieving each level looks like. In the document's own words, "Benchmarks have been developed to provide clarity on the national standards expected within each curriculum area at each level." A child in P3 is usually working through First level, a child in P6 through Second level, and the official snapshot is taken at the end of P1, P4 and P7.

**Primary numeracy by level, a selection of Benchmark statements (Education Scotland, June 2017)**

| Level | Usually reported at | What the Benchmarks expect, in part |
|---|---|---|
| Early | End of P1 | Counts forwards within 0 to 30 from any number and backwards from 20; recognises small groups without counting; doubles to a total of 10 and adds and subtracts mentally to 10; names every coin up to the two pound coin; reads o'clock times |
| First | End of P4 | Reads, writes and orders whole numbers to 1000; explains 867 as 800 + 60 + 7; rounds to the nearest 10 and 100; counts in 2s, 5s, 10s and 100s; solves two step problems; writes fractions to tenths; tells the time to quarter past and quarter to |
| Second | End of P7 | Works with whole numbers to 1 000 000 and decimals to three places; uses multiplication facts to the 10th table; multiplies by two digit numbers; links fractions, decimals and percentages; applies the order of operations; converts between 12 hour and 24 hour time; finds areas of rectangles and right-angled triangles |

Two lines in the Benchmarks matter for parents. The first is that the statements "should not be ticked off individually for assessment purposes": a level is a judgement across a breadth of learning, not a checklist completed. The second is that "It is not necessary for learners to demonstrate mastery of every individual aspect of learning within Benchmarks at a particular level before moving on to the next level", provided there are no major gaps. A teacher also looks for whether the child has "demonstrated application of what they have learned in new and unfamiliar situations", which is why a child who can recite facts but freezes on an unfamiliar word problem may not yet have achieved the level.

### Some topics wait for later levels

The Benchmarks list no Experiences and Outcomes for multiples, factors and primes at Early or First level; they arrive at Second level. Powers and roots wait until Third level, in secondary.

So a P3 child who has never heard of a prime number is not behind. The curriculum simply has not reached it.

### Levels overlap with stages

Some children reach Second level before P7 and begin Third level work; others are still securing First level in P5. The level describes the learning, not the birthday.

When we place a child, we place them by level. For the secondary years that follow, see [our lower secondary maths page](/ks3-maths-tuition-online).

## Are there P7 maths past papers in Scotland?

No national ones. Scotland has no end of primary maths exam, so there is no official P7 paper, past or present. What exists instead is teacher judgement against the Benchmarks and a short adaptive online assessment that the government says children do not need to prepare for.

The National Standardised Assessments for Scotland are taken once in the school year by "Children and young people in P1, P4, P7 and S3", in literacy and numeracy, as part of everyday learning. Their own website is unusually direct: "Children and young people do not have to revise or prepare for these assessments and there is no pass or fail." The questions adapt as the child goes, so that "if a learner is experiencing difficulty, the questions will get easier", and the teacher receives a diagnostic report to plan next steps. Worksheets sold as P7 past papers are therefore someone's practice material, not a paper any Scottish child has sat.

**The National Standardised Assessments in numeracy (standardisedassessment.gov.scot)**

| Question | What the official site says |
|---|---|
| Who takes them | Every child in P1, P4 and P7, and young people in S3 |
| How often | Once within the relevant school year, at a time the school chooses |
| Format | Online, marked automatically, adapting to the learner |
| Pass mark | None: "There is no pass or fail." |
| Time | "There is no time limit." Most P1 learners finish in under 30 minutes; P4 and P7 learners average within 40 |
| Preparation | Not needed; a familiarisation section shows the question types |

## Which maths skills do the Scottish Benchmarks look for?

Beyond the topics, the Benchmarks name eight numeracy and mathematical skills that a child should show with growing independence from Early to Second level. They are the closest thing Scotland has to a marking scheme for primary maths.

Education Scotland is clear that "Numeracy and mathematical skills are embedded in the Experiences and Outcomes and cannot be taught in isolation." In other words, a child does not learn "justifying" on a Tuesday and "estimating" on a Thursday; every topic is a chance to practise all eight. This is how we use the list in class.

**The eight skills named in the Benchmarks, and how our lessons practise them**

| Skill (Benchmarks) | What it looks like in a P5 to P7 lesson (ours) |
|---|---|
| Interpret questions | Underlining what a word problem asks, crossing out information it does not need, drawing a quick bar model |
| Select and communicate processes and solutions | Saying aloud which operation was chosen and why, before working it out |
| Justify choice of strategy used | Comparing two ways of finding 25 per cent and deciding which was quicker |
| Link mathematical concepts | Seeing that dividing by 4 and finding a quarter are the same move |
| Use mathematical vocabulary and notation | Writing units every time, and using words such as product, factor and denominator correctly |
| Use mental agility | Recalling tables facts quickly enough to spend effort on the problem, not the arithmetic |
| Reason algebraically | Finding the missing number in 7 &times; ? = 56 and explaining it with the inverse |
| Determine the reasonableness of a solution | Estimating first, then asking whether the answer could possibly be right |

### Talk is evidence

The Benchmarks ask children to share thinking and explain strategy. In our classes a child who gets the right answer is always asked how they know.

### Estimate first

At First level the Benchmarks expect rounding to 10 and 100 "routinely" to check a solution. We make an estimate the first line of every calculation.

### Unfamiliar problems

Achieving a level includes applying learning in new situations, so every lesson ends with a problem the child has not seen in that form.

## Four primary maths examples, First to Second level

Written by us, not taken from any assessment. Each one shows the reasoning a teacher would hope to hear, because in Scotland the reasoning is part of what is judged.

### 1. Place value and rounding (First level)

**Question (ours).** A school library has 586 books. Round this to the nearest 10 and to the nearest 100, and say which rounding is more useful for ordering new shelves.

**Place value first.** 586 is 500 + 80 + 6. The tens digit is 8 and the ones digit is 6.

**Nearest 10.** 6 ones is closer to 10 than to 0, so 586 rounds up to 590.

**Nearest 100.** 86 is closer to 100 than to 0, so 586 rounds to 600.

**Reasonableness.** For shelves, 600 is safer: it leaves room. That last sentence is the part that shows the skill.

### 2. A fraction of an amount (Second level)

**Question (ours).** A class of 30 votes on a trip; two fifths choose the science centre. How many is that?

**One fifth.** Share 30 into 5 equal groups: 30 &divide; 5 = 6.

**Two fifths.** Two of those groups: 2 &times; 6 = 12.

**Check.** Two fifths is a bit less than a half, and 12 is a bit less than 15. The answer is reasonable.

### 3. Percentages without a calculator (Second level)

**Question (ours).** A jacket costs 40 pounds and is reduced by 15 per cent in a sale. What is the sale price?

**Build 15 per cent.** 10 per cent of 40 is 4. Half of that, 5 per cent, is 2. So 15 per cent is 4 + 2 = 6.

**Subtract.** 40 &minus; 6 = 34, so the sale price is 34 pounds.

**Strategy talk.** Building from 10 per cent and 5 per cent is one route; another is 85 per cent of 40. A child who can explain both is showing the "justify" skill.

### 4. 24 hour time and durations (Second level)

**Question (ours).** A ferry leaves at 09:45 and arrives at 13:20. How long is the crossing, and what is the arrival time on a 12 hour clock?

**Bridge the hours.** 09:45 to 10:00 is 15 minutes. 10:00 to 13:00 is 3 hours. 13:00 to 13:20 is 20 minutes.

**Add.** 3 hours and 35 minutes.

**Convert.** 13:20 is 1:20 pm, because 13 &minus; 12 = 1.

**A practice set for the end of P7 (ours, with answers).** (1) Write 4 307 052 in words. (2) Round 6.48 to one decimal place. (3) 36 &times; 24. (4) 57 &divide; 4, as a decimal. (5) Write three fifths as a decimal and as a percentage. (6) 8 + 6 &times; 3. (7) How many minutes are in two and a quarter hours? (8) A rectangle is 9 cm by 6 cm; a right-angled triangle has the same base and height. Find both areas. **Answers:** (1) four million, three hundred and seven thousand and fifty-two; (2) 6.5; (3) 864; (4) 14.25; (5) 0.6 and 60 per cent; (6) 26, because multiplication comes first; (7) 135; (8) 54 square centimetres and 27 square centimetres.

This set is ours and is not a past paper; none exists. It covers several Second level statements at once, which is closer to how a teacher actually judges the level than any single topic test.

## How do online primary maths lessons work for a child in Scotland?

They start from the level, not the year group, and they ask the child to explain as much as to calculate. Lessons are live, with a teacher who sees and hears the child working.

1. **A free first class to find the level.** The teacher works through a few short problems across number, fractions and time and listens to how the child reasons. Afterwards we tell you, in plain words, whether the child looks secure at Early, First or Second level and where the gaps are.
2. **A plan by organiser.** The Benchmarks group maths into organisers such as number and number processes, fractions, money, time and measurement. We plan the term by those organisers so that nothing major is left out.
3. **Concrete, pictorial, abstract.** Younger children handle counters, coins and a clock; older ones draw bar models and number lines before writing a calculation.
4. **Explain and estimate, every time.** Each problem begins with an estimate and ends with a sentence about whether the answer makes sense.
5. **A short note to parents.** After each block of lessons you get a brief summary of what is now secure and what comes next, in level language your child's teacher will recognise.

### Group or 1-on-1?

A group of 15 to 20 children at a similar level suits a child who is broadly on track and enjoys hearing how others think, which is itself one of the eight skills.

1-on-1 suits a child with a particular gap, for example place value at First level, or one who is racing ahead into Third level work in P6 or P7.

## What Scottish parents ask about primary maths

### Are there P7 maths past papers in Scotland?

Not official ones. Scotland has no national maths exam at the end of primary, so no P7 paper exists. Teachers judge Second level against Education Scotland's Benchmarks instead.

### What level should my child be at in P4?

The Scottish Government reports First level as the expected level by the end of P4. Children work through it from about P2, and some reach it earlier or later.

### What maths should a child know by the end of P7?

Second level includes whole numbers to a million, decimals to three places, multiplication facts to the 10th table, fractions, decimals and percentages as equivalent forms, the order of operations, 24 hour time and simple area. The Benchmarks list the full set.

### Does my child need to prepare for the P7 standardised assessment?

No. The official site says children do not have to revise or prepare, there is no pass or fail, and the questions adapt to the child. A short familiarisation section shows the question types.

### How long does the numeracy assessment take?

There is no time limit. The official guidance says most P1 learners finish in under 30 minutes and P4 and P7 learners average within 40 minutes.

### How many children reach the expected level in numeracy?

In 2024 to 2025, teachers judged 85 per cent of P1 pupils at Early level, 78 per cent of P4 pupils at First level and 79 per cent of P7 pupils at Second level, according to the Scottish Government.

### Is Scottish primary maths the same as KS2?

No. England sets out content year by year in Key Stage 2, while Scotland describes it by level. Much of the content overlaps, but the order and the way progress is judged differ.

### When are lessons?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Every slot is quoted in UK time.

### Which course should my child start with?

Most P3 to P7 children start with Elementary Maths at their level. A P1 or P2 child still at Early level usually begins with Early Maths Foundations; the free first class decides.
