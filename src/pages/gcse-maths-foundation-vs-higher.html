GCSE maths Foundation vs Higher tier: which should you sit?

The short answer. Foundation tier awards grades 1 to 5. Higher tier awards grades 4 to 9, plus an allowed grade 3 for students just below the grade 4 boundary. Both tiers sit three 1 hour 30 minute papers, one of them without a calculator. Sit Higher when grade 6 or above is a realistic target; otherwise Foundation usually protects the grade.

What each tier covers

Higher is Foundation plus a layer on top, not a different course.

The Department for Education sets one list of GCSE maths content and prints it in three styles. Standard type is for everyone. Underlined content is assessed for all students. Bold content is assessed only for "the more highly attaining students", which in practice means the Higher papers.

So a Higher student meets every Foundation topic too, and the shared middle is where grades 4 and 5 live on both tiers. What changes is the share of the paper. On Edexcel's Foundation tier, number and ratio take 22 to 28% each; on Higher, algebra rises to 27 to 33% and number drops to 12 to 18%. Higher papers also give more marks to reasoning and problem solving and fewer to routine methods.

Examples of Higher-only content

  • Solving quadratics by completing the square and with the quadratic formula
  • Finding approximate solutions by iteration
  • The sine rule, the cosine rule and ½ab sin C for the area of a triangle
  • Proving the circle theorems; the equation of a circle and of a tangent to it
  • Inverse and composite functions; algebraic fractions
  • Rationalising denominators; upper and lower bounds
  • Conditional probability from tree diagrams, two-way tables and Venn diagrams

All printed in bold in the DfE subject content.

The real risk

Why the wrong tier can cost a grade either way.

Entering Higher too early

A student working at a grade 3 who sits Higher is aiming at papers where most marks sit above their level. The allowed grade 3 only catches students a small number of marks below the grade 3/4 boundary. Fall further and the result is a U, where Foundation would have given a 3 and a real chance of a 4.

Staying on Foundation too long

Grade 5 is the ceiling on Foundation. A student who could reach a 6 or 7 cannot get it there, however well they do. And a student who wants A level maths will need the Higher-only content anyway, because A level is built on the whole GCSE content.

The decision is about evidence, not ambition. A run of full papers on both tiers, marked properly, shows which side of the line a student is really on.

How to decide

Four questions that settle the tier.

1. What do full mocks show?

Use whole papers sat in time, not topic tests. One good topic test says little about a 1 hour 30 minute paper.

2. Is a 6 or above realistic?

If yes, Higher. If the honest target is a 4 or 5, Foundation lets every mark count toward it.

3. What comes next?

A level maths, or a course that asks for it, points to Higher content whatever the tier sat.

4. What does the school say, and when?

Schools make the entries. Ask when tiers are decided and what evidence they use, then bring that evidence.

Our own rule of thumb, which is advice and not a board rule: if a student is not yet scoring reliably at grade 5 on Foundation papers, we would not move them to Higher. A grade 5 is a strong result on either tier.

See the difference

Three questions we wrote: one Foundation, one shared, one Higher.

Foundation style

A 400 ml drink is 15% fruit juice. How much juice is in it?

10% of 400 = 40
5% of 400 = 20
15% = 40 + 20 = 60 ml

One clean method, shown in full, banks every mark.

On both tiers

Solve 3(x − 2) = 2x + 5.

3x − 6 = 2x + 5
3x − 2x = 5 + 6
x = 11
check: 3 × 9 = 27 and 22 + 5 = 27

Questions like this carry grade 4 and 5 marks on both papers.

Higher only

Solve x² + 4x − 7 = 0, giving answers to 2 decimal places.

x = (−4 ± √(16 + 28)) ÷ 2
= (−4 ± √44) ÷ 2 = −2 ± √11
x = 1.32 or x = −5.32

The quadratic formula is Higher-only content.

Common questions

Foundation and Higher: what parents and students ask.

What is the difference between Foundation and Higher GCSE maths?

They are two tiers of the same GCSE. Foundation awards grades 1 to 5 and Higher awards grades 4 to 9, with an allowed grade 3. Higher includes all the Foundation content plus Higher-only topics such as the quadratic formula, the sine and cosine rules and conditional probability.

What is the highest grade on Foundation tier?

Grade 5. Grades 4 and 5 can be reached on either tier.

What happens if you fail Higher tier GCSE maths?

A student a small number of marks below the grade 3/4 boundary can be awarded an allowed grade 3. Below that, the result is unclassified (U). That is the main reason not to enter Higher too early.

Can you get a grade 4 on Higher tier?

Yes. Grade 4 is the lowest standard grade on Higher. The same grade can also be earned on Foundation.

Are the papers the same length on both tiers?

Yes. Each tier has three papers of 1 hour 30 minutes. AQA and Edexcel papers are worth 80 marks each and OCR papers 100 marks each. All three must be at the same tier and in the same series.

Which paper is non-calculator?

Paper 1 for AQA and Edexcel. For OCR it is Paper 2 on Foundation and Paper 5 on Higher.

Do you get a formulae sheet in GCSE maths?

Yes, in the 2025, 2026 and 2027 exams on both tiers, under Ofqual's rules. Ofqual has since decided the sheets continue for the lifetime of the current specifications.

Who decides the tier?

The school enters the student. Ask when tiers are decided and what evidence is used, and share mock papers and tutor feedback with the maths department.

Do I need Higher tier to do A level maths?

A level maths is built on the whole GCSE content, including the Higher-only topics. Sixth forms set their own entry requirements, so check with the one you are applying to.

Can an adult resit GCSE maths on either tier?

Yes. The tier follows the current working level. For the November series, Edexcel restricts entry to students who were at least 16 on the preceding 31 August. See our adult maths classes.
