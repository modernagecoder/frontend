Quick answer

Yes: Modern Age Coders teaches Python and AI to learners in Huaraz, Peru, live online and in English. Each class has a mentor on live video, with a group of fifteen to twenty or one learner, at any hour you choose. Learners are taught to reason about code, never to copy it.

Close to the national picture

The real limit is the computer. Python is written on a keyboard; a phone can join a video call but cannot carry a programming lesson, so we ask families to check this before booking.

A quick test at home

Join any video call for twenty minutes at the hour you would take class. If the picture holds, the line is fine. If it freezes, try a different hour, or move the computer closer to the router, before deciding.

Headphones with a microphone help more than a faster plan: the mentor hears the learner clearly and the learner hears every instruction, even in a busy house.

Why secundaria students start here

Secundaria in Huaraz follows the national CNEB, whose 35-hour week has no computing area; digital competency is spread across subjects instead. MINEDU's Fábrica de Programadores pilot, which teaches Python and the PCEP exam to 1,000 pupils of 4° and 5° de secundaria, runs in Lima Norte only.

MINEDU's 2025 assessment, reported in INEI's compendium, put 10.7 per cent of Áncash second-year secundaria pupils at the satisfactory level in maths, against 12.7 nationally. That is a reason we teach every teen course with small steps and plenty of checking, never assuming the maths is already secure.

Why university students and adults come

Engineering and science students meet Python in data work long before anyone teaches it to them properly. A few months of structured classes turn copy-and-adjust habits into code they can defend in a lab report or a thesis.

Adults in Huaraz come for practical reasons: spreadsheets that take hours, reports that repeat every month, and curiosity about AI at work. Our page on Python for engineering students in Peru goes further for the first group, and SQL and data analysis for the second.

AI, by age

What does AI mean in our classes for children, teenagers and adults?

Something different at each age, always with the learner in charge. We teach how AI works and where it fails before anyone relies on it.

  1. Children, 9 to 12: AI from the inside. Children write tiny programs that learn from examples, such as a guesser that improves with each answer, and see it get things wrong. No chatbot accounts; the point is understanding, not using a product.
  2. Teenagers, 13 to 18: models and honest testing. Teenagers train simple models on real data, then test them on data the model has not seen. They may use AI coding tools only where the tool's own age rules allow; some need a parent's permission until 18 and some, Claude among them, are for adults only.
  3. University students and adults: AI as a colleague to check. Generative AI, prompting, automation and agents, with one rule on top: if the AI produced it, a person reads, tests and explains it before it is used.

At every age we teach thinking over copying. Paste in something you cannot explain and the mentor will have you reconstruct it piece by piece; those ten minutes tend to teach more than the rest of the week. The glacier project below is a good example: the first answer is easy to compute and wrong in an interesting way, and only reasoning about the data shows why.

Taught in English

How do Huaraz learners cope with classes in English?

Better than many expect, but not all of them at first. Every class is taught only in English, and we would rather you knew that before you book.

The 2025 figures

EF's English Proficiency Index 2025 rates the Áncash region at 475, in its low band, and gives no separate score for Huaraz. Peru scores 519, 52nd of 123 countries, and Lima city 555. In other words, a learner in Huaraz is more likely than a learner in Lima to find a lesson in English hard going at first.

Some learners already use English at work or in their studies, and others have had only school English. No published figure tells us which is which in Huaraz, so we do not guess; the free class tells us what each learner needs.

What the mentors do

Mentors keep sentences short, show before they explain, and type new words in the chat so learners can read them twice. Python itself helps: its keywords are a small set of everyday English words, and the code on the screen is identical whatever language the class is spoken in.

A learner who struggles in a group can switch to one to one, where the mentor can slow down without holding anyone else back. Adults who already read technical English usually settle in within the first two or three classes.

A Huaraz project

What is the real daily temperature on the Llaca glacier?

INAIGEM, Peru's national institute for glacier and mountain-ecosystem research, publishes daily readings from an automatic weather station on the Llaca glacier, in the district of Independencia. Our learners use it to test a shortcut that weather records often rely on.

The data

The dataset is INAIGEM's Registro meteorológico en alta montaña: temperatura y humedad relativa en el glaciar Llaca, part of a research project called PASTURE, published on datosabiertos.gob.pe under the ODC-By licence (Open Data Commons Attribution). INAIGEM says the measurements have been taken on the glacier itself since December 2024.

We saved the file on 10 November 2026 as llaca-inaigem.csv. It holds 147 days, from 2 December 2024 to 27 April 2025, each with the day's average, lowest and highest temperature and the same three figures for relative humidity. Every row passes a first sanity check: the lowest is never above the average, and the average never above the highest.

The question

Many weather records do not keep a true daily average. They keep only the lowest and highest reading and take the middle: (minimum + maximum) / 2. It is quick, and on a day that warms and cools evenly it is close to the truth.

Is it close on a glacier? Version 1 computes both for every day and compares the averages over the whole record. Learners predict the result first; most say the two should be about the same.

The shortcut comes out half a degree warmer. On a glacier where the average day sits under three degrees, half a degree is a lot. But an average of averages can hide what is happening day by day, so Version 2 compares the two on every single day, counts how often the shortcut is too warm, and finds the days where it misses most.

The shortcut is too warm on 140 of the 147 days, so this is a pattern, not bad luck. The worst day, 12 February 2025, had a minimum of 0.37 and a maximum of 6.26, so the shortcut says 3.32, while the station's own average was 1.87. Our reading of the shape: the warmest moments are short, perhaps when the sun breaks through, while most of the day stays near the cold end, so the middle of the two extremes sits above the real average. For humidity the bias flips: the shortcut runs almost three points too dry, which on the same reading would mean the air stays close to saturation most of the day and dries out only briefly.

  1. Children, 9 to 12. Draw a day as a line of 24 dots, most of them cold and three of them warm. Work out the middle of the coldest and warmest dot, then the true average of all 24. Which is bigger?
  2. Teenagers, 13 to 15. Run both versions and explain in four sentences why the shortcut runs warm for temperature and dry for humidity.
  3. Teenagers, 15 to 18. Group the gaps by month. Is the shortcut worse in the wettest months? What would you need from INAIGEM to test the sunshine explanation?
  4. University students and adults. Fit a simple correction, shortcut minus a constant, using December to February, and test it on March and April. How much of the error does it remove, and is it honest to use it on another station?

Huaraz learners ask

Python and AI classes in Huaraz: questions and answers

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. Every class is live and online, in English and nothing else. Mentors use short sentences, show each step on screen and type new words in the chat; a learner who slips into Spanish is understood and helped to say it in English.

Can I take Python classes in Huaraz without going anywhere?

Yes. Learners in Huaraz and Independencia join from a computer at home or work, with the mentor on live video. There is no centre to reach and no travel time.

How young can a learner start Python?

At about nine, when typing is no longer a struggle; before that, Scratch blocks are the gentler road. From thirteen upwards, and for adults of any age, zero experience is fine.

Will my teenager use AI chatbots in class?

Only tools the teenager's age allows under the provider's own terms; some require a parent's permission until 18, and Claude is for adults only. Children under 13 use none: they learn how AI works by coding small examples themselves.

At what times are classes held?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India runs 10 hours 30 minutes ahead of Huaraz all year: 08:30 in Huaraz is 19:00 for the mentor.

What equipment do I need?

A laptop or desktop, an internet line steady enough for video, and a headset with a microphone. A phone cannot carry a Python class. INEI found a computer in 30.3 per cent of Áncash homes in 2025.

Is this useful for a university student at UNASAM?

Python is widely used in engineering and science, and our university-level courses teach it properly, with testing and data work. We have no link with the university, and our certificate is not a university credit.

Do you promise results?

We promise real teaching and work you can show and explain. We make no promise of grades, a university place, a job or a salary.

How are fees paid?

Monthly, in US dollars: the page shows dollars, the card is charged in dollars and your bank converts to soles. Visitors from India see rupees. The trial class is free.

Do you give a certificate?

Where a course has one, it is our own certificate of completion. It is not a Peruvian qualification, and neither MINEDU nor SUNEDU nor any university issues or recognises it.
