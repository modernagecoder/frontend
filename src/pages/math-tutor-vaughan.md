## Maples, tall trees and a two-way table on Vaughan&rsquo;s regional roads

York Region publishes a dataset of the street trees along the regional roads it owns or maintains. We downloaded every Vaughan record and built a two-way table. The counts and probabilities below are ours.

York Region describes the data as the location and information about street trees within regional road right-of-ways owned or maintained by York Region. Its description adds a caveat that a careful student should copy into any report: the data includes all Regional owned street trees in urban areas and partial data available for Regional owned street trees in rural areas. So these are trees on regional roads such as Rutherford Road and Highway 7, not every tree in the city, and the rural count is incomplete by York Region&rsquo;s own account.

**Vaughan rows of the York Region Street Trees layer, downloaded 22 October 2026: our counts**

| Most common trees by common name | Ivory Silk Lilac 2,294; Kentucky Coffee Tree 1,496; Silver Maple 963; Honey-Locust 873; Sugar Maple 791 |
| Maples (genus Acer, all species) | 4,365, the largest of 53 genera |
| Height bands | 0 to 2 m: 399; 2 to 5 m: 11,483; 5 to 10 m: 4,570; 10 to 15 m: 2,075; 15 to 20 m: 1,041; over 20 m: 429; blank: 130 |

**Two-way table, our counts: maples against other trees, by whether the tree is in the 15 m or taller bands (blank heights counted as not tall)**

|  | 15 m or taller | Under 15 m or blank | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maple | 601 | 3,764 | 4,365 |
| Not a maple | 869 | 14,893 | 15,762 |
| Total | 1,470 | 18,657 | 20,127 |

Every probability in MDM4U is a fraction with a carefully chosen denominator, and a two-way table makes the choice visible. Out of all 20,127 trees, 4,365 are maples, so P(maple) is about 0.217. Out of the 4,365 maples, 601 are in the tall bands, so P(tall given maple) is about 0.138, while for other trees it is 869 out of 15,762, about 0.055. The program below rebuilds the table from the raw files and prints each probability:

```
import glob, json
from collections import Counter

trees = []
for name in sorted(glob.glob("vtrees_*.json")):
    trees += [f["attributes"] for f in json.load(open(name))["features"]]

table = Counter()
for t in trees:
    maple = (t["SPECIES"] or "").startswith("Acer")
    tall = t["TREEHEIGHT"] in ("15 - 20 metres", "20+ metres")
    table[maple, tall] += 1

maples = table[True, True] + table[True, False]
print("trees:", len(trees), "maples:", maples)
print("P(maple):", round(maples / len(trees), 4))
print("P(tall | maple):", round(table[True, True] / maples, 4))
print("P(tall | not maple):", round(table[False, True] / (len(trees) - maples), 4))
print("P(maple | tall):", round(table[True, True] / (table[True, True] + table[False, True]), 4))
```

### The year 9999

One hundred and four records give 9999 as the year planted. That is not a prediction; it is a placeholder meaning the year is unknown. A student who computes an average planting year without removing it gets a number a few decades in the future.

### Blank heights

One hundred and thirty records have no height band. We counted them as not tall, and the caption says so. Dropping them instead moves P(tall given maple) only from 0.1377 to about 0.139, and checking that is good practice.

### A genus, not a name

Silver Maple, Sugar Maple, Norway Maple and Hedge Maple all belong to Acer. Grouping by the first word of the scientific name, as our code does, catches every maple; grouping by common name would split them.

## Flip the condition: if a regional-road tree in Vaughan is tall, how likely is it a maple?

Students who are comfortable with P(tall given maple) often stumble when the question is turned around. This problem, ours, uses the two-way table above.

**Question.** A tree is chosen at random from the 20,127 Vaughan records. You are told it is in the 15 m or taller bands. What is the probability that it is a maple? Compare your answer with P(maple) for a tree chosen with no information at all.

**Step 1, change the denominator.** Knowing the tree is tall means we only look at the tall column, which has 1,470 trees. That is the new &ldquo;out of&rdquo;.

**Step 2, count the favourable cases.** Of those 1,470 tall trees, 601 are maples. So P(maple given tall) = 601 &divide; 1,470 &asymp; 0.409, about 41 percent.

**Step 3, compare.** With no information, P(maple) &asymp; 0.217. Learning that the tree is tall almost doubles the chance it is a maple. The two events are not independent; if they were, the two probabilities would be equal.

**Step 4, check with the multiplication rule.** P(maple and tall) = P(maple) &times; P(tall given maple) = (4,365 &divide; 20,127) &times; (601 &divide; 4,365) = 601 &divide; 20,127 &asymp; 0.0299. Dividing that by P(tall) = 1,470 &divide; 20,127 gives 601 &divide; 1,470 again. Two routes, one answer: this is Bayes&rsquo; rule doing nothing more mysterious than changing the denominator.

**Try it yourself.** Using the full height bands in the first table, find the probability that a randomly chosen record is in the 2 to 5 m band, then the probability that two records chosen at random without replacement are both in that band. Explain why the second answer is not simply the first one squared.

## Can a Vaughan student just ask AI for the probability?

It will give a number. Whether the number answers the question that was asked is another matter.

Ask a chatbot for &ldquo;the probability a tall tree is a maple&rdquo; and it may well return 0.138, which is the probability a maple is tall: the condition reversed. The arithmetic is perfect and the answer is wrong. Catching that is exactly the skill MDM4U assesses, and it cannot be delegated.

Our teachers ask students to name the denominator before computing, sketch the table, and only then use a calculator or code to check. Anything handed in for marks is written by the student alone.

Data management is about judging evidence. A tool can count; it cannot decide which count answers the question. The longer argument is in [how clear thinking still beats the shortcut](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
