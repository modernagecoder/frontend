The Informatik-Biber asks children to think like a computer scientist without writing a line of code: put things in order, spot a hidden rule, find the shortest way, decode a message. ## What is the Informatik-Biber, and who can take part?

A free online puzzle contest for every school year from Klasse 3 to 13, run by the Bundesweite Informatikwettbewerbe (BWINF), which needs no programming knowledge at all.

### What BWINF says it is

BWINF describes the Informatik-Biber as the largest school competition in informatics in Germany: an online quiz of puzzles that trains informatical thinking and costs nothing to enter. Its FAQ says the Biber is the German partner of the international Bebras Challenge, which began in Lithuania in 2004, and that the German contest has run since 2007.

The 2026 round is the 20th. BWINF counts more than 580,000 participants in 2025 from more than 3,224 schools; at the first Biber in 2007 the figure was 21,802.

### Who may enter

Pupils of every school type from Klasse 3 to 13, with no prior knowledge needed. BWINF lists German schools in Germany and abroad, and also out-of-school learning places with a coordinator, such as open all-day schools, lunchtime clubs and coding groups.

There is no minimum number: a school may take part with a single pupil. Pupils work alone or in pairs, on a computer or tablet with internet access, one device per team.

A Biber task is usually a short story with a picture: beavers sorting logs, a message hidden in coloured stones, a robot that follows arrows. Under the story sits a real idea from computer science, such as sorting, encoding, graphs or a stack. That is why the contest suits children who have never coded, and also why it is worth practising: the ideas repeat, even when the stories change.

## How does a child in Germany enter the Biber in 2026?

Only through a school or another registered institution. BWINF says plainly that pupils cannot sign themselves up; an adult at the school or club must act as coordinator.

1. **A coordinator registers.** From September a teacher, or another responsible adult at the institution, registers as coordinator at login.bwinf.de. BWINF says no knowledge of informatics is needed for this role.
2. **The coordinator enters the pupils.** Pupils are added at admin.informatik-biber.de and each gets their own login. BWINF's FAQ says this can be done up to the weekend before the contest.
3. **Practice on the real platform.** The Schnupper-Biber has been open since 14 September 2026: six tasks in each age group and up to 30 minutes, with the score shown at the end. It closes a few days before the contest.
4. **Sit the contest.** The Biberwochen 2026 run from 9 to 20 November 2026. The school chooses any day and time within those two weeks, and the contest fits into a single lesson.
5. **Results and certificates.** BWINF publishes results in December 2026 and sends certificates and prizes in January 2027. Every participant receives a certificate.

## How are Biber points counted, and does a wrong answer cost anything?

It depends on the school year. The rule changes at Klasse 5, and it is the single most useful thing a child can know before the contest.

### Klasse 3 and 4

Correct answers earn points and wrong answers cost nothing, according to BWINF's Teilnehmen page. A child in these years loses nothing by answering every task, so we teach them to give every question a sensible try before time runs out.

### From Klasse 5

A wrong answer now subtracts points, and an unanswered task leaves the score unchanged. To keep things fair, everyone starts with a positive balance, so the lowest possible result is zero. Harder tasks are worth more and cost more.

For these pupils we practise one habit above all: check the answer against the story before you click.

The time limit matters too. Fifteen tasks in 40 minutes, the load from Klasse 7, leaves under three minutes a task, and the hardest ones need longer. Our practice rounds are timed in the same way so that a pupil learns to leave a stubborn task, finish the rest, and come back.

## What do Biber puzzles train? Four practice puzzles of our own

These four puzzles were written by Modern Age Coders for this page. They are not Biber tasks and are not taken from any contest. Each one is solved by reasoning first; for two of them we then let a program try every possible case, the habit that turns a good guess into a certain answer.

### Puzzle 1: lantern signals (ours)

A beaver signals across the pond with four lanterns in a row, each on or off. Two lanterns that are on may never stand side by side, or their light runs together. How many different signals are possible?

**Worked solution.** Count by the last lantern. If it is off, the first three can be any safe signal of three lanterns. If it is on, the one before must be off, and the first two can be any safe signal of two. Two lanterns give 3 safe signals and three give 5, so four give 5 + 3 = 8. Each new lantern adds the two previous counts together.

### Puzzle 2: the narrow tunnel (ours)

Beavers 1, 2, 3 and 4 wait in that order outside a tunnel so narrow that only the last one in can come out. A beaver may go in at any moment, and the beaver nearest the exit may leave at any moment. Which order of leaving is impossible: 4 3 2 1, 2 1 4 3, 3 1 4 2 or 1 2 3 4?

**Worked solution.** For 3 to leave first, 1, 2 and 3 must all be inside, with 2 nearest the exit once 3 has gone. So 1 cannot come out next. The answer is 3 1 4 2. The tunnel is what computer scientists call a stack: last in, first out.

**Checking Puzzle 1: try every signal** (Python)
```python
from itertools import product

def safe(signal):
    return "11" not in signal

for n in range(3, 8):
    signals = ["".join(s) for s in product("01", repeat=n)]
    good = [s for s in signals if safe(s)]
    print(n, "lanterns:", len(good), "safe signals")
    if n == 4:
        print("  ", " ".join(good))
```
It prints 3 lanterns: 5 safe signals, then 4 lanterns: 8 safe signals followed by the eight signals 0000 0001 0010 0100 0101 1000 1001 1010, then 5 lanterns: 13 safe signals, 6 lanterns: 21 safe signals and 7 lanterns: 34 safe signals.

The program does not think; it simply writes down all sixteen on and off patterns for four lanterns and throws away the ones with two lights together. Eight survive, which agrees with the reasoning. Running it for longer rows shows the numbers 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, each the sum of the two before. Spotting that rule in the output and then explaining why it must hold is exactly the step from checking to understanding that we want a pupil to make.

**Checking Puzzle 2: every order of four beavers** (Python)
```python
from itertools import permutations

def can_leave(order):
    tunnel = []
    waiting = [1, 2, 3, 4]
    for beaver in order:
        while beaver not in tunnel and waiting:
            tunnel.append(waiting.pop(0))
        if not tunnel or tunnel[-1] != beaver:
            return False
        tunnel.pop()
    return True

orders = list(permutations([1, 2, 3, 4]))
possible = [o for o in orders if can_leave(o)]
print("Possible exit orders:", len(possible), "of", len(orders))
for test in [(4, 3, 2, 1), (2, 1, 4, 3), (3, 1, 4, 2), (1, 2, 3, 4)]:
    print(test, can_leave(test))
```
It prints Possible exit orders: 14 of 24, then (4, 3, 2, 1) True, (2, 1, 4, 3) True, (3, 1, 4, 2) False and (1, 2, 3, 4) True.

Of the 24 ways four beavers could leave, only 14 are possible through a last-in, first-out tunnel, and 3 1 4 2 is one of the ten that are not. The function copies what the beavers do: let beavers in until the one we want is inside, and fail if it is not the one nearest the exit.

### Puzzle 3: the hidden rule (ours)

A beaver stamps a code on each log: 2 becomes 4, 3 becomes 9, 5 becomes 25, and 10 becomes 100. A careless helper says 7 becomes 14. What should 7 become, and what mistake did the helper make?

**Worked solution.** Every code is the number multiplied by itself, so 7 becomes 49. The helper saw only the first pair, where doubling and squaring give the same answer, and stopped checking. A rule is only safe when it fits every example.

### Puzzle 4: the shortest swim (ours)

Four lodges, A, B, C and D, are joined by channels: A to B takes 4 minutes, A to C takes 1, C to B takes 2, B to D takes 1 and C to D takes 5. What is the quickest swim from A to D?

**Worked solution.** The direct-looking route A, C, D takes 6 minutes, and A, B, D takes 5. But A, C, B, D takes 1 + 2 + 1 = 4 minutes, the quickest. The route with the most stops wins, which is the lesson: compare every route, not only the obvious ones.

1. **Start.** Solve all four puzzles on paper and write one sentence on why each answer is right.
2. **Check.** Run the two programs and compare their output with your answers.
3. **Stretch.** Change the lantern rule so that three lights may not be on in a row. Predict the count for five lanterns before running the program.
4. **Explain.** Tell someone at home, in your own words, why the tunnel is a stack and a queue at a bakery is not.

### For children

Read the question twice. Half of all Biber mistakes are answers to a different question.
If two answers look right, one of them breaks a rule hidden in the story. Find it.

### For parents

Ask your child to explain one puzzle to you each week. Explaining is where the learning sticks.
Praise the method, not the score. The Biber rewards careful thinking, and so do we.
