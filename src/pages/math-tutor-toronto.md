A drop in a math mark usually has a cause older than the test that revealed it. A Grade 11 student stuck on transformations is often missing a Grade 9 idea about slope; a Grade 4 child who freezes on word problems may simply never have been asked to explain a step aloud. ## Why the average day on the Toronto Island ferry is not a typical day

The City of Toronto publishes ticket counts for the Island ferries as open data. We took every 2025 row, added up each day ourselves, and found a mean far above the median. The figures are our calculations from the City's file, not numbers the City publishes.

The dataset is called Toronto Island Ferry Ticket Counts, kept by the City's Fleet Services division. Its description says the counts are provided in 15 minute intervals and are updated hourly, and that the ferries operate year-round out of Jack Layton Ferry Terminal. Our download on 22 October 2026 held 284,387 rows, starting on 1 May 2015, each with a timestamp, a redemption count and a sales count. We used only redemptions, which are tickets used to board, and only calendar year 2025.

**Daily ferry ticket redemptions in 2025, by month: our sums of the City of Toronto 15-minute rows, downloaded 22 October 2026**

| Month of 2025 | Days with data | Mean per day | Median per day | Highest day |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| January | 30 | 571.0 | 586.5 | 829 |
| March | 31 | 877.6 | 838 | 1,518 |
| May | 31 | 3,769.5 | 3,180 | 12,436 |
| June | 30 | 7,992.2 | 5,947.5 | 18,242 |
| July | 31 | 11,784.0 | 10,124 | 24,341 |
| August | 31 | 12,283.3 | 12,020 | 21,160 |
| September | 30 | 5,555.7 | 4,384.5 | 19,598 |
| November | 30 | 983.2 | 874 | 2,288 |
| All of 2025 | 364 | 4,191.9 | 1,690 | 24,341 |

Look at the bottom row. The mean day in 2025 saw 4,191.9 redemptions, yet the median day saw only 1,690. Only 121 of the 364 days were above the mean at all, while 139 days had fewer than 1,000 redemptions. A short run of enormous summer days, 47 of them above 10,000, drags the mean upward. The median ignores how large those days were and simply finds the middle of the sorted list, so it describes an ordinary Toronto day far better.

The short program below reproduces our yearly figures. It reads the file line by line, keeps 2025, adds the 15-minute counts into one total per date, and prints the count of days, the mean, the median and the busiest date. It needs only Python 3 and the CSV in the same folder:

```
import statistics
from collections import defaultdict

day_total = defaultdict(int)
with open("toronto-island-ferry-ticket-counts.csv", encoding="utf-8") as file:
    next(file)  # the first line holds the column names
    for line in file:
        _id, stamp, redeemed, sold = line.strip().split(",")
        if stamp.startswith("2025-"):
            day_total[stamp[:10]] += int(redeemed)

values = sorted(day_total.values())
print("days:", len(values))
print("mean:", round(statistics.mean(values), 1))
print("median:", statistics.median(values))
print("busiest:", max(day_total, key=day_total.get), values[-1])
```

### Quartiles tell the shape

Our lower quartile was 712.25 and our upper quartile 5,885.5. The gap from the median up to the upper quartile is more than four times the gap down to the lower quartile, the numerical signature of a right-skewed distribution.

### Weekends in summer

In July 2025 the 23 weekdays averaged 9,881.9 redemptions and the 8 weekend days averaged 17,252.5. A student who reports one July average for the whole month has blended two different populations into one number.

### A gap in the record

The file has rows for 364 dates in 2025, not 365: nothing is dated 26 January. Before computing anything, a careful student decides whether that day was quiet, closed or simply missing, and says which assumption the answer depends on.

## One busy Saturday, worked through as a Grade 12 data question

This is the kind of question we set a Toronto student after the ferry lesson. The data come from our sums for Saturday 12 July 2025, the busiest day in the 2025 file; the question and the solution are ours.

**Ferry redemptions by hour on Saturday 12 July 2025, our sums of the City of Toronto 15-minute rows (selected hours)**

| Hour starting | Redemptions |
|---|---|
| 9:00 | 1,142 |
| 10:00 | 2,030 |
| 11:00 | 2,657 |
| 12:00 | 3,158 |
| 13:00 | 3,469 |
| 14:00 | 2,299 |
| 15:00 | 2,837 |
| 16:00 | 2,196 |
| Whole day | 24,341 |

**Question.** What share of the day's redemptions fell between 10:00 and 13:59? Is it fair to say that the ferry was busiest at lunchtime?

**Step 1, add the four hours.** 2,030 + 2,657 + 3,158 + 3,469 = 11,314 redemptions. These are four of our own hourly sums, so adding them is legitimate; we are not adding someone else's rounded figures to manufacture a total.

**Step 2, divide by the day.** 11,314 divided by 24,341 is about 0.465, so roughly 46.5 percent of the day's boarding happened in four of the twenty-four hours.

**Step 3, test the claim.** The single busiest hour began at 13:00, not at noon, and the 15:00 hour was busier than the 14:00 hour, so the pattern is not a smooth peak. A student who writes &ldquo;busiest around midday, with a second rise mid-afternoon&rdquo; has read the table; one who writes &ldquo;busiest at lunch&rdquo; has guessed. In MDM4U terms, the conclusion has to match the evidence, not the expectation.

**Try it yourself.** Using the table, find the mean redemptions per hour from 9:00 to 16:59 and compare it with the median of those eight hours. Which is larger, and why is the difference small here when it was huge across the whole year?

**The CEMC February contests for 2026 to 2027, as CEMC lists them (read 22 October 2026)**

## Why a Toronto teen still needs to do the math when AI can

Because the answer an AI gives is only as good as the question, and only a person who understands the math knows whether the question was right.

Give a chatbot the ferry file and ask for the average day, and it will happily report the mean. It will not stop to ask whether the mean is the right summary for a set of numbers that is mostly quiet winter days with a few huge summer ones. That decision is the mathematics, and it belongs to the student.

Our teachers ask students to predict before they calculate, show the step that matters, and explain the result in a sentence. Older students may check work with tools, but marked school work stays the student's own, and the thinking is never handed over.

Ontario math asks students to reason, model and communicate; those are exactly the parts a tool cannot do for them. The longer argument is in [our essay on why thinking skills outlast any tool](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
