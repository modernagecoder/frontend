## How do you choose an online coding class for a child?

Ask what happens when your child gets something wrong. Every course looks good when things work; the difference between a strong class and a weak one shows at the moment of a mistake.

### Live, or recorded videos?

A recorded course can explain a loop beautifully, but it cannot see that your daughter has put the "forever" block in the wrong place, and it cannot ask her why. Children of 6 to 12 rarely know how to describe what went wrong, so they need someone watching.

In a live class the mentor sees the screen, waits a few seconds, and then asks a question instead of giving the answer. That pause, repeated every week, is most of what teaching a child to think looks like.

### Group, or one to one?

A group of fifteen to twenty children of a similar age is lively: children see other solutions to the same puzzle and learn to explain their own. It suits a child who is happy to speak up, even a little.

One to one suits a child who is very young, very shy, new to English, or racing ahead. The mentor sets the pace for one learner. Many families start one way and switch after a month; that is normal.

Whichever provider you try, use the free class to ask questions like these. They are our list, written for parents, and we answer every one of them in the first class.

1. **Will my child type and build, or mostly watch?** In a good class the child's hands are on the mouse or keyboard within the first ten minutes.
2. **Will it be the same teacher each week?** A child explains a bug far more easily to someone who already knows how they think.
3. **How are groups formed?** By age and starting level. A six year old should never be in the same class as a twelve year old.
4. **What happens after a mistake?** Listen for "we ask them to predict, then test" rather than "we show the answer".
5. **Which AI tools will my child use?** The right answer names tools that allow children, and none that need an adult account.
6. **What will I hear about progress?** Ask for notes that describe what was built and what was hard, not a count of hours.

## What age should kids start coding?

Six is a good age to start with blocks, and nine is a good age to start typing. Those ages line up neatly with the Peruvian primaria years.

### Primaria in a few lines

Peru's Ley General de Educación (Ley 28044) says primaria "dura seis años", and its Reglamento says it serves children "a partir de los 6 años de edad". So a Peruvian child is usually in 1st grade of primaria at 6 and in 6th grade at 11.

Families arriving from abroad should know that MINEDU places a child by age on 31 March of each year, the "edad normativa". A child who turns six in April may therefore be a grade behind a classmate born in February. We group children by age and level, not by grade, so this never matters in our classes.

### Our age path

**6 to 8:** Scratch. Children drag blocks, watch a character move and learn sequence, repetition and simple conditions without needing to read long instructions.

**9 to 10:** the first typed Python, short programs that draw or count. **11 to 12:** longer projects, games with scores, first data and a careful look at how AI makes decisions. A child who has done well in Scratch often moves to Python early; the free class tells us.

If your child is ten and wondering about Python specifically, [our Spanish-language page on Python for children of 9 to 12](/curso-de-python-para-ninos-peru) explains when to move from blocks to code. For bilingual families, [clases de programación para niños en inglés](/clases-de-programacion-para-ninos-en-ingles) sets out why English-taught coding helps at this age.

## Do Peruvian primary schools teach coding?

There is no coding lesson in the national primaria timetable. MINEDU's curriculum, the CNEB, leaves computing out of its list of areas and treats digital skills as something all subjects share.

### What the curriculum sets

In the CNEB's primaria plan of studies, Matemática has 5 teaching periods a week in grades 1 and 2 and 4 periods from grade 3, and Inglés has 2 periods in grades 1 and 2 and 3 periods from grade 3. No line of that table is called computing or programming.

Competency 28, on working in virtual environments, is filed under the CNEB heading for skills that cross every area ("competencias transversales"). In practice, how much a child codes at school depends on the school and the teacher, not on the national plan.

### Where our classes fit

MINEDU reopened 53,631 schools for 6,225,000 pupils on 16 March 2026, the start of the 2026 school year. Our classes do not replace any of that; they run alongside the school year and through the January to March summer break.

In 2025 MINEDU's national assessment, the ENLA, tested 4th grade of primaria in reading and mathematics. Coding helps both in an honest, indirect way: a child who writes programs reads instructions carefully and checks numbers twice. We make no promise about any school result.

## What does a first Python lesson look like for a child?

Here is one we teach to children of about 9 to 11 in their first weeks of typed code. They build a pyramid out of text, and on the way they discover a piece of mathematics nobody told them about.

### The idea

In Python you can multiply text by a number. "#" * 3 gives ###. Children find this funny at first, then useful: it is the quickest way to draw a row of bricks.

We ask for a pyramid four rows high, with one brick on top and two more on every row below. Most children write something like Version 1 within a few minutes, and it runs first time.

### The catch

It runs, but it draws a slope, not a pyramid. Every row starts at the left edge. The mentor does not point this out; we ask the child to compare the screen with the picture they had in mind and say what is different, in English.

The fix is to push each row to the right with spaces. The top row needs the most spaces and the bottom row none, so the number of spaces is the height minus the row number.

**Version 1: one brick, then two more on every row** (Python)
```python
height = 4
for row in range(1, height + 1):
    print("#" * (2 * row - 1))
```

**What Version 1 prints** (Output)
```output
#
###
#####
#######
```
Exact output when we ran it with Python 3: four rows of 1, 3, 5 and 7 bricks, all starting at the left edge.

**Version 2: spaces in front, and a running total** (Python)
```python
height = 4
total = 0
for row in range(1, height + 1):
    spaces = height - row
    bricks = 2 * row - 1
    total = total + bricks
    print(" " * spaces + "#" * bricks)
print("Bricks used:", total)
```

**What Version 2 prints** (Output)
```output
   #
  ###
 #####
#######
Bricks used: 16
```
Exact output of our run: a centred pyramid, then Bricks used: 16.

Now the surprise. We ask the child to change the first line to height = 10 and, before running it, to guess how many bricks the pyramid will need. Most guess something near 50. The program ends with Bricks used: 100. For four rows the answer was 16, which is 4 times 4; for ten rows it is 10 times 10. The rows hold 1, 3, 5, 7 bricks and so on, and **odd numbers added in order always make a square number**.

Nobody had to teach the rule. The child predicted, ran the program, was surprised, and then found the pattern by testing a third height. That is the habit we want: guess first, test second, and trust the test over the guess. Children who meet a pattern this way remember it far longer than a rule copied from a board.

1. **Ages 6 to 8, on paper.** Draw the pyramid with counters or squares of paper, one row at a time. Count the counters after three rows, then four. What do you notice?
2. **Ages 8 to 9, in Scratch.** Use a pen sprite and a repeat block to stamp a row of squares. Make each new row two squares longer and start it a little to the right.
3. **Ages 9 to 11, in Python.** Type Version 2 without looking. Then try a height of 7 and predict the total before you run it.
4. **Ages 11 and 12.** Turn the pyramid upside down with as few changes as possible. Then tell your mentor, in English, what stayed the same and why.

## How will I know my child is learning?

Through things your child can do and say, not hours logged. These stages are ours; they decide which group a child joins and what the mentor works on next.

**Our stages for children of 6 to 12**

| Stage | Usual age | Can do on screen | Can say in English |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Steps | 6 to 7 | Put blocks in order so a character moves and speaks | "First it walks, then it jumps." |
| 2. Loops | 7 to 8 | Repeat an action and stop it at the right moment | "This repeats ten times." |
| 3. Choices | 8 to 9 | Make a game react to a key or a touch | "If it touches the wall, it stops." |
| 4. Typing | 9 to 10 | Write a short Python program that draws or counts | "I changed the number and it moved." |
| 5. Patterns | 10 to 11 | Predict an output, run it, explain the difference | "I guessed 50, but it was 100 because..." |
| 6. Projects | 11 to 12 | Plan and finish a small game or tool alone | "Here is what it does and how I tested it." |

### For parents

Ask your child to show you the last thing they built and to explain one part. If they can, the class is working.
Stage 5 is the pyramid lesson. A child who reaches it starts asking "why?" about numbers well beyond the class.

### For children

Guess what your program will do before you press run. Being wrong is how you find the interesting part.
If you are stuck, say what you expected and what happened. That sentence is half the answer.

## What parents in Peru ask about online coding for kids

### Are the classes in Spanish?

No. Every class is live, online and taught in English only. Mentors speak slowly and clearly, adapt to your child's pace and accent, and show each step on the shared screen, so a child who is still learning English can follow. If you prefer to read about the classes in Spanish, our Spanish-language page describes them, but the teaching itself is always in English.

### From what age can a child join?

Six. Between 6 and 8 a child works in Scratch, dragging coloured blocks, so reading long instructions is not needed. From about 9, most children are ready to type Python. In Peru that usually means Scratch in the first years of primaria and Python from 4th or 5th grade, though we place children by age and level, not by grade.

### Is online coding good for a 10 year old?

Yes, if it is live. A ten year old learns quickly but rarely knows how to describe a problem, so a recorded video cannot help when they are stuck. In a live class the mentor sees the screen, asks the right question and lets the child find the fix.

### Group or one-to-one: which is better for my child?

Groups of fifteen to twenty children of a similar age suit children who enjoy talking about their work. One to one suits very young or shy children, children new to English and children who are racing ahead. You can switch after the first month if the format does not fit.

### When are classes in Peru time?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. With Peru five hours behind UTC and India five and a half ahead, the gap is ten and a half hours on every day of the year: 17:30 in Peru is 04:00 the next morning for the mentor.

### Can my child join from a phone or tablet?

Not for coding. Coding needs a laptop or desktop with a keyboard and a mouse or trackpad, plus a steady connection; a headset with its own microphone makes listening much easier. A tablet can show the video, but typing code and dragging blocks on it is slow and frustrating.

### Does school in Peru already teach coding?

Rarely as its own lesson. MINEDU's national curriculum, the CNEB, gives primaria no computing area; digital skills are a transversal competency shared by every subject. Some private schools add their own computing lessons, so ask your child's school what they cover.

### Will my child use ChatGPT or other AI tools?

Never on adult accounts. Anthropic sets 18 as the minimum age for Claude, and OpenAI asks that a parent or guardian agree before a minor uses ChatGPT. Children study how AI behaves with child-appropriate tools, once they have already worked the problem out by themselves.

No. Modern Age Coders is a private online school whose mentors work from India, with no tie to MINEDU or to any Peruvian school. We give no Peruvian marks or qualifications; a child who finishes a course gets a completion certificate issued by us.
