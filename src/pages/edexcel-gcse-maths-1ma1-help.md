# Edexcel GCSE Maths 1MA1 help

Pearson's mark schemes for GCSE Maths use a letter most students never notice: P, for process. It sits beside the familiar M for method and A for accuracy, and it goes on the problem-solving questions, where there is more than one sensible way in. Pearson's content guidance says examiners must credit any valid route, and that a partly finished solution still earns the P marks it has reached. The same guidance has a sting: where a question says working must be shown, "the correct solution with no working will score no marks." This page sets out how 1MA1 is examined, what the P mark changes about revision, what Pearson itself says about method and calculus and graphs, and three worked problems of our own written to be marked the Edexcel way.

At a glance: Qualification: Pearson Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Mathematics 1MA1; Foundation: Grades 1 to 5; Higher: Grades 4 to 9, grade 3 allowed; Papers: Three, 80 marks, 1 hour 30 minutes; Paper 1: No calculator; Classes: Group of 15 to 20, or 1-on-1; When: Weekdays or weekends, flexible; First class: Free. ## What are the three Edexcel 1MA1 papers, and when are they in 2027?

One tier, three papers, one series. Every figure below is from Pearson's Issue 2 specification or its final timetables for November 2026 and summer 2027.

| Paper and code | Calculator | Marks and time | November 2026 | Summer 2027 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Paper 1, 1MA1/1F or 1MA1/1H | No | 80 marks, 1 hour 30 minutes | Wednesday 4 November, morning | Friday 14 May, morning |
| Paper 2, 1MA1/2F or 1MA1/2H | Yes | 80 marks, 1 hour 30 minutes | Friday 6 November, morning | Thursday 27 May, morning |
| Paper 3, 1MA1/3F or 1MA1/3H | Yes | 80 marks, 1 hour 30 minutes | Monday 9 November, morning | Monday 14 June, morning |

Pearson is explicit that "Individual papers are not graded." The grade comes from the total across all three, so a weaker non-calculator paper on 14 May 2027 can be made up on the calculator papers later in the series. November entry is restricted: the specification says a November candidate "must be at least 16 years of age on the preceding 31st August", which makes November a resit series for students past Year 11.

### Grade 3 allowed

Higher tier awards grades 4 to 9, and the specification adds "(grade 3 allowed)". A Higher student who falls just short of grade 4 can still be given a 3 rather than a U.

It is a safety net, not a strategy. A student working securely at grade 3 normally has more to gain on Foundation, where grade 4 and grade 5 marks are spread across the whole paper.

### Topic shares, as ranges

Pearson prints its topic weightings as bands rather than single figures. On Foundation, number and ratio each take 22 to 28 per cent; on Higher, algebra takes 27 to 33 per cent and number drops to 12 to 18 per cent.

A range means one paper can lean a little more on a topic than another. Practise whole papers, not just the biggest topic.

## What is a P mark in an Edexcel GCSE Maths mark scheme?

A P mark is a process mark, given for a correct step in a problem-solving question, by whatever valid route the student chose. It is the part of the Edexcel mark scheme that rewards thinking on paper.

Pearson's content guidance explains that "The P (process) mark denotes those questions that have been classified as AO3 (problem solving) and therefore may have a number of different methods of solution." The mark scheme shows the commonest methods, but examiners are told to apply it to other methods too. And, in Pearson's words, "The P mark works in exactly the same way as the M (method) mark": a partly correct solution earns the process marks it has reached, as long as the working shown is a possible route to the answer.

**Method and process marks, as Pearson's content guidance describes them**

| Mark | Used on | How it is earned |
|---|---|---|
| M (method) | Questions testing a technique | A correct method shown, credited even when the final answer is wrong |
| P (process) | Questions classified as AO3, problem solving | A correct step by any valid route; a partial solution earns the P marks it reaches |
| No working shown | Questions that say working must be shown | Pearson: the correct solution with no working will score no marks |

What the P mark changes is the value of an unfinished answer. A student who has done two of four steps in a problem and stops has not scored zero: they have probably scored two process marks. A student who does all four steps in their head and writes only the answer may score full marks, or, if the question says "show your working", nothing at all.

### Start every problem

Because process marks are cumulative, the first written step of a hard question is worth more than an empty space and a perfect attempt at the next one.

### Write the route

One line per step, with a word saying what it is: "total area", "cost per tin". Examiners can only credit a route they can see.

### Obey "show working"

Pearson is clear that in those questions "the correct solution with no working will score no marks". A calculator answer copied down is not working.

## What does Pearson's content guidance say about methods, calculus and graphs?

Pearson's senior examiners wrote a commentary under every 1MA1 content statement. A few of their notes settle arguments students and tutors have every year.

**From Pearson's GCSE (9-1) Mathematics Content Guidance, Issue 5**

| Topic | What the guidance says | What it means for practice |
|---|---|---|
| Written calculation (N2) | Examiners "will not be prescribing the method that candidates should use" | Grid, column or any correct written method earns the marks |
| Turning points of quadratics | "No use of calculus is expected", but a correct AS or A level method "will be awarded marks" | Completing the square or symmetry is enough; calculus is allowed, not needed |
| Area under a curve | "a maximum of four equal intervals is expected" | Practise trapezium estimates with up to four strips, not ten |
| Kinematics formulae | Students may rearrange or substitute into them, but "no application of these formulae is expected" | Treat suvat as algebra practice, not as mechanics |
| Imperial units | Conversions from metric to imperial "will be given within the question" | Learn metric conversions; read imperial ones off the paper |

The specification adds two structural points. Content is printed in three styles: standard type for everyone, underlined type that is assessed for all students, and bold type that only Higher students are assessed on. And recalling facts is a small share of the marks: under the first assessment objective, the strand for recalling facts, terminology and definitions "Should be no more than 10% of AO1". Most marks go to using what is known, not to remembering it.

### Pearson's free practice material

Pearson's getting started guide lists resources schools can use alongside past papers. Two are unusually useful for revision. Shadow Papers are "alternative versions of the past exam papers with the numbers or contexts changed", so a student who has seen a paper can sit a fresh one of the same shape.

Reordered papers are past papers "re-ordered in descending mean performance score for all students", so the questions run from the ones most students got right to the ones most got wrong.

### Ask the school for these

Crossover question papers collect the questions that appear on both tiers, which is where grades 4 and 5 are decided. "Aiming for" practice tests are built around a target grade.

Several of these sit behind Pearson's teacher login. A school maths department can usually share them; we work from the public past papers and our own questions.

## Three problems of our own, marked the Edexcel way

Written by us to practise the habits Pearson's guidance describes, and not taken from any Pearson paper. The bracketed notes are our guess at where process or method marks would sit, not a Pearson mark scheme.

### Problem 1, both tiers: a process question

**Problem (ours).** A garden fence has 14 panels. Each panel needs 0.6 litres of wood stain for one coat, and the fence needs two coats. Stain is sold in 2.5 litre tins. How many tins are needed?

**Step 1, stain per coat.** 14 × 0.6 = 8.4 litres [process].

**Step 2, two coats.** 8.4 × 2 = 16.8 litres [process].

**Step 3, tins.** 16.8 ÷ 2.5 = 6.72 [process], so **7 tins**, because 6 tins hold only 15 litres [final answer, with the reason].

### What the steps protect

A student who gets step 2 wrong (forgetting the second coat) but divides correctly has still shown two valid process steps. A student who writes "7" with no working has bet every mark on the final number.

Rounding up, not to the nearest whole number, is the classic accuracy trap in this kind of question: 6.72 tins means 7 must be bought.

### Problem 2, Higher: a turning point without calculus

**Problem (ours).** Find the coordinates of the turning point of y = x² + 6x + 4.

**Complete the square.** x² + 6x + 4 = (x + 3)² &minus; 9 + 4 = (x + 3)² &minus; 5 [method].

**Read it off.** (x + 3)² is never negative and is zero when x = &minus;3, so the minimum value of y is &minus;5.

**Answer.** The turning point is (&minus;3, &minus;5), and it is a minimum.

### Why this route

Pearson's guidance says no calculus is expected here. Completing the square is the GCSE route, and it gives the minimum value and its position in one line.

A student who already knows differentiation from extra study may use it and will be credited, but should still be able to complete the square, because other questions ask for that form directly.

### Problem 3, Higher: area under a curve

**Problem (ours).** A car's speed in m/s at times 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 seconds is 0, 5, 8, 9 and 9. Use four equal strips to estimate the distance travelled in the 8 seconds.

**Trapezia, width 2.** ½ × 2 × (0 + 5) = 5; ½ × 2 × (5 + 8) = 13; ½ × 2 × (8 + 9) = 17; ½ × 2 × (9 + 9) = 18 [method].

**Total.** 5 + 13 + 17 + 18 = 53, so about **53 metres**.

**Interpret.** The speed rises more and more slowly, so the curve bows above each straight-topped strip and 53 m is an underestimate.

### Four strips, as expected

Pearson's guidance says "a maximum of four equal intervals is expected" when estimating area under a curve, so this is the full scale of the task at GCSE.

The last line, saying whether the estimate is too high or too low and why, is the reasoning part of the question and is often where Higher students drop the final mark.

**Practice set (ours, with answers).** (1) A recipe for 6 people uses 450 g of flour; how much for 10 people? Show each step. (2) Write x² &minus; 8x + 3 in the form (x &minus; a)² &minus; b and state the turning point. (3) Show that 0.4 m³ is 400 000 cm³. (4) A rectangle has a perimeter of 30 cm and its length is twice its width; find its area. **Answers:** (1) 75 g per person, so 750 g; (2) (x &minus; 4)² &minus; 13, turning point (4, &minus;13); (3) 1 m³ = 100 × 100 × 100 = 1 000 000 cm³, and 0.4 × 1 000 000 = 400 000; (4) width 5 cm, length 10 cm, area 50 cm².

## How do you revise for Edexcel GCSE Maths 1MA1?

A weekly routine we use with 1MA1 students from Year 10, written around how Pearson marks. Steps are ours; the papers are Pearson's.

1. **One timed section a week.** Twenty questions from a Pearson past paper at the right tier, under time, rather than whole papers too early.
2. **Mark with the scheme, letter by letter.** Student and teacher go through the M, P, A and B marks together, so the student sees where each mark came from.
3. **Tick sheet for working.** Our own checklist: every step written, units on the answer, rounding stated, a final sentence where the question asks why.
4. **One problem-solving question, three routes.** Each week, one P-mark question solved in more than one valid way, so the student stops looking for "the" method.
5. **Non-calculator fluency, ten minutes a day.** Fractions, percentages and written multiplication for Paper 1, which comes first in both series.
6. **Full papers from spring.** From March of Year 11, one full paper a week, alternating calculator and non-calculator, ready for 14 May 2027.

## What students and parents ask about Edexcel GCSE Maths

### What is the Edexcel GCSE Maths specification code?

It is 1MA1, for Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Mathematics. The papers are coded 1MA1/1F to 1MA1/3F at Foundation and 1MA1/1H to 1MA1/3H at Higher.

### How many marks is each Edexcel GCSE Maths paper?

Each of the three papers is worth 80 marks and lasts 1 hour 30 minutes, at both tiers. The papers are equally weighted, and the grade comes from the total across all three.

### When are the Edexcel GCSE Maths exams in 2027?

Pearson's final summer 2027 timetable lists Paper 1 on Friday 14 May, Paper 2 on Thursday 27 May and Paper 3 on Monday 14 June 2027, all in the morning. The November 2026 papers are on 4, 6 and 9 November.

### What does grade 3 allowed mean on Higher tier?

Higher tier awards grades 4 to 9, but a student who narrowly misses grade 4 can be awarded a 3 instead of a U. Below that, the result is unclassified.

### What is a P mark in Edexcel maths?

A process mark, used on problem-solving questions. Pearson says it works like a method mark: any valid route is credited, and a partly correct solution earns the process marks it reaches.

### Do I lose marks for not showing working?

Often you lose the chance of part marks, and where a question tells you to show working, Pearson's guidance says a correct answer with no working scores no marks. Writing each step is the safest habit.

### Do I need calculus for Edexcel GCSE Maths?

No. Pearson's guidance says no calculus is expected for turning points; completing the square or symmetry is enough. A correct calculus method would still be credited.

### Is there a formulae sheet in the Edexcel GCSE Maths exams?

Yes. Ofqual requires a formulae sheet in GCSE Maths exams in 2025, 2026 and 2027, including the November series, and Pearson provides a clean copy with each paper.

### Are you part of Pearson or Edexcel?

No. We are an independent online school with no connection to Pearson. We teach to the published 1MA1 specification, use Pearson's public past papers, and do not promise grades.
