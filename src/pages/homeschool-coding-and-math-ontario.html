Homeschool coding and math in Ontario

Ontario sets out how home schooling works in one document: Policy/Program Memorandum 131, issued on June 17, 2002 and still in effect. It is short, and much of what parents worry about is answered in it plainly. This page quotes PPM 131 on the points that matter for a family planning coding and math at home, then shows a weekly plan, a project and a portfolio that fit what the memorandum describes. We teach coding and math alongside the family's own plan; we do not grant Ontario credits, and nothing here is legal advice, so check the memorandum itself and your school board before you act.

In short

Families who home school in Ontario follow Policy/Program Memorandum 131: they notify their school board in writing each year, and they may use the Ontario curriculum or a different one. The official document

What Ontario's PPM 131 says about home schooling

Every line in this table is quoted from Policy/Program Memorandum 131 on ontario.ca, which we read on 22 October 2026 (the page shows "Updated: December 18, 2024"). The memorandum is addressed to school boards, so it describes what boards should do as well as what parents should do.

Policy/Program Memorandum 131, Home Schooling, as published on ontario.ca (read 22 October 2026)
TopicWhat PPM 131 says
The document"Date of Issue: June 17, 2002". "Subject: Home Schooling".
Telling the board"Parents who decide to provide home schooling for their child(ren) should notify the school board of their intent in writing."
Every yearParents who continue "should give notification each year in writing prior to September 1 to the school board in whose jurisdiction their child last attended school."

Free curriculum documentsHome-schooling parents "may download curriculum policy documents and curriculum support material produced by the Ministry of Education free of charge" from the ministry's website.

What this means for coding and math

PPM 131 does not list required subjects, but numeracy is named directly in what a board would look for. A family that can show a plan for math, examples of work, and how progress is checked is describing exactly the three things the memorandum names.

Coding is not mentioned as a subject. It does appear indirectly: in the questions a board official may ask about materials, the memorandum's examples include "computer programs".

What this page is not

This is a reading of the memorandum for families, not legal advice. PPM 131 is the official source; your school board applies it; and the Education Act sits behind both. Check them before you notify, withdraw or change anything.

Modern Age Coders is not connected with the Ontario Ministry of Education or any school board. We teach coding and math; we do not register, supervise or report on home schooling.

Writing your plan

The three things PPM 131 names, answered for coding and math

If a board ever asks, PPM 131 says determining whether instruction is satisfactory "should therefore focus on" a plan, literacy and numeracy, and assessment. Here is how a coding and math plan can speak to each one. The right-hand columns are our suggestions, not the ministry's.

PPM 131's three focus points and a coding and math answer to each (the last two columns are ours)
PPM 131 focusA math answerA coding answer
"a plan for educating the child"A one-page outline for the year: number, fractions and ratio, algebra, geometry, data, in the order you will teach themA sequence from block coding or a first Python program to small projects of the child's own
"plans to ensure literacy and numeracy at developmentally appropriate levels"Two live math classes a week at the child's level, plus short daily practice; a placement check at the startPrograms that use the math being learned: a pattern rule as a loop, a mean from a list of numbers
"plans for assessing the child's achievement"A monthly talk with the teacher about what is secure, a dated problem set each month, and a short end-of-term reviewA folder of finished programs with one sentence each on what the child learned, dated

Following the Ontario curriculum, or not

One of the questions PPM 131 suggests for a board official is: "Is your instructional program based on the Ontario curriculum or on a different curriculum?" Either answer is acceptable in the memorandum's terms; a family simply says which documents it uses.

Families who choose the Ontario curriculum often like its math, because it builds coding in from Grade 1, where students write code that involves "sequential events", up to Grade 7, which adds a "subprogram".

Keeping it light

A plan does not need to look like a school timetable. PPM 131 itself says the methods, materials and schedule may differ from those of the school system.

What makes a plan convincing is that it is specific and that the work matches it. A short plan you actually follow is worth more than a thick binder.

A project from our classes

Project: Feeder Watch, two weeks of bird counts analysed in Python

We wrote this project for home-schooled children in Grades 7 and 8. It brings together data, averages and a few lines of code, and it ends with a decision, which echoes Ontario's Grade 8 math expectation for code that involves "the analysis of data in order to inform and communicate decisions".

  1. Collect. For fourteen days, at the same time each morning, count the birds at a backyard or balcony feeder for ten minutes and write the number down.
  2. Predict. Halfway through, the feeder moves closer to a window. Before analysing anything, the child writes down a prediction: will week 2 be busier?
  3. Analyse. Type the fourteen numbers into the program below and run it.
  4. Decide. Compare the two weekly means and say, in a sentence, whether moving the feeder made a difference.
  5. Communicate. Present the result to a parent in two minutes, with one number that supports the conclusion.

What the numbers say

Week 2 includes the two busiest days, 21 and 22 birds, so it feels busier. But the weekly means are 13.3 and 13.1, almost the same, because week 2 also has the quietest day, with 6.

The honest conclusion is that two weeks of counts show no clear difference. That is a better answer than the prediction, and it is real data analysis.

What goes in the portfolio

The tally sheet with dates, the prediction, the program, its output, and the child's one-paragraph conclusion. Together they show a plan, numeracy and assessment in one project.

Extensions: add a median, draw a bar chart by hand, or collect a third week to see whether the pattern holds.

Tests and contests

EQAO and Waterloo contests for home-schooled students in Ontario

Home schooling does not shut a child out of Ontario's provincial assessments or the University of Waterloo's math and computing contests, but both run through schools, so they need planning early in the school year.

High school at home

Homeschooling Ontario high school: coding, math and the diploma

Teenagers who home school often ask how their work connects to an Ontario Secondary School Diploma (OSSD). The official sources answer part of that; your school board and the Independent Learning Centre answer the rest. We do not grant Ontario credits.

High school facts from ontario.ca and PPM 131 (read 22 October 2026)
TopicWhat the official source says
The diploma depends on start yearOntario's graduation page: "What you need to earn an Ontario Secondary School Diploma depends on the year you started Grade 9."
Credits for Grade 9 in 2024 or laterThe same page lists 17 compulsory credits and 13 optional credits, plus other requirements.

Coding and math courses a teen might study

Ontario's computer studies courses include ICD2O in Grade 10, where students "use fundamental programming concepts to write simple programs", and ICS3U and ICS3C in Grade 11, both of which list no prerequisite.

In math, Grade 9 (MTH1W) says students "will use mathematical processes, mathematical modelling, and coding". Senior university-bound math leads to Advanced Functions (MHF4U) and Calculus and Vectors (MCV4U).

How we fit in

We teach the content of these subjects live, so a teen learns the programming and the math properly. We do not grant Ontario credits, and our classes do not replace a credit course from a school or the Independent Learning Centre.

A teen who later needs a credit can take it through a recognised provider; what they learned with us is then real preparation rather than a substitute.

Sources: Ontario, high school graduation requirements, the Ontario curriculum course descriptions, and PPM 131, read 22 October 2026. Families should check each source for their own start year and situation.

Class times

Class times for Ontario families

Home schooling frees the timetable, which makes daytime and weekend classes easy to arrange.

Most of Ontario is on Eastern time: UTC-5 after the clocks go back on Sunday 1 November 2026, and UTC-4 after they go forward on Sunday 14 March 2027. Northwestern Ontario west of the 90th meridian is filed under Winnipeg's zone in the tz database, which records UTC-5 all year from 1 November 2026, so families around Kenora or Dryden should tell us the clock they actually keep and we will match it. We state every class time in your own zone. Many home-schooling families choose a weekday slot, since school hours do not get in the way.

For parents

Stage 2 is the one to watch: a child who can explain code is learning, not copying. It also produces exactly the kind of evidence a portfolio needs.

Children often move faster at home than in a classroom because nobody waits for the slowest or the fastest.

Questions from Ontario home-schoolers

Homeschool coding and math in Ontario: common questions

Do I have to tell the school board that we home school in Ontario?

PPM 131 says parents who decide to home school should notify the school board of their intent in writing, and should give notification each year in writing before September 1. Check the memorandum and your board for the details.

Do I have to follow the Ontario curriculum when homeschooling?

PPM 131 recognises that a parent may not be following the Ontario curriculum, and one of its suggested questions simply asks which curriculum documents a family uses. Many families use the Ontario curriculum for math because it is free to download.

What does a board look at if it asks about our instruction?

PPM 131 says the focus should be a plan for educating the child, plans for literacy and numeracy at developmentally appropriate levels, and plans for assessing achievement. It also says that normally the board should not investigate.

Do you grant credits for homeschooled high school students in Ontario?

No. We do not grant Ontario credits. We teach coding and math content alongside the family's plan. PPM 131 describes the Independent Learning Centre as one route to courses for home-schooled students; check with the ILC and your board.

Can my home-schooled child write EQAO?

PPM 131 says home-schooling parents may wish their children to take part in the Grade 3, 6 and 9 assessments or the literacy test, and must contact the school board by September 30, or another date the board sets.

Can a homeschooled student write Waterloo math or computing contests?

Yes, through a school. CEMC says all students must participate through their school, and suggests a student whose own school is not participating look for another local school that will let them join.

How much coding and math does a home-schooled child need each week?

There is no official number. Our sample plan uses two live hours a week plus short daily practice, about four hours in total for a child of Grade 6 or 7.

What should go in a coding and math portfolio?

Dated work: a monthly problem set, finished programs with a sentence on what each shows, and one larger project such as our Feeder Watch data analysis.

When are classes for Ontario home-schooling families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Daytime weekday slots are easy for home-schooling families.

Is this page legal advice?

No. It quotes PPM 131 and other official pages so families can find the source quickly. Always check the memorandum, the Education Act and your school board before making decisions.
