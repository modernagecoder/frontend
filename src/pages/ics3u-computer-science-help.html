In short

ICS3U is Ontario’s Grade 11 university-preparation computer science course, Introduction to Computer Science, with no prerequisite. Students write programs with variables, arrays, loops and subprograms, then design software as a team. The Ontario curriculum

What ICS3U covers, strand by strand

Every ICS3U class in Ontario is built on one document: The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 10 to 12: Computer Studies, issued in 2008. It describes ICS3U plainly: “This course introduces students to computer science.” It also says students “will also write and use subprograms within computer programs”, and its course entry ends with “Prerequisite: None”. The course is organized in four strands.

The details students meet most

Strand A is where the marks are won and lost, and its specific expectations are precise. Students describe arrays in terms of “elements, indexes, and bounds”, and they write algorithms to “count elements in an array, calculate a total, find highest or lowest value, or perform a linear search”. Those four algorithms turn up again and again in tests, so we make students rebuild each one from a blank file.

The same strand asks students to “identify and correct syntax, logic, and run-time errors”, to “use a tracing technique to understand program flow” and to “validate a program using a full range of test cases”. Tracing and testing are written into the course, not optional extras. It also covers how a computer stores data internally, naming “binary, hexadecimal, ASCII, Unicode”.

Problem solving is in the curriculum by name

Strand B lists the strategies students should use: “stepwise refinement, divide and conquer, working backwards, examples, extreme cases, tables and charts, trial and error”. The worked problem further down uses three of them on one page: stepwise refinement, a table, and extreme cases.

Strand B also names example tasks, including how to “add data to a sorted array”, the “determination of primes”, and algorithms that handle “division by zero, roots of negatives”. Question 9 in our practice set is a prime checker for exactly this reason.

Why every school’s course outline looks different

Families searching for an ICS3U course outline often find that two schools teach the course in a different order, or in different languages. The 2008 document explains why: “The computer programming language used and the order in which concepts are taught is left to the teacher’s professional judgement.” It adds that some teachers choose an “objects-first” approach.

So the outline your teen’s teacher hands out is the real map. In our first class we ask for it, and we plan around its unit order rather than our own.

How the final mark is built

The 2008 curriculum document sets the rule for Grades 9 to 12: “Seventy per cent of the grade will be based on evaluations conducted throughout the course.” The other part is a final evaluation: “Thirty per cent of the grade will be based on a final evaluation in the form of an examination, performance, essay, and/or other method of evaluation suitable to the course content and administered towards the end of the course.”

In ICS3U that 30 per cent is usually a final exam, a final project (often called the culminating task), or both. How a school splits it is the school’s choice, so ask the teacher early. The document also says each course “is worth one credit”, granted for a grade of “50% or higher”.

Choosing between U and C

The choice is about destination, not ability alone. A student aiming at a university program in computer science, engineering or mathematics usually takes the U course, because only ICS3U leads to ICS4U. A student heading for a college program in programming or IT has a real course in ICS3C, with its own Grade 12 follow-on.

Neither course has a prerequisite, so a Grade 11 student who has never written code can enrol in either. That is also why the first month of ICS3U can feel steep: some classmates arrive having coded for years, and others are starting from zero.

What the Grade 10 change means for a family

The current website describes ICD2O this way: “Students will learn and apply coding concepts and skills to build hands-on projects and investigate artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, and other emerging digital technologies.” ICS2O appears in the 2008 document and not on that list.

Neither source we read says one course replaced the other, so we will not say it either. What matters for ICS3U is simpler: the 2008 table lists no prerequisite for it, so no Grade 10 course is required to take it. A Grade 10 course with programming in it is still a gentle start.

Python or Java

If the school teaches Python

Python lets students write a working program in the first lesson, which is good for confidence and risky for habits. Because a variable can hold anything, a mistake such as adding a string to a number only shows up when that line runs, sometimes in the middle of a test.

Our Python students learn to read a traceback from the bottom up, to name variables clearly, and to stop leaning on shortcuts the course will not accept, such as built-in max() when the task is to write the highest-value algorithm themselves.

If the school teaches Java

Java asks for more before anything works: a class, a main method, declared types, braces and semicolons. Students often spend the first weeks fighting the compiler. That strictness pays off later, because the compiler catches whole families of mistakes before the program ever runs.

Our Java students learn to read a compiler message for its line number and its one useful word, to keep integer division in mind, and to compare strings with .equals(). If cannot find symbol is the current enemy, our page on the cannot find symbol error walks through its usual causes.

The curriculum asks students to “explain the difference between an interpreter and a compiler”, and the first row of the table above is that answer in practice. A student who switches language between ICS3U and ICS4U keeps every idea and relearns only the spelling. For the longer argument about which language suits a beginner, see Python vs Java: which to learn first.

A worked problem, ours

One ICS3U problem, solved in Python and Java

This is our own problem, written for this page, at the level of an ICS3U unit test on arrays and loops. The code matters less than the thinking around it: break the problem down, trace it by hand, then test the edges. Both versions below were run and print the same line.

The problem. A student logs the minutes they practise coding each day. Given the daily log and a goal, find the longest run of consecutive days on which they practised at least the goal, and the day that run started. Days are numbered from 1. If no day meets the goal, report 0 and 0. If two runs tie, report the earlier one.

  1. Restate the output. Two numbers: a length and a start day. Writing this down first stops the classic half-answer that reports the length and forgets the start.
  2. Decide what to remember while walking the array once. The current run (how long, and where it began) and the record so far (how long, and where it began). Four variables, each with one job.
  3. Write the rule for one day. If the day meets the goal, the current run grows by one, and if it was 0 it begins today. If the day misses the goal, the current run drops to 0.
  4. Decide when to update the record. Every time the current run grows, compare it with the record. Updating only when a run breaks misses a run that lasts to the final day, which is the bug in practice question 7.
  5. Decide the tie rule in code. Use strictly greater than. An equal run never replaces the record, so the earlier run wins, as the problem asks.
  6. List the extreme cases before running anything. An empty log, a log where no day qualifies, a single day exactly at the goal, a run that ends on the last day, and two runs of equal length.

What the trace table shows

Look at the last three rows. Days 8, 9 and 10 make a run of 3, the same length as the run starting on day 4, and the record does not move. Without the trace, a student who used greater-than-or-equal would get day 8 and never see why.

The curriculum asks for “manual code tracing, extra code to output the state of variables” as debugging methods. This table is both: we wrote it by hand first, then printed the same eight columns from the program to check it.

Why this is worth an hour

The test plan follows the steps the curriculum spells out for a test plan, including to “calculate expected outcomes, record actual outcomes”. Students who write the expected column before running the code catch their own mistakes; students who run first tend to believe whatever the program prints.

Every later ICS3U topic reuses this shape: walk an array once, keep a small amount of state, update it carefully, test the edges. Our students solve versions of it for marks, temperatures and game scores until the shape is automatic.

Practice, ours

Ten ICS3U practice questions

These ten questions are our own, written for this page. They are not taken from any school, board or exam. Questions 1 to 4 are tracing, the next three are debugging, then two ask you to write a method and the last asks you to choose a data structure. Work each one on paper before you check the answers below.

Question 8 · write a method. In Python or Java, write count_above(values, limit) (Java: countAbove) that returns how many values are strictly greater than limit. What does it return for the values 55, 72, 90, 72 and 40 with a limit of 72?

Question 9 · write a method. Write is_prime(n) (Java: isPrime) that returns true only for prime numbers. List the test values you would use and why.

Question 10 · choose a data structure. For each task, say what you would store the data in and why. (a) The 30 marks of one class, to find the average and count how many are above it. (b) How many times each word appears in a paragraph. (c) Each student’s name with their mark, to be printed from highest mark to lowest.

Answers, ours

Full answers to the ten questions

Each answer says what the program does and why, because the reasoning is what earns marks. Every program was run to confirm the output.

  1. 16. range(1, 8, 2) starts at 1, steps by 2 and stops before 8, so k takes 1, 3, 5 and 7, and the total is 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16. The stop value is never included: the habit to check whenever range appears.
  2. 3 1 3.5. With two ints, x / y is integer division, so 7 / 2 is 3. x % y is the remainder, 1. The cast turns x into 7.0 before the division, so the third part is 3.5. The cast applies to x only, which is why it has to come first.
  3. 42 15 23. len(nums) - 1 is 5, the last index. nums[2] is the third element, because indexes start at 0. nums[-2] counts from the end and is 23. Java has no negative indexes; there you would write nums[nums.length - 2].
  4. 9. The trace of n is 12, 6, 3, 10, 5, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1, with steps counting each change, so steps ends at 9. Tracing a while loop is about the condition: the loop body runs only while n is not 1, so the final 1 is never processed.
  5. A run-time error. With <=, i reaches 3, but the last index of a three-element array is 2, so Java throws ArrayIndexOutOfBoundsException (Index 3 out of bounds for length 3). Change <= to <. Fixed, it prints 221.
  6. It prints 0; it should print -3. This is a logic error: the program runs without complaint and gives a wrong answer. Starting top at 0 assumes every value is positive, and none of these are. Start with the first element instead: top = temps[0], and decide separately what an empty list should do (return None, or refuse it).
  7. It prints 0; it should print 3. The record is only updated when a run breaks, and this run never breaks: it lasts to the last day. Either add the same comparison once more after the loop (if run is greater than record, copy it across), or update the record every time the run grows, as the worked problem does. Testing a run that ends on the last day is the extreme case that finds it.
  8. It returns 1. Only 90 is strictly greater than 72; both 72s are equal, so they do not count. The test that matters is a value equal to the limit, because that is where greater-than and greater-than-or-equal disagree. Both versions are in the code below.
  9. See the code below. Numbers below 2 are not prime. For anything else, try divisors from 2 while the divisor squared is at most n: if n had a factor larger than its square root, its partner factor would be smaller and already tried. Test with -3, 0, 1 (not prime), 2 (the smallest prime and the only even one), 9 (a square, the edge of the loop), 29 (prime) and a large prime. Our run listed 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23 and 29 as the primes below 30.
  10. (a) A one-dimensional array or list of 30 numbers: one pass to total them, a second pass to count those above the average. (b) At ICS3U level, two parallel arrays, one of words and one of counts, with a linear search for each word; a Python dictionary or a Java HashMap does the same job faster and is worth learning next. (c) Parallel arrays of names and marks work if every swap during sorting moves both arrays together; in ICS4U the cleaner answer is one array of student objects, which the Grade 12 curriculum describes as arrays of “compound data types”.

An honest answer

Is ICS3U hard?

It depends less on talent than on four specific skills, and all four can be taught. Here is what our Grade 11 students actually find hard. Nobody can honestly promise a mark in advance, and we do not.

Loops and indexes

Where a loop starts, where it stops and which index is the last one. Python’s range stops before its end value; a Java array of length 3 ends at index 2. In our classes, most marks lost on arrays come from being one position out.

Debugging without guessing

Faced with an error, many students change things at random. The curriculum asks them to interpret error messages and trace programs, and that is a habit: read the message, find the line, predict, then test.

Planning before typing

The course expects pseudocode, flow charts and a test plan. Students who type first get through short programs, then hit a wall when the project arrives.

The project near the end

Strand B asks students to “apply a software development life-cycle model to a software development project”, and names the phases as “problem definition, analysis, design, writing code, testing, implementation, maintenance”. In most schools that becomes a larger final project, often called the culminating task, worked on over several weeks.

What goes wrong is rarely the code. It is a project chosen too big, started too late, with no working version saved along the way. We help students cut a project to a size they can finish, then add features only once the core works.

Who finds it easier, and who finds it harder

Students who have coded before often coast for a month, then meet arrays and subprograms with the same habits that worked for ten-line programs. Students who have never coded find the first weeks steep but often build cleaner habits, because they learn tracing from the start.

A student comfortable with Grade 10 math usually finds the logic manageable. A better warning sign than any math mark is a student who cannot explain, line by line, a program they wrote last week.

Exam review

An ICS3U final exam review plan

A plan of ours for the last month of the course, written for a final evaluation that is part exam and part project. Adjust it once the teacher confirms what the final evaluation actually contains.

  1. Find out what the final evaluation is. Ask whether there is a written exam, a coding exam on the computer, a project, or a mix, and how much each part is worth. The curriculum allows an examination, performance, essay or other method.
  2. Audit the course outline. List every unit from the school’s own outline and mark each one solid, shaky or lost. Start revision with the shaky ones, not the lost ones: they move fastest.
  3. Rebuild the vocabulary. Data type, array, index, bound, parameter, return value, local and global scope, syntax, logic and run-time error. Write a one-line definition and a two-line example for each.
  4. Trace by hand every day. Three short programs a day, on paper, with a trace table, before running them. Written questions often ask students to read code, and tracing is how you read it.
  5. Rewrite the classic algorithms from a blank file. Total, highest or lowest, count, linear search, then inserting into a sorted array. No notes, no copying, then compare with your class versions.
  6. Practise the three kinds of error. Take a working program, plant one syntax, one logic and one run-time error, and swap with a classmate. Naming the kind of error is often its own exam question.
  7. Write one complete test plan. For one of your programs: normal values, boundary values and invalid input, with expected results written before you run it.
  8. Finish the project before polishing it. Keep a saved working version at every milestone, write the documentation as you go, and stop adding features a week before it is due.
  9. Sit one timed practice under exam conditions. Use your own old tests and our ten questions above, at the real length, with no computer for the written part.
  10. Use the last day for review only. Reread your error log and trace tables. No new topics the night before.

Why ICS3U still matters

A first-year computer science course moves quickly. Students who arrive able to trace code, test the edges and plan a program spend their first term on new ideas rather than on the habits ICS3U teaches.

Contests are the other bridge. The Canadian Computing Competition is run by the University of Waterloo’s CEMC, which says “all students must participate through their school.” Its Junior level starts with questions CEMC describes as “basic loops and conditions”, very like ICS3U. Online, summer school and the credit

ICS3U online and in summer school: help with the course, not the credit

Many searches for ICS3U online or ICS3U summer school come from families who want the credit itself. That part we do not offer, and it is better to say so clearly.

Where the credit comes from

An ICS3U credit is granted by the school that teaches and evaluates the course: the student’s own school, a board’s summer or night school, or an Ontario credit provider. Modern Age Coders is none of these. We teach alongside the student’s school course and do not grant Ontario credits.

For the diploma, computer studies counts in the compulsory-credit choice groups on Ontario’s graduation requirements page: in Group 3 for students who started Grade 9 in 2023 or earlier, and in the STEM-related course group for later starters (page updated September 02, 2026).

Where we fit

Families come to us when a student is taking ICS3U at school, online or in summer school and wants a second teacher: someone to trace their code with them, explain an error, review the plan for the project or run a practice exam. We work from the student’s own assignments and outline.

Some families start the summer before Grade 11, so that the first unit of ICS3U is a second look rather than a first. A summer course with a credit attached moves fast, and the same groundwork helps there too.

For parents

ICS3U: common questions

What is ICS3U?

ICS3U is Introduction to Computer Science, Grade 11, University Preparation, an Ontario course described in the 2008 Computer Studies curriculum for Grades 10 to 12. It covers programming with data types, one-dimensional arrays, control structures and subprograms, software development in teams, computer systems, and topics in computer science research and careers.

Do you grant the ICS3U credit?

No. We teach alongside the student's school course and do not grant Ontario credits. Families who need the ICS3U credit take the course at school, at a board's summer or night school, or through an Ontario credit provider, and use our classes for the teaching and practice around it.

Is ICS3U hard?

It is manageable for most students who keep up, but four things catch people: loop bounds and array indexes, debugging without guessing, planning before typing, and the larger project near the end. All four are skills that improve with practice. Nobody can honestly promise a mark in advance.

Does ICS3U use Python or Java?

Either, depending on the school. The 2008 curriculum says the programming language and the order of topics are left to the teacher's professional judgement. We teach in whichever language your teen's class uses.

What is the prerequisite for ICS3U?

None. The 2008 curriculum lists no prerequisite for ICS3U, and the current Ontario curriculum website shows none either. ICS3U is itself the prerequisite for ICS4U, the Grade 12 university preparation course.

What is the difference between ICS3U and ICS3C?

ICS3U is the university preparation course, Introduction to Computer Science, and leads to ICS4U. ICS3C is the college preparation course, Introduction to Computer Programming, which focuses on programming skills and leads to ICS4C. Neither has a prerequisite and each is worth one credit.

How is the ICS3U final mark worked out?

The 2008 curriculum sets 70 per cent of the grade from evaluations during the course and 30 per cent from a final evaluation near the end, such as an examination, a performance or another suitable method. Each school decides the exact form, so ask the teacher what the final evaluation contains.

Do I need ICS3U for computer science at Waterloo or U of T?

On 22 October 2026, Waterloo listed Grade 11 U Introduction to Computer Science as a recommended course for Computer Science, with Advanced Functions and Calculus and Vectors among the required courses. The University of Toronto pages listed English and math courses for all three campuses and named no computer science course among the prerequisites.

What happened to ICS2O?

ICS2O, Introduction to Computer Studies, is described in the 2008 curriculum document. The current Ontario curriculum website lists ICD2O, Digital Technology and Innovations in the Changing World (2023), as its Grade 10 computer studies course and does not list ICS2O. Neither source says one replaced the other.

How should my teen study for the ICS3U final exam?

Start four weeks out: confirm what the final evaluation contains, audit the school course outline, trace short programs by hand every day, rewrite the classic array algorithms from a blank file, practise naming syntax, logic and run-time errors, and finish the project before polishing it.

What times are classes for Ontario families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Times are agreed in Eastern time for most of Ontario; the clocks change on 1 November 2026 and 14 March 2027, so confirm the class time again in those weeks.

How much does ICS3U help cost?

The first class is free. After that, families pay a monthly fee and can stop at the end of any month. The fees section on this page shows the current figure for a batch and for one to one.
