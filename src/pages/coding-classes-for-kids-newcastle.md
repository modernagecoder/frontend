Ask a Year 4 child what 3 plus 6 is and you will get 9 before you finish the question. Ask a computer, and sometimes the answer is 36. That is not a broken computer. It is what happens when numbers are stored as text, and Newcastle's park benches give us a perfect example. OpenStreetMap records 191 benches across central and coastal Newcastle in the rectangle we chose, and two of them note how many people they seat: 3 and 6. Add them the wrong way and the total is 36. Our young Newcastle coders find out why, and learn the one small fix that makes it 9.

## Why does the computer say 3 plus 6 is 36?

Two Newcastle park benches, the seat counts written on them in OpenStreetMap, and what happens when a program forgets those counts are stored as text.

**What we found and ran on 7 October 2026 (our calculation from OpenStreetMap)**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Benches mapped in our Newcastle rectangle | 191 |
| Benches that record how many people they seat | 2, seating 3 and 6 |
| Adding the two values exactly as stored | "3" + "6" gives "36" |
| Adding them after converting to whole numbers | 3 + 6 gives 9 |
| Sorting "3", "6" and "240" as text | "240", "3", "6" |
| The largest of the three, compared as text | "6" |

### 1. Everything arrives as text

OpenStreetMap stores every value as text, even ones that look like numbers. To a program, the seat count "3" is the character 3, no different in kind from the letter a.

### 2. Plus means two things

For numbers, plus adds. For text, plus joins one piece onto the end of another. Joining "3" and "6" makes "36", which is exactly what the program was asked to do.

### 3. One word fixes it

Wrapping each value in int() turns the text into a whole number first. Now 3 plus 6 is 9, and the program agrees with every Year 4 child in Newcastle.

### Why "240" sorts before "3"

One of the benches also records the direction it faces, 240. Put "3", "6" and "240" in order as text and you get "240", "3", "6", because text is ordered by its first character and 2 comes before 3. Ask for the largest and text comparison answers "6". Convert to numbers first and the order becomes 3, 6, 240, with 240 the largest, as any child would expect.

These results are from our own Python run on an OpenStreetMap snapshot taken on 7 October 2026. OpenStreetMap is edited by volunteers; benches may be missing or have changed, and few record their seat count at all. The rectangle is our own choice, not a council boundary, and we make no claim about how many benches Newcastle has.

## Words that look like numbers, from Kindergarten to Year 8

The difference between a number and the symbol for a number is a big idea, so we bring it in gently and early.

**Text and numbers at each stage**

| Stage | Activity | Understanding built |
|---|---|---|
| Kindergarten to Year 2 | Joins number cards side by side to make bigger numbers, then adds them with counters | A digit is a symbol; putting symbols together is not the same as adding |
| Years 3 to 6 | Builds a Scratch quiz where typed answers are checked, and spots when 10 and 010 are treated differently | Input, comparison and converting what a player types |
| Years 7 and 8 | Reads the Newcastle bench tags in Python, adds, sorts and finds the largest, before and after int() | Data types, conversion and testing results against common sense |

### Check with common sense

A total of 36 seats on two benches should make anyone suspicious. Children learn that a quick reasonableness check catches many bugs before any fancy testing.

### Not everything converts

What if a tag said "three" or "3 to 4"? int() would stop with an error. Older children write code that reports such values instead of crashing.

### Same idea in spreadsheets

Numbers stored as text cause the same trouble in spreadsheets at school. Once children understand it in code, they recognise it everywhere.

## If an AI tool adds the numbers, does a Newcastle child need to understand data types?

Yes, because the tool adds whatever it is given, and a seat count of 36 looks just as tidy on screen as a seat count of 9.

AI tools and spreadsheets both work with data as it arrives. If the numbers came in as text, a quickly generated program may join them instead of adding them, or sort 240 before 3, and the output looks perfectly confident. A child who has met "3" plus "6" makes "36" will glance at the result and know something is off.

We teach young Newcastle coders to expect the computer to do exactly what it was told, no more, and to check that what it was told is what they meant. AI tools appear in our lessons only in ways suited to the child's age, with a teacher present throughout.

So, is coding still worth learning in 2026? For a child who wants to understand the answers a computer gives, more than ever. A Newcastle child who has fixed 36 into 9 already knows why. The longer argument is in [is learning to code still worth it](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).

Live classes start at age 6, normally in Kindergarten or Year 1. Young children begin with blocks and pictures before they type.

### What does it mean when a number is stored as text?

It means the computer is holding the characters that look like a number rather than the number itself. Text can be joined and sorted letter by letter, but not added or compared by size until it is converted, for example with int() in Python.

### What is the Newcastle bench project?

Children take the 191 benches OpenStreetMap records in a rectangle over central and coastal Newcastle, find the two that list seat counts, 3 and 6, and see that adding them as stored gives "36" until they convert the values to numbers and get 9.

Not at all. Younger children explore the same idea with number cards: putting a 3 next to a 6 makes 36, while adding three counters to six makes nine. Code comes later; the idea starts early.
