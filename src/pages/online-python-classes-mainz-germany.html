What "live online" means

What happens in a live online Python class, minute by minute?

An hour, on video, with a shared screen. Here is a typical one for a teenage group, step by step.

  1. Minutes 0 to 5: a warm-up question. The mentor puts three lines of code on screen and asks what they print. Learners answer before anyone runs it, because predicting is half of programming.
  2. Minutes 5 to 20: one new idea. The mentor writes a short program live, talking through every line, and makes a deliberate mistake so the group sees a real error message and how to read it.
  3. Minutes 20 to 45: the learners type. Each learner writes their own version in their own editor and shares the screen when stuck. The mentor reads the code aloud with them, points to the line, and asks a question rather than giving the fix.
  4. Minutes 45 to 55: show and explain. Two or three learners run their program for the group and explain one choice they made.
  5. Minutes 55 to 60: the next step. A short task for the days between classes, small enough to finish, with the mentor available on the class chat.

A one-to-one class follows the same shape, but the middle part is longer and the mentor can move as fast or as slowly as the learner needs. Children's classes break the hour into shorter pieces with more drawing and games, because a nine year old concentrates in bursts.

What you need

A laptop or desktop computer with a keyboard, a stable internet connection and a headset or quiet room. Python and a simple editor are installed together in the first class; tablets are fine for watching but not for typing code.

What you do not need

No previous programming, no special maths, no paid software. Python is free, and every tool we use in class runs on Windows, macOS and Linux.

Why live beats video

A recorded lesson cannot see your screen. Most beginners stop not because Python is hard but because one small error blocks them for an evening. A mentor clears that block in a minute and teaches the learner how to clear the next one.

Taught in English

Are the online Python classes taught in German?

No. Every class is in English, and we would rather you knew that before you book.

Why English suits Python

Python's keywords are English words: if, for, while, return, import. Its error messages are in English, and so is the official documentation. A learner in Mainz who studies Python in English never has to translate between the classroom and the tool.

Our mentors are based in India and teach only in English. We do not have German-speaking teachers and do not pretend to.

Your pace, your accent

Mentors speak clearly and not too fast, rephrase when an explanation does not land, and get used to each learner's accent quickly. Learners may answer in simple English; nobody is marked down for grammar in a programming class.

Because the code is on the shared screen the whole time, a missed word rarely means a missed idea.

A real Mainz project

How fast did the Rhine rise at Mainz? A sliding-window project

The Rhine gauge at Mainz reports the water level every fifteen minutes. We saved a month of readings and asked a question that sounds simple and is not.

The data

PEGELONLINE, the gauge service of the Wasserstraßen- und Schifffahrtsverwaltung des Bundes, publishes the level at gauge MAINZ (Rhine kilometre 498.27) as open data under the Datenlizenz Deutschland Zero 2.0, which allows commercial and non-commercial reuse. We requested the last 31 days on 3 November 2026 and saved the answer as rhein_mainz.json: 2,975 readings, each a timestamp and a level in centimetres.

The level is measured from the gauge zero, which PEGELONLINE gives as 78.373 metres above sea level (NHN). So 120 cm does not mean the river is 120 cm deep; it is a reading on a scale.

The question

How much did the Rhine rise in Mainz this month? The quick answer is to subtract the first reading from the last. Version 1 does that, and also finds the lowest and highest readings.

Then we ask a better question: what was the biggest rise within any 24 hours? That needs a window that slides along the readings, one step at a time, which is Version 2.

A rise of 23 cm in a month sounds calm. But the lowest and highest readings are 68 cm apart, and they are only three weeks apart. The first-to-last figure hides everything that happened in between: the river can climb, fall back and climb again, and subtracting two endpoints sees none of it.

In one day, from the early hours of 25 October 2026, the Rhine at Mainz rose 28 cm, more than the whole "change" in Version 1. Look closely at the clock: the window runs from 02:30 to 01:30 the next night, which looks like 23 hours. It is 24. Summer time ended that very night, the clocks went back an hour, and because each timestamp carries its offset (+02:00, then +01:00), Python measured real elapsed time. Had we stripped the offsets, the window would have been wrong by an hour.

Three ways to describe the same month of the Rhine at Mainz (our calculations)
QuestionAnswerWhat it hides
Last reading minus first23 cmEvery rise and fall in between
Highest minus lowest68 cmHow long the change took
Biggest rise within 24 hours28 cmSlower rises spread over several days

Data: WSV, PEGELONLINE, gauge MAINZ, series W (water level, raw data), readings from 3 October 2026 20:00 to 3 November 2026 18:45, downloaded 3 November 2026, licence Datenlizenz Deutschland Zero 2.0. Calculations and code are ours. Raw gauge data can be revised later; we are not connected with the WSV.

  1. Ages 9 to 12. Draw ten readings on squared paper. Mark the first and last, then find the steepest climb between any two neighbours. Are they the same?
  2. Teenagers. Change the window to 6 hours, then to 72. Print the rise for each and explain why a longer window never gives a smaller answer.
  3. Adults. Version 2 calls min() on the whole window every step. Rewrite it so it keeps track of the lowest value as the window moves, and count how much faster it runs.
  4. Everyone. Find the biggest fall within 24 hours. Then download a fresh copy from PEGELONLINE and see whether the answer has changed.

Mainz schools

Do schools in Mainz teach Python or Informatik?

Some do, many do not yet. Rhineland-Palatinate is one of six Länder where Informatik is not compulsory for every pupil, but that is scheduled to change.

Compiled from the GI Informatik-Monitor 2026/27 (the Rhineland-Palatinate entry and the national overview), which we read on 2 November 2026.

The Oberstufe in Rhineland-Palatinate is known by the name of this city: the Land ordinance for it is the "Mainzer Studienstufe". In the Abitur, the Leistungsfach Informatik is examined in writing; the GI notes that the written tasks are drafted by teachers at each school and submitted to the education ministry, and that practical parts with a computer are allowed but not required. A pupil who keeps a Grundfach from the Einführungsphase onwards can be examined in it orally.

For a Mainz pupil, that means the school course is the one that decides the grade, and we teach beside it rather than instead of it. What we add is time at the keyboard: most school Informatik is three hours a week shared between theory, written work and a class of twenty-five, while our learners write Python for most of every hour. Mainz asks

Online Python classes in Mainz: frequent questions

Are the Python classes taught in German?

No. We teach in English and nothing else; our mentors work from India and slow down, rephrase and adjust to how each learner speaks. Python itself uses English keywords and error messages, so learning it in English is a natural fit.

Are the classes live or recorded?

Live. A mentor is on the video call for the whole hour, sees the learner's screen and corrects mistakes as they happen. We do not sell recorded courses; the live mentor is the point of the class.

What computer does a learner need?

A laptop or desktop with a keyboard, a reliable internet connection and a headset. Python and the editor are free and are installed together in the first class. A tablet alone is not enough for typing code.

From what age can my child learn Python?

From about 9, in our Python and AI for Kids course. Younger children usually start with Scratch, where blocks replace typing, and move to Python when reading and typing feel comfortable.

Do Mainz schools teach Python?

Some do. The Gesellschaft für Informatik reports compulsory Informatik in grade 5 at 21 Informatik-Profil-Schulen in Rhineland-Palatinate and electives at most Gymnasien. From 2028/29 Informatik becomes compulsory at all schools in the Land, four weekly hours in total.

When are classes in Mainz time?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Late afternoons and weekend daytimes are the usual choices in Mainz.

Can adults in Mainz learn Python from zero?

Yes. The adult Python masterclass assumes no experience at all and moves from first programs to working with files, data and automation. Many adult learners bring a task from their own job to practise on.

What is the Rhine project on this page?

An original exercise of ours on a month of water levels from the federal gauge at Mainz. Learners compare the first-to-last change with the biggest rise inside any 24 hours, using a sliding window, and see why the two answers differ.

How many learners share a class?

A group batch holds fifteen to twenty people of roughly the same age. One-to-one classes give a learner a mentor to themselves for two classes a week, at agreed times.

How do I pay, and in which currency?

Monthly. The fee is displayed in euro at the European Central Bank reference rate, while the payment is taken in US dollars and converted by your bank, so the statement amount can differ a little. The first class is free with no card needed.
