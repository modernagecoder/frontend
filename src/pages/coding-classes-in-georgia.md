## The HOPE Scholarship, and why course choice is worth money

Most states treat computer science as a good idea. Georgia attached it to a cheque.

A student meeting the requirements to be a **HOPE Scholar** at graduation must earn a minimum of **four full rigor credits** from the **Academic Rigor Course List**. It is a gate, not a tiebreak, and it sits alongside the grade point average requirement rather than replacing it.

Computer science is on that list. **Computer Science Principles** and **Games, Apps and Society** both count, and Advanced Placement courses in core subjects are approved rigor courses. For a family whose child already likes computers, the thing the child wants to do is also the thing the funding formula rewards.

**Check the list yourself, every year.** The Academic Rigor Course List is maintained by the Georgia Student Finance Commission and it is revised. Do not take a course on the strength of a web page, including this one. Ask your counselor to show you the current list at **gafutures.org**, and confirm the exact course code your school will enter on the transcript.

Two quieter points. Four credits is a small number, and a student who fills all four with the least demanding qualifying options satisfies the letter while learning less. HOPE checks the count; universities read the transcript. And the grade point average has not gone anywhere, so a rigor course scraped through does less than the same course taken with the mathematics in place.

## What the state actually requires

Georgia legislated a computer science requirement in **Georgia Code 20-2-149.3** and phased it deliberately.

| Who | Obligation | From |
|---|---|---|
| All middle schools in a local system | Offer instruction in **exploratory computer science** | Per the statute, including state charter schools serving middle grades |
| At least 50 percent of a system's high schools | Offer a course in computer science | 2023 to 2024 school year |
| All high schools in a local system | Offer a course in computer science | 2024 to 2025 school year |

The statute also supplies the definition: computer science means the study of computers, **algorithmic processes, coding, and logical thinking**, including computer principles, their hardware and software designs, their implementation, and their impact on society.

**Read that as a parent.** It is a wide door, deliberately so, because a narrow one would have been unenforceable in districts without specialist staff. But two schools can both truthfully report offering computer science while running courses that share almost nothing. "Our school offers computer science" carries less information in Georgia than families assume.

So do not ask whether the school offers it. The answer is now yes almost everywhere, by law. Ask which course, whether it is on the current rigor list, what a student produces in it, and what they can take the year afterwards.

## The Georgia student data project

Atlanta is home to the **Centers for Disease Control** and Prevention, so Georgia students get the question its analysts answer daily, and the one almost every adult gets wrong.

A student picks a condition, finds its real prevalence from published CDC figures, and takes a screening test that correctly identifies almost everybody who has the condition and wrongly flags only a small percentage of those who do not.

The question a real person asks in a waiting room: my test came back positive, what is the probability I have this?

Nearly everybody answers with the accuracy of the test. The correct answer often has almost nothing to do with it.

**The trap.** Work it on a hundred thousand imaginary people rather than in symbols. If the condition is rare, true positives are few, while false positives are drawn from the enormous group who do not have it. A one percent error rate applied to ninety-nine thousand healthy people produces far more positives than a near-perfect test applied to the thousand who are ill. Most positives are wrong, and the test is still excellent.

The number that decides the answer is the one nobody was given: how common the condition is to begin with. The same test means something different in population screening than in a clinic where everybody has symptoms. The test did not change; the base rate did.

We teach it as a two by two table of counts, not a formula. A student who has filled four cells and read the answer off understands what Bayes' theorem does before ever seeing it written, and stops being impressed by an accuracy figure quoted alone.

It generalises unpleasantly: it is why a fraud alert on a rare event is mostly false alarms, why face recognition with impressive accuracy misidentifies people constantly at airport scale, and why an AI classifier flagging one document in a thousand needs a human reading its output rather than acting on it.

Students look up current prevalence figures themselves.
