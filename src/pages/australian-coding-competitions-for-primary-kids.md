# Coding competitions for primary kids

Australian primary students can take part in more coding and computational thinking competitions than most parents expect, and several need no coding at all. Some are a single sitting at school; others run over a term as a team project or a robot build. This page brings them together by year level, with every detail checked at the organiser, and links to our fuller guide for each one. It also includes a short unplugged puzzle in the style these competitions use, which children can try at the kitchen table with four cards. We are independent of every organiser named here.

## Bebras, the CAT and the coding challenge in detail

The Australian Maths Trust runs three of the most accessible options for primary students. The details below come from its 2026 pages.

**Australian Maths Trust computing competitions for primary students, 2026**

|  | Bebras | CAT | Raspberry Pi Foundation Coding Challenge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary eligibility | Middle Primary (Years 3 and 4), Upper Primary (Years 5 and 6) | Upper Primary (Years 5 and 6) | From Year 5 |
| Time | 60 minutes for Middle Primary, 45 for others | 60 minutes | 45 minutes |
| Questions | 15, mixing multiple choice, integer and interactive | Three-stage tasks on test data of growing size | 10 tasks in two sections |
| Coding needed? | No | No | Yes, in blocks or text, by chosen division |
| 2026 dates | Monday 9 February to Sunday 8 March | 19 to 21 May | 16 to 18 June |
| Also worth knowing | Teams of up to four allowed | Free CAT Coding long-form challenge | Free to enter |

Bebras is an international computational thinking challenge; the AMT says it involves over 2.9 million students from 60 countries. Bebras Australia began in 2014, was delivered by CSIRO until 2023, and has been run by the AMT since 2024. A follow-up event is held in Term 4 for international Bebras week.

The CAT, according to the AMT, uses unique three-stage tasks that encourage students to develop informal algorithms and apply them to test data of increasing size or complexity. It needs no programming experience and suits any maths class.

The Raspberry Pi Foundation Coding Challenge was previously delivered as the Coding Challenge and the Oxford University Computing Challenge. Its four divisions are chosen by skill, not age: Novice and Explorer for block-based programmers, Navigator and Trailblazer for text-based programmers.

In the coding challenge, questions 1 to 7 are worth 4 marks each and questions 8 to 10 worth 8 marks each. The AMT points to the UK Bebras website for practice challenges.

## A sorting network with four cards

Computational thinking competitions love puzzles about putting things in order with simple, fixed steps. This one is ours, and needs only four numbered cards and a pencil.

### The rule

Lay four cards face up in positions 1 to 4. A "comparator" looks at two positions and swaps the cards if the left one is bigger. Nothing else is allowed.

### The network

Compare positions 1 and 2, and 3 and 4. Then 1 and 3, and 2 and 4. Finally 2 and 3. That is five comparators in a fixed order, whatever the cards are.

### The challenge

Shuffle the cards and run the five comparators. Do they always end up in order? Try several shuffles, then try to explain why it works.

**One run of the network, starting from the cards 7, 3, 9, 1**

| Step | Compare positions | Cards after the step |
|---|---|---|
| Start |  | 7, 3, 9, 1 |
| 1 | 1 and 2 (7 is bigger, swap) | 3, 7, 9, 1 |
| 2 | 3 and 4 (9 is bigger, swap) | 3, 7, 1, 9 |
| 3 | 1 and 3 (3 is bigger, swap) | 1, 7, 3, 9 |
| 4 | 2 and 4 (7 is smaller, no swap) | 1, 7, 3, 9 |
| 5 | 2 and 3 (7 is bigger, swap) | 1, 3, 7, 9 |

We checked all 24 possible orders of four different cards: this five-step network sorts every one of them. Leave out the last comparator, though, and 16 of the 24 orders finish with the middle two cards the wrong way round, which shows exactly why that step is there. Steps 1 and 2 never touch the same cards, so two children could do them at the same moment, and the same is true of steps 3 and 4. The whole network therefore runs in three rounds of work, where checking every pair one after another, as a simple bubble sort does, takes six comparisons in a row. Doing work at the same time like this is called parallel processing.

### Making it a game

Draw four lines on paper, one for each position, and join pairs with short bridges for the comparators. Children walk cards along the lines, swapping at each bridge if needed. With several children as cards, it works in a playground too.

For a harder challenge, ask whether any network with only four comparators can sort four cards every time. Exploring that question is real computer science.

### Where it leads

The same thinking underlies how computers sort data with many processors at once. It also prepares children for competition questions that ask them to follow fixed rules exactly, a skill Bebras and the CAT both test.

Once the card version is easy, children can build the network in Scratch: four variables, five "if bigger then swap" blocks, and a check that the result is sorted.

## Picking a first competition, and preparing well

The right first competition depends on what a child enjoys and how they like to work. A short, low-pressure one is usually a better start than an ambitious one.

**A starting guide for primary students**

| If your child likes | Years 1 and 2 | Years 3 and 4 | Years 5 and 6 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Puzzles and logic | Unplugged puzzles at home | Bebras | Bebras, the CAT |
| Building with LEGO | FIRST LEGO League Explore | FIRST LEGO League Explore | FIRST LEGO League Challenge, RoboCup Junior |
| Making apps or games | Scratch projects for fun | Young ICT Explorers (Years 3-4) | Young ICT Explorers (Years 5-6), the coding challenge |
| Using computers well | Everyday software skills | ICAS Digital Technologies | ICAS Digital Technologies |

Our classes teach the thinking these competitions reward: reading instructions carefully, spotting patterns, tracing steps, and building small working programs in Scratch and later Python. Practice uses our own puzzles, never copied competition papers.

For team events, we teach children to program and debug; the team's robot or project stays the team's own work.

We are independent of every organiser on this page and do not enter students, run competitions or handle results. Most entries go through schools, and each organiser explains how families can take part.

We do not promise a score, a certificate level or a place. For a primary child, the main value of a first competition is the experience of thinking hard and enjoying it.

## Four rungs through primary school

Children move at their own pace; this is the usual order.

**From following steps to designing algorithms**

| Around | Rung | The sign it is secure |
|---|---|---|
| Years 1 and 2 | 1. Follows exact steps | Carries out a short sequence of instructions without skipping |
| Years 3 and 4 | 2. Spots patterns | Predicts what comes next in a sequence or puzzle |
| Years 4 and 5 | 3. Builds working programs | Makes a Scratch project that does what was planned |
| Years 5 and 6 | 4. Designs algorithms | Writes a step-by-step method and tests it on new cases |

### After a first competition

Ask which question was most fun and which was most confusing. Both answers point to what to explore next.
Keep the focus on enjoyment and effort. A primary child's first result says little about later ability.

## What parents and teachers ask

### Is there a coding competition for primary students in Australia?

Yes, several. Bebras from Year 3, the CAT and the Raspberry Pi Foundation Coding Challenge from Year 5, ICAS Digital Technologies from Year 2, plus team events such as RoboCup Junior, FIRST LEGO League and Young ICT Explorers.

### Which competitions need no coding?

Bebras and the Computational and Algorithmic Thinking competition need no coding experience, according to the Australian Maths Trust. ICAS Digital Technologies includes some programming questions but is mostly about digital skills.

### Is any primary coding competition free?

The Raspberry Pi Foundation Coding Challenge, run in Australia through the AMT, is free. GPN Junior workshops in Sydney are free too, though they are a program rather than a competition.

### What is a sorting network?

A fixed set of compare-and-swap steps that puts any list of a given length in order. Four items can be sorted by five comparators done in three rounds.

### What is Bebras?

An international computational thinking challenge delivered in Australia by the Australian Maths Trust. In 2026 it ran from 9 February to 8 March, with 60 minutes for Years 3 and 4 and 45 minutes for older divisions.

### What are the coding challenge divisions?

Novice and Explorer for block-based programmers, Navigator and Trailblazer for text-based programmers. Students choose by skill, not age.

### Can Year 1 or 2 children take part in anything?

FIRST LEGO League Explore is for ages 6 to 10 (Years 1 to 4), and ICAS Digital Technologies starts with an Introductory paper for Year 2.

### How do children enter?

Usually through their school. Each organiser explains its own entry process, and some team events can be entered through community groups.

### Do you prepare children for these competitions?

We teach the thinking and coding skills they reward, with our own practice puzzles. We are independent of every organiser and do not promise any result.

### What happens in the free class?

Your child tries a short puzzle and a small Scratch task with a teacher, and you get honest advice on which competition might suit them.
