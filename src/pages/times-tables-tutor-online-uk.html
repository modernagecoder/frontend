Times tables tutor online: recall built on understanding

Most parents searching for times tables help have a child who can chant the 2s and 5s but stalls on 7 × 8, or who plays the school's times tables game every evening without getting much faster. The national curriculum is clear about the destination: by the end of Year 4, children should have memorised their tables up to 12 × 12. It is just as clear that the route starts with arrays, doubling and the order of multiplication, not with a stopwatch. This page sets out which tables each year learns, a short count showing that only 28 facts are really hard, six strategies that turn a known fact into a new one, where apps such as TT Rock Stars help and where they do not, and how our live lessons work.

In short

Times tables in England follow the national curriculum: the 2, 5 and 10 tables in Year 2, the 3, 4 and 8 tables in Year 3, and every table up to 12 × 12 by the end of Year 4, when pupils take the statutory multiplication tables check of 25 on-screen questions. Fluent recall is the goal, built on understanding. Modern Age Coders teaches times tables live online for ages 6 to 11, in a group of 15 to 20 or 1-on-1, through Mental Maths for Kids. Year by year

Which times tables should my child know in each year?

The national curriculum introduces the tables in three waves across Years 2, 3 and 4, then keeps using them in Years 5 and 6.

Times tables in the national curriculum for England (DfE mathematics programmes of study)
YearAgesStatutory focusHow the curriculum links it
Year 26 to 7Recall and use facts for the 2, 5 and 10 tables, including odd and even numbersThe 10 table to place value, the 5 table to the clock face; multiplication can be done in any order, division cannot
Year 37 to 8Recall and use facts for the 3, 4 and 8 tables"Through doubling, they connect the 2, 4 and 8 multiplication tables."
Year 48 to 9Recall facts for tables up to 12 × 12; use factor pairs and commutativityCount in multiples of 6, 7, 9, 25 and 1000
Years 5 and 69 to 11Use the facts in multiples, factors, primes, fractions and long multiplicationFluency assumed from Year 4 onwards

The curriculum's summary for Years 3 and 4 sets the bar in one sentence: "By the end of year 4, pupils should have memorised their multiplication tables up to and including the 12 multiplication table and show precision and fluency in their work." Note the word division too. Every year's statement asks for "multiplication and division facts", so a child who knows 6 × 7 = 42 is expected to know 42 ÷ 7 = 6 just as quickly.

The Year 4 check, briefly

In June of Year 4 every child in a state-funded school in England takes the multiplication tables check. The STA's leaflet for parents describes it as "an onscreen check consisting of 25 times table questions", with 6 seconds for each one.

It adds: "There is no pass mark for the check", and that parents do not need to prepare anything extra. Our Year 4 check explainer and MTC practice page go into it in detail.

Why it is staying

The 2025 Curriculum and Assessment Review considered primary tests and concluded: "We recommend retaining the Multiplication Tables Check (MTC) in Year 4 as an important indicator of multiplicative competence."

In the 2025 check the national average score was 21.0 out of 25, and 37 per cent of eligible pupils scored full marks, the most common result, according to the DfE's published statistics.

The 28 hard facts

How many times tables facts are there to learn?

Fewer than it seems. A 12 by 12 grid has 144 squares, but our count shows that only 28 different facts are truly new once a child knows the easy tables and that order does not matter.

Shrinking the 12 × 12 grid (our own count)
StepFacts leftWhy
Start with the full grid14412 rows of 12 products, from 1 × 1 to 12 × 12
Use order (commutativity)783 × 8 and 8 × 3 are the same fact, so each pair counts once: 12 + 11 + ... + 1
Remove the 1, 2, 5, 10 and 11 tables28These follow simple patterns. What remains are the products of 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 12 with each other
Of those, squares such as 7 × 77 of the 28Squares are often learned as a set of their own

That third row is the honest size of the job. Twenty-eight facts, from 3 × 3 up to 12 × 12, are the ones that need real practice, and the curriculum gives children tools for most of them: doubling links the 2, 4 and 8 tables, and Year 4 asks children to "recognise and use factor pairs and commutativity in mental calculations". A child who has those tools rarely meets a fact that is completely new.

Patterns first

The 10s add a zero, the 5s are half the 10s, the 11s to 9 × 11 repeat the digit. These take days, not months.

Then links

The 4s are double the 2s and the 8s double the 4s; the 6s double the 3s. One known table opens another.

Then the stubborn few

Facts such as 7 × 8, 6 × 9 and 12 × 7 usually need a strategy and then steady, short practice to stick.

Strategies

What is the fastest way for a child to learn times tables?

Understanding first, then short daily recall. Here are six strategies of ours, each one building a new fact from a known one, in the spirit of the curriculum's derived facts.

1. The 9s from the 10s

Fact (ours). 9 × 7.

Known. 10 × 7 = 70.

Build. Nine sevens is one seven fewer than ten sevens: 70 − 7 = 63.

Why it works. Multiplying by 9 is multiplying by 10 and taking one group away. Draw it as an array of 10 rows with one row crossed out.

2. The 6s by doubling the 3s

Fact (ours). 6 × 8.

Known. 3 × 8 = 24.

Build. Six eights is two lots of three eights: 24 + 24 = 48.

Why it works. Six is double three, so every 6 times fact is double a 3 times fact.

3. Splitting 7 × 8 into 5 and 2

Fact (ours). 7 × 8, the one most children find hardest.

Split. Seven eights is five eights and two eights.

Build. 5 × 8 = 40 and 2 × 8 = 16, so 40 + 16 = 56.

Why it works. This is the distributive law that Year 4 pupils use, made visible by cutting an array into two parts.

4. The 8s as double, double, double

Fact (ours). 8 × 6.

Build. Double 6 is 12, double 12 is 24, double 24 is 48.

Check. It matches strategy 2, because 6 × 8 is the same fact.

Why it works. Eight is 2 × 2 × 2, which is exactly the curriculum's link between the 2, 4 and 8 tables.

5. The 12s as 10s and 2s

Fact (ours). 12 × 7.

Split. Twelve sevens is ten sevens and two sevens.

Build. 70 + 14 = 84.

Why it works. The 12 table is the newest in Year 4 and the easiest to derive, since 10 and 2 are both easy tables.

6. Division from the same fact

Fact (ours). 54 ÷ 9.

Ask. "Nine times what is 54?"

Recall. 6 × 9 = 54, so the answer is 6.

Why it works. Every times fact gives two division facts. Practising them together halves the work and matches the curriculum's "multiplication and division facts".

Practice set (ours, with answers). (1) 9 × 8 from 10 × 8. (2) 6 × 7 by doubling a 3 times fact. (3) 7 × 7 split into 5 × 7 and 2 × 7. (4) 4 × 12 by doubling twice. (5) 72 ÷ 8. (6) Name two factor pairs of 36. (7) Which is bigger, 6 × 9 or 7 × 8? Answers: (1) 80 − 8 = 72; (2) 21 + 21 = 42; (3) 35 + 14 = 49; (4) 24 then 48; (5) 9; (6) for example 4 and 9, 6 and 6, 3 and 12; (7) 7 × 8 = 56 is bigger than 6 × 9 = 54.

TT Rock Stars and apps

Is TT Rock Stars enough, or does my child need a tutor?

For many children a good practice app is enough once they understand the facts. A tutor helps when a child is practising facts they do not yet understand, or practising without getting faster.

Times Tables Rock Stars, run by Maths Circle Ltd, is the app many UK primary schools set for homework. Its own site says it is "Suitable for all learners aged 6 years and up" and that its "question-based games automatically adapt to each child's unique learning needs". For families it lists "eight different game modes to choose from, including multiplayer modes", and states plainly "No chat functions. No in-game purchases. No ads." It also lets a parent "De-clutter the screen or hide the timer to help minimise anxiety", a setting worth knowing about for a child who freezes under time pressure. Its FAQ says a child with a school login does not need a family subscription.

A weekly plan

How long should a child practise times tables each day?

A few minutes a day beats an hour on Sunday. Here is a sample week of ours for one stubborn table, which fits around school homework.

  1. Monday: build it. Make the 7 times table with an array or counters, and say each fact as a sentence: "four sevens are twenty-eight".
  2. Tuesday: link it. Use 5 × 7 and 2 × 7 to make the rest. Write the facts the child already knows in one colour.
  3. Wednesday: both ways. Mix multiplication and division: 7 × 6 and 42 ÷ 7, out loud, untimed.
  4. Thursday: little and quick. Five minutes on the school app, or ten flash cards, aiming for quick answers rather than perfect ones.
  5. Friday: use it. One real problem, such as "a week has 7 days; how many days are in 8 weeks?", and a check of which facts still need a strategy.

For a Year 2 child

Stay with the 2, 5 and 10 tables and odd and even numbers, as the curriculum asks. Count in fives round a clock face, and in tens along a number line.

There is no need to rush to the 7s. Secure 2s, 5s and 10s make Year 3 much easier.

For a Year 5 or 6 child

Gaps here hold back fractions, factors and long multiplication. Spend a few weeks closing them through the 28 hard facts, then use them straight away in real problems.

Times tables questions

What parents ask about times tables

By what age should a child know their times tables?

The national curriculum in England expects children to have memorised the tables up to 12 × 12 by the end of Year 4, usually age 9. The 2, 5 and 10 tables come in Year 2 and the 3, 4 and 8 tables in Year 3.

Which times tables are the hardest?

Usually the 6, 7, 8, 9 and 12 tables, and facts such as 7 × 8 and 6 × 9. By our count only 28 different facts remain once the easy tables and the order of multiplication are taken into account.

What is the fastest way to learn times tables?

Understand each fact first, by building it from a known one, then practise for a few minutes every day. Short daily practice works better than long weekly sessions.

Is TT Rock Stars good for learning times tables?

Its own site describes adaptive games for ages 6 and up, with no chat, ads or in-game purchases. It is strong for practice; a teacher helps when a child does not yet understand a fact or has stopped improving.

Do I need to pay for TT Rock Stars if the school uses it?

According to its FAQ, no: a child whose school has a subscription logs in with the account the teacher created. We are not connected with TT Rock Stars.

What is the Year 4 multiplication tables check?

A statutory on-screen check in June of Year 4, with 25 times table questions and 6 seconds for each. The STA says there is no pass mark and parents do not need to prepare their child specially.

Should my child learn division facts too?

Yes. Every year of the curriculum asks for multiplication and division facts together, so 42 ÷ 7 should be as quick as 6 × 7.

My child is in Year 6 and still unsure. Is it too late?

No. Most gaps come down to a handful of facts. A few weeks of strategies and short practice usually closes them, and it helps straight away with fractions and long multiplication.

When are lessons?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. All times are UK time.

Are you connected with schools or the STA?

No. Modern Age Coders is independent and has no link with any school, the Standards and Testing Agency, the Department for Education, TT Rock Stars or Maths Circle Ltd.
