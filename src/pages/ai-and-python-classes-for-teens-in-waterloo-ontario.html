AI and Python classes for teens in Waterloo

Where can a Waterloo teenager learn Python and AI properly?

Modern Age Coders teaches Python and AI live online to Waterloo teens aged 13 to 18, either in a group of fifteen to twenty students or with a private teacher. Most start with Python for Teens, then move to AI and Machine Learning for Teens, CCC preparation or the Ontario Computer Science Companion. Lessons are booked in Eastern time, and the first is free.

Few cities put a teenager closer to Canadian computing than Waterloo. The University of Waterloo’s Centre for Education in Mathematics and Computing, known as CEMC, writes the Canadian Computing Competition, hosts the Canadian Computing Olympiad and publishes free Python lessons, and a student here can use every one of them from a laptop at home. Being near all that does not, on its own, teach anyone to program. What turns it into skill is steady practice with someone who reads the code back and asks why. This page sets out what CEMC offers a Waterloo teen in 2026 to 2027, quoting CEMC, what Ontario computer studies courses expect, which AI tools a teenager may legally use, and a first machine learning project we built on the City of Waterloo’s own trail counter records.

What the University of Waterloo’s CEMC offers a teenager in 2026 to 2027

CEMC is part of the University of Waterloo, and most of what it publishes is free. Quotes below are CEMC’s, read from its own pages on 22 October 2026; summaries are ours.

CEMC computing resources a Waterloo teen can use, with what CEMC says about each (read 22 October 2026)
ResourceWhat CEMC saysWhat it means for a teen
Canadian Computing Competition (CCC)“North and South America: Wednesday, February 17, 2027”One online sitting at school. Junior or Senior is chosen by skill, “regardless of their grade”

One line on CEMC’s CCC page matters a great deal for a Python student: “it may not always be possible to achieve a perfect score with a particular language choice (for example, Python or Java)”. That is CEMC being candid about time limits on the hardest problems. Our reading is simple. Python is the right first language for almost every teen, and the right contest language for Junior and early Senior work; a student aiming at the fifth Senior problem, or at the CCO, eventually needs C++ as well, and we plan for that switch rather than discovering it in February.

Entry goes through a school

CEMC is plain that “all students must participate through their school.” A Waterloo teen cannot sign up for the CCC alone. The contest is “Written in school, participants write online, individually.” We help with the preparation; a teacher at school handles the registration.

Canada’s informatics team

CEMC’s 2026 news item says CEMC, “along with the Faculty of Mathematics at the University of Waterloo, supports the team that represents” Canada at the International Olympiad in Informatics, which “returned from Tashkent, Uzbekistan with four medals”.

Not an admission ticket

CEMC writes that the CCC “is not required for admission to the Faculty of Mathematics at the University of Waterloo.” Teens write it to learn and to be tested fairly, not because a door depends on it. Our Waterloo CS admission page covers the courses that do count.

No connection to CEMC or the university

Modern Age Coders is not part of CEMC or the University of Waterloo and is not endorsed by either. We never copy CEMC problems or solutions into lessons; students read the originals in CEMC’s free archive and practise on problems we write ourselves.

ICD2O is the only Ontario course on the current list whose own expectations name artificial intelligence. It asks students to weigh benefits and limitations, which is a different thing from building a model; its programming strand stops at the instruction to “use fundamental programming concepts to write simple programs”. ICS3U and ICS4U are where serious programming happens, and the 2008 document notes that “Each of the courses listed above is worth one credit.” None of these courses trains a neural network or evaluates one, and none has to. That gap, between talking about AI and testing a model against data it has never seen, is where our AI and machine learning course spends its time.

Waterloo data project

Can a model guess tomorrow’s traffic on the Laurel Trail? A first machine learning project

The City of Waterloo publishes hourly counts from counters on its trails. Our students download one counter’s history, train three very simple models on 2018 and test them on 2019. Every number in this section is our own calculation, not the City’s.

Our results on City of Waterloo Trail Counter Data, counter LOC_ID 4, Laurel Trail at University Ave. W. (44,114 hourly rows fetched 22 October 2026; trained on 356 complete days in 2018, tested on 360 complete days in 2019)
Model (trained on 2018)How it guesses a 2019 dayAverage miss on 2019 (people per day)
BaselineThe same number every day: the 2018 daily average, about 17190
Weekday modelThe 2018 average for that day of the week90
Month modelThe 2018 average for that month, from about 59 a day in January to 324 in July65

Here is the whole project in standard-library Python. It pages through the City’s public query service 2,000 rows at a time, adds the hourly counts up by date, keeps only dates with all 24 hours, and scores each model by its mean absolute error. It printed the three numbers in the table when we ran it on 22 October 2026:import json, urllib.request, datetime URL = ("https://services.arcgis.com/ZpeBVw5o1kjit7LT/arcgis/rest/services/" "City_of_Waterloo_Trail_Counter_Data/FeatureServer/0/query" "?where=LOC_ID%3D4&outFields=DATE,TOTAL_COUNT&orderByFields=OBJECTID" "&resultRecordCount=2000&f=json&resultOffset=") daily, hours, offset = {}, {}, 0 while True: page = json.load(urllib.request.urlopen(URL + str(offset))) rows = page["features"] if not rows: break for r in rows: a = r["attributes"] day = datetime.datetime.fromtimestamp(a["DATE"] / 1000, datetime.timezone.utc).date() daily[day] = daily.get(day, 0) + (a["TOTAL_COUNT"] or 0) hours[day] = hours.get(day, 0) + 1 offset += len(rows) full = [d for d in daily if hours[d] == 24] train = [(d, daily[d]) for d in full if d.year == 2018] test = [(d, daily[d]) for d in full if d.year == 2019] def fit(key): groups = {} for d, n in train: groups.setdefault(key(d), []).append(n) return {k: sum(v) / len(v) for k, v in groups.items()} def error(key): model = fit(key) return round(sum(abs(model[key(d)] - n) for d, n in test) / len(test)) print(offset, "hourly rows,", len(train), "train days,", len(test), "test days") print("same guess every day:", error(lambda d: 0)) print("guess by weekday:", error(lambda d: d.weekday())) print("guess by month:", error(lambda d: d.month))

1. Beat the baseline

A model is only interesting if it beats a dumb guess. Guessing the yearly average every day misses by 90 people a day on 2019. Knowing the weekday does not help at all here, which surprises most students; knowing the month cuts the miss to 65.

2. Split by time

Training on 2018 and testing on 2019 is honest in a way a random split is not: the model never sees the future it is graded on. Students then try training on 2017 to see whether a longer history helps.

3. Audit the labels

The same counter can carry two spellings. In the City’s file, LOC_ID 2 appears as both “Iron Horse Tail at John St. W.” and “Iron Horse Trail at John St. W.”, and LOC_ID 10 as both “Spur Line Trail at Roger Street” and “Spurline Trail South of Roger St.”. Group by ID, never by name.

What the model cannot know

The 2019 days averaged about 133 people against 171 in 2018, so every 2018 model guesses high. Is that a real change in walking and cycling, a counter moved or partly blocked, or something else? The data alone cannot say. The City’s own note warns that construction meant traffic “has been diverted around many EcoCounter installations resulting in a perceived drop in traffic”, without naming this counter. And 15 April 2018, 14 September 2019 and 15 September 2019 record zero people across all 24 hours, which looks more like a counter gap than an empty trail. A student who flags those days before trusting the error figure is doing real data science.

Two more honest details. The item description says “This data is updated daily”, yet the newest rows we could fetch are dated 30 November 2021, so this is a historical exercise, not a live feed. The whole table held 534,489 rows across all counters on the day we queried it; we used one counter so a laptop finishes in seconds. The data is used under the Waterloo Open Data User Licence, which lets anyone “use the Information commercially”. AI tools and age rules

Which AI tools a Waterloo teen may use, and where they are banned

Age limits come from each company’s own terms, and contest rules come from the organiser. We read each document on 22 October 2026 and teach inside those limits.

AI tool rules for students aged 13 to 17, from each organisation’s own document (read 22 October 2026)
Tool or settingWhat the document saysWhat we do
ChatGPT (OpenAI Terms of Use, effective January 1, 2026)“If you are under 18 you must have your parent or legal guardian’s permission to use the Services.”Used only with a parent’s permission, and never to write graded work
Claude (Anthropic Consumer Terms, effective Oct 8, 2025)“You must be at least 18 years old”Never recommended to anyone under 18
The CCC itself (CCC Online Grader rules)Internet use is forbidden, including “generative AI such as CoPilot or ChatGPT”, and “AI-based tools within an IDE cannot be used.”Contest practice is done with every AI helper switched off

The CCC rule is the one that changes how a teen should practise. On 17 February 2027 a CCC writer will have an editor, the official language documentation and their own head; the autocomplete that finishes a loop in a school assignment is gone. Students who learned with an assistant always on tend to freeze at that point. So our contest lessons run with AI tools off from the first week, and our machine learning lessons treat an AI tool as something to test, not something to trust.

Parents decide on ChatGPT

OpenAI puts the permission decision with the parent until 18. We tell families exactly where in a course a tool would be used, and a teen whose parent says no loses nothing: every lesson works without it.

Adults only, for some tools

Claude is an 18-and-over product under Anthropic’s terms, so it never appears in a lesson for teenagers. The agentic coding course in the row of four above is an adult course for the same reason, and we do not place teens in it.

Build the model yourself

The trail counter project uses no AI service at all. Fitting averages by hand is the start of machine learning, and a teen who can explain why the month model wins understands more than one who pasted a prompt.

Python before AI

Why a Waterloo teen should write Python before asking AI to

Because the hardest part of the trail project is not the code; it is noticing what is wrong with the result.

An AI assistant will happily write the trail counter script. It will not stop to ask why 2019 runs low, whether a zero day is a broken counter, or why the same ID carries two names. Those questions come from a student who has written the loop, seen the output and felt something was off. That instinct is what we are trying to build.

So we teach thinking first: break the problem into steps, write each step, predict the output, then check it. AI tools come in later, within the age rules above, as something a student questions the way a reviewer questions a draft. A teen who learns that way can use any tool that appears in the next five years; one who learned by pasting cannot.

The CCC rules ban AI helpers for exactly this reason: on contest day the thinking has to be the student’s own. The longer argument is in is coding still worth learning in 2026.

FAQ

Waterloo teen Python and AI questions

What Waterloo parents and teenagers ask us most.

Do you run Python and AI classes in Waterloo?

Yes, online and live. There is no Waterloo centre: teenagers from 13 to 18 log in from home, either to a group of fifteen to twenty or to private lessons with their own teacher.

Which course should a 13 or 14 year old start with?

Python for Teens, unless the free class shows they already write full programs. AI and Machine Learning for Teens comes after Python is fluent enough to read every line.

Does your course give an ICS3U or ICS4U credit?

No, and we do not grant Ontario credits of any kind. The credit belongs to the school course; our companion adds practice, explanations and project feedback on the side.

Can my teen use ChatGPT in your classes?

Only with your permission, because OpenAI's terms say users under 18 need a parent's or legal guardian's permission. Every lesson also works without it.

Is Claude suitable for a teenager?

No. Anthropic's terms say users must be at least 18, so we never recommend Claude to anyone under 18.

Can AI tools be used in the CCC?

No. The CCC grader rules forbid generative AI such as CoPilot or ChatGPT, and AI-based tools inside an editor. We train contest students with AI switched off.

How does a Waterloo student enter the CCC?

A teacher at the student's school registers them; CEMC does not accept individual sign-ups. The 2027 sitting is on Wednesday 17 February 2027, per CEMC, and we prepare students with problems we write ourselves.

Is Python good enough for the CCC?

For Junior and most of Senior, yes. CEMC itself warns that a perfect score may not always be possible in Python or Java, so students aiming at the top Senior problems or the CCO add C++.

What time are classes for Waterloo families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. All times are confirmed in Eastern time.

Is Modern Age Coders part of CEMC or the university?

No. We are an independent online school. We point students to CEMC's free resources and never reuse CEMC problems in our lessons.
