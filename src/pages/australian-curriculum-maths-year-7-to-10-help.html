Australian Curriculum maths, Years 7 to 10

Secondary maths in Australia is set out in the Australian Curriculum, Version 9.0, one year at a time from Year 7 to Year 10. It covers six strands, asks for reasoning and problem solving rather than only procedures, and by Year 10 expects students to model growth and decay, prove geometric results and analyse statistical claims in the media. Your state or territory decides how it is taught, and some have their own syllabuses, but the national content is the common map underneath. This page summarises that map, quotes what ACARA says each year should achieve, and works one lesson from the Years 9 and 10 content on measurement error and rounding, a topic students find easy to underestimate.

In short

Years 7 to 10 maths in the Australian Curriculum, Version 9.0, is written year by year in six strands: Number, Algebra, Measurement, Space, Statistics and Probability. Each year has an achievement standard. The content builds from prime factors and integers in Year 7 to irrational numbers, quadratics and trigonometry in Year 9, and growth and decay modelling in Year 10. States and territories decide how it is taught.

How the content is organised

Six strands, and the proficiency that runs through them

Every content description belongs to one strand and one year. The strands are listed in ACARA's Years 7 to 10 file.

The six Mathematics strands, Version 9.0, with one Year 9 or 10 content description from each, quoted
StrandExample content descriptionCode
Number"recognise the effect of using approximations of real numbers in repeated calculations and compare the results when using exact representations"AC9M10N01
Algebra"identify and graph quadratic functions, solve quadratic equations graphically and numerically, and solve monic quadratic equations with integer roots algebraically, using graphing software and digital tools as appropriate"AC9M9A04

ACARA's description of the learning area says the curriculum "focuses on the development of increasingly sophisticated knowledge and understanding of mathematical concepts, fluency in representations and procedures, and sound mathematical reasoning and problem-solving skills."

Those four ideas, understanding, fluency, reasoning and problem solving, are not separate topics. They are how every content description is meant to be learned, which is why a correct answer without working often earns less than parents expect.

On problem solving, ACARA says students engage in it "when they are presented with a problem situation for which they do not immediately know the answer". That definition matters at home: a page of identical exercises builds fluency, but it is not problem solving.

The codes are a practical tool. AC9M9M04, for example, reads as Australian Curriculum Version 9, Mathematics, Year 9, Measurement, content description 4. Quoting one to a teacher makes it easy to agree on exactly what needs work.

Where students usually stumble

Year 8 brings Pythagoras' theorem (AC9M8M06) and linear relations on the Cartesian plane. Students who are shaky on negative numbers and fractions from Year 7 tend to struggle here, because both topics lean on them constantly.

Year 9 is often the hardest jump. Expanding binomials, factorising monic quadratics, gradients and distances on the plane, and trigonometry in right-angled triangles all arrive within one year.

What Year 10 asks for

Year 10 adds exponential relations, growth and decay modelling, deductive proofs in geometry, scatterplots and two-way tables, and conditional probability.

It also asks for judgement. Content descriptions such as AC9M10M04, "identify the impact of measurement errors on the accuracy of results in practical contexts", have no single procedure; students have to think about where error comes from and how much it matters.

A lesson from Years 9 and 10

Error and approximation, worked through

AC9M9M04 asks students to calculate absolute, relative and percentage errors, and AC9M10N01 asks them to see what approximation does over repeated calculations. One elaboration suggests comparing a guessed attendance with the real one. We start there.

Three errors from one guess: an event expected 1,200 people and 1,050 came (our numbers)
MeasureHow it is worked outResult
Absolute errorDifference between estimate and actual: 1,200 minus 1,050150 people
Relative errorAbsolute error divided by the actual value: 150 divided by 1,050About 0.143
Percentage errorRelative error as a percentageAbout 14.3 per cent

The elaboration under AC9M9M04 describes exactly this: "comparing an estimation of the number of people expected to come to an event by subtracting the actual number that turned up to give an error, then converting this into a percentage error". It also asks students to consider when to use the absolute value and when not, depending on whether over-estimating and under-estimating should count the same.

Measurement brings in the same idea from the other side. A length read as 12.4 cm on a ruler marked in millimetres could be anywhere from 12.35 to 12.45 cm, so the absolute error is at most 0.05 cm and the percentage error is at most about 0.40 per cent. "All measurements are estimates" is a phrase from the content description itself.

AC9M10N01 extends this to repeated calculations. Take a circular path of radius 30 metres. One lap is 2 times pi times 30, about 188.50 metres, so 10 laps is 1,884.96 metres to two decimal places.

Use 3.14 for pi and 10 laps comes to 1,884.00 metres, out by 0.96 metres, about 0.05 per cent. Round each lap to the nearest metre first, giving 188, and 10 laps becomes 1,880 metres, out by 4.96 metres, about 0.26 per cent. Rounding early made the error five times larger, which is the effect the Year 10 standard asks students to recognise.

Students who understand this stop rounding in the middle of a calculation, keep exact values or full calculator precision until the end, and round once, to an accuracy that suits the context. That habit, which AC9M7N05 begins in Year 7 with rounding "to a given accuracy appropriate to the context", protects marks in every senior maths subject.

Quotations: ACARA, Mathematics curriculum content 7 to 10, Version 9.0, AC9M7N05, AC9M9M04 and AC9M10N01 with elaborations, read on 7 October 2026. The event, ruler and track figures are ours, computed in Python.

Looking past Year 10

The optional content for senior maths pathways

Year 10 is where students start choosing between senior maths subjects, and ACARA provides extra material for those heading towards the most demanding ones.

ACARA's description of the learning area includes optional content for post Year 10 pathways. It says that "preparation for subsequent study of Mathematical Methods Units 1 and 2 can be supported by further development of aspects of mathematics content in Year 10".

In other words, students who intend to take the more advanced senior maths subject benefit from going further in Year 10 than the core content requires. Schools decide whether and how to offer this.

Senior subjects are not national. Each state's authority sets its own maths courses and names them differently. We cover several of those on separate pages, linked below, rather than guessing at one set of rules for all of Australia.

What carries across every state is the Years 7 to 10 foundation. A student secure in algebra, functions and trigonometry by the end of Year 10 has options; a student with gaps there finds the senior choices narrowed for them.

Questions about Years 7 to 10 maths

What Australian families ask

What are the strands of the Australian Curriculum maths for Years 7 to 10?

Number, Algebra, Measurement, Space, Statistics and Probability. Each year from Year 7 to Year 10 has content descriptions in all six and its own achievement standard.

What is percentage error?

The absolute error, which is the difference between a measured or estimated value and the actual value, divided by the actual value and written as a percentage. The Year 9 content asks students to calculate and interpret it.

What is the difference between absolute and relative error?

Absolute error is the size of the difference in the original units. Relative error divides that difference by the actual value, so errors in large and small quantities can be compared fairly.

Why does rounding too early matter?

Each rounded step adds a small error, and repeated steps can add those errors up. The Year 10 content asks students to recognise this effect and compare it with exact calculation.

Is maths taught the same way in every state?

The national curriculum is the shared content, but each state and territory decides how it is implemented and some publish their own syllabuses. Senior maths subjects are set by each state.

What is the optional content in Year 10?

ACARA provides optional content for students heading towards the most demanding senior maths, to support later study of Mathematical Methods. Schools decide whether to use it.

Which year is hardest?

Many students find Year 9 the biggest step, because quadratics, coordinate geometry and trigonometry arrive close together.

Can you help my child catch up?

Yes. We begin by finding the specific gaps, usually from earlier years, then rebuild those before moving on. We do not promise a particular grade.

Are you connected with ACARA?

No. We are an independent online school, and we quote ACARA because it publishes the curriculum.

When would lessons be in Australia?

We set a weekly time after the free class, usually after school or in the evening in your state. Every Australian time zone is ahead of India time.
