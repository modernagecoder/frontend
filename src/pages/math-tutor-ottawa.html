Where can an Ottawa student get live online math tutoring?

Modern Age Coders gives Ottawa students live online math lessons from Grade 1 through Grade 12, one to one or in a class of 15 to 20 at a single level. For Grades 11 and 12 most choose Ontario Grade 12 University Math (MHF4U and MCV4U); contest-keen students choose Waterloo Math Contests: Gauss to Euclid; children choose a maths course for their age. A first lesson is free.

Ottawa has a calendar students can feel: long cold winters, warm summers, and a return of each on a fairly reliable schedule. Mathematics has a name for that kind of regular rise and fall, a periodic function, and Ontario students spend much of Grade 11 learning to model it with sine and cosine. This page follows periodic behaviour and rates of change through the Ontario courses, sends you to our detailed page for each stage, and then builds a model from real Ottawa data. The City of Ottawa publishes the daily temperature of Ottawa River water entering the Britannia water purification plant, for every year since 2005, and one year of it is enough to build, test and question a sinusoid the way MCR3U asks.

Sinusoidal modelling is a place where Grade 11 students can lose marks without knowing why. The algebra of y = a cos(k(x − d)) + c is short, but each letter has to be read off a real situation: how high and low, how long one cycle lasts, where it peaks. A student who only memorises transformations struggles the moment the data are real, which is exactly when the course starts to matter.

The ministry lists Grade 10 academic math as the entry to MCR3U, and says MHF4U must come before or alongside MCV4U. For an Ottawa student planning Grade 12 calculus, that makes Grade 11 the hinge year: secure sinusoids and rates there, and both Grade 12 courses are much easier.

Ottawa data lesson

A year of Ottawa River temperatures, modelled with a cosine curve

The City of Ottawa publishes the daily temperature of raw river water at the Britannia water purification plant. We built a sinusoid from the 2025 column and compared it with every day of that year. The model and its errors are our calculations.

The City’s description is unusually exact. Each daily value is the average of two readings, recorded 12 hours apart, around 06:00 and 18:00, using a certified probe accurate to plus or minus half a degree. The intake sits approximately 427 m out from the plant, where 12 reinforced fibreglass bells on the last 29 m of pipe rest at an approximate depth of 10 m, with an intake capacity of 364 ML/day. The workbook we downloaded has one row per calendar day and one column for each year from 2005 to 2025; it has no row for 29 February, so leap days are absent.

Our figures from the City of Ottawa workbook, 2025 column, downloaded 22 October 2026 (degrees Celsius)
Measure2025
Warmest day26.75 on 13 August (day 225)
Coldest day0.27 on 17 January (day 17)
Mean and median of the 365 days11.17 and 9.5
Monthly means, January to March0.89, 1.02, 1.15
Monthly means, July and August24.87 and 24.76
Days at or above 20 degrees102
Days at or below 1 degree47

From those numbers a Grade 11 student can write a model in four moves. The amplitude is half the range, (26.75 − 0.27) ÷ 2 = 13.24. The midline is the average of the extremes, 13.51. The period is one year, 365 days. And the curve peaks on day 225, so a cosine shifted right by 225 fits: T(d) = 13.24 cos(2π(d − 225) ÷ 365) + 13.51. The program below builds exactly that model from the workbook and measures how far it sits from the real temperatures:import datetime, math import openpyxl # pip install openpyxl sheet = openpyxl.load_workbook("2025_Ottawa_River_water_temperature.xlsx", read_only=True).worksheets[0] rows = list(sheet.iter_rows(max_row=400, values_only=True)) header = next(i for i, row in enumerate(rows) if row[0] == "Date") col = rows[header].index(2025) temps = [row[col] for row in rows[header + 1:] if isinstance(row[0], datetime.datetime)] amp = (max(temps) - min(temps)) / 2 mid = (max(temps) + min(temps)) / 2 peak_day = temps.index(max(temps)) + 1 def model(day): return amp * math.cos(2 * math.pi * (day - peak_day) / 365) + mid errors = [abs(temps[d - 1] - model(d)) for d in range(1, 366)] print("amplitude", amp, "midline", mid, "peak on day", peak_day) print("mean error, degrees:", round(sum(errors) / 365, 2))

How good is it?

Across all 365 days the model is off by 2.49 degrees on average. For a curve built from only two numbers and a date, that is respectable, and it captures the shape of the year.

Where it fails

In spring the model runs warm: on 1 June it predicts 17.60 degrees against 13.45 measured. The coldest day is 208 days before the warmest, not the 182.5 a symmetric sine wave assumes, so the river warms more slowly than it cools.

The winter floor

From January to March the monthly means stay between 0.89 and 1.15. River water at the intake cannot cool far below freezing, so the bottom of the curve is flat. A sinusoid has no flat parts, which is a limitation worth naming in any report.

A worked problem, ours

How fast was the river warming on 1 June 2025?

A calculus question with a twist, for an MHF4U or MCV4U student. The temperatures are the City of Ottawa’s 2025 values; the question and solution are ours.

Selected 2025 daily temperatures from the City of Ottawa workbook (degrees Celsius)
Date1 May17 May27 May31 May1 June2 June6 June16 June
Temperature7.2513.012.614.213.4513.616.6519.6

Part (a), an average rate. From 1 May to 1 June the temperature rose from 7.25 to 13.45 degrees over 31 days, an average rate of change of (13.45 − 7.25) ÷ 31 = 0.20 degrees per day.

Part (b), narrowing in. MCV4U estimates an instantaneous rate with ever smaller intervals centred on the moment. Using 17 May and 16 June (15 days either side): (19.6 − 13.0) ÷ 30 = 0.22. Using 27 May and 6 June (5 days either side): (16.65 − 12.6) ÷ 10 ≈ 0.405.

Part (c), the twist. Using 31 May and 2 June (1 day either side): (13.6 − 14.2) ÷ 2 = −0.30. The river was not cooling in early June; day-to-day wobble in the measurements, each only accurate to half a degree, swamps the signal when the interval is tiny. With real data, shrinking the interval does not converge the way it does for a formula.

Part (d), use the model instead. Differentiate T(d) = 13.24 cos(2π(d − 225) ÷ 365) + 13.51 to get T′(d) = −13.24 × (2π ÷ 365) sin(2π(d − 225) ÷ 365). At d = 152, the angle is 2π(−73) ÷ 365 ≈ −1.257 radians and its sine is about −0.951, so T′(152) ≈ 13.24 × 0.01721 × 0.951 ≈ 0.217 degrees per day: close to the 15-day estimate, and far steadier than the 1-day one.

Try it yourself. On which day of the year does the model say the river warms fastest, and what is that rate? Compare it with the real temperatures a week either side of that day.

Contests for Ottawa students

A Grade 11 contest year in Ottawa: Fermat in February, Hypatia in April

CEMC, the University of Waterloo centre behind the most widely written Canadian math contests, sets one paper for each grade. For a Grade 11 student in Ottawa there are two to plan around.

The two CEMC contests for Grade 11 in 2027, as CEMC lists them (read 22 October 2026)
ContestPaperDate
Fermat25 multiple-choice questions, 60 minutes23 or 24 February 2027
Hypatia4 questions, a mix of answer only and full solution, 75 minutesWednesday 7 April 2027

The two papers test different strengths. Fermat rewards speed and the instinct to avoid long calculations; Hypatia rewards patient, complete reasoning that a marker can follow line by line, much like part (c) of the river problem, where the honest answer is that the data cannot support the calculation. Our Fermat preparation and Fryer, Galois and Hypatia guide cover each paper, and the Waterloo contests page covers every grade.

School entry only

CEMC contests are written through schools; there is no individual entry. An Ottawa student whose school is not taking part can, on CEMC’s suggestion, ask another local school to host them.

Ten years of papers

CEMC’s past contests are free to download with no sign-up. We use them for timed practice and teach with original problems in class.

A second organiser

The Canadian Mathematical Society runs the COMC, held on 29 October 2026; its registration closed on 22 October. The COMC leads by invitation to the CMO. Our COMC guide explains the path.

Not connected with CEMC or CMS

CEMC’s rule is that “all students must participate through their school”. We are not connected with CEMC, the University of Waterloo or the Canadian Mathematical Society. We never reproduce their problems or solutions; our practice problems are original and labelled as ours.

Math and AI

Can an Ottawa student hand the river model to AI?

It will fit a curve. It will not tell you the curve is wrong in winter unless you already know to ask.

Give an AI tool the 2025 column and it can return a fitted sinusoid in seconds. It is much less likely to notice the flat winter floor, the lopsided spring, or that a one-day derivative from noisy data is meaningless. Seeing those things is mathematical judgement, and it belongs to the student.

Our lessons keep that judgement where it belongs: sketch the year first, estimate amplitude and period by eye, build the model by hand, and only then let software check. Work handed in for a school mark is the student’s own.

Ontario math asks students to model, reason and communicate. Software can draw the curve; deciding whether to trust it is still a person’s job. The longer argument is in why careful thinking still matters when software does the sums.
