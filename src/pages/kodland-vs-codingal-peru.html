Why this comparison

Why do Peruvian parents compare Kodland and Codingal?

Because both appear when a parent searches for online coding classes for children, and on the surface they look alike: live classes, a free trial, projects, certificates. Underneath, the language and the shape of the class differ.

The language question

One site is written in Spanish and the other in English. For many Peruvian families that single fact decides more than any feature list.

The format question

Small group, one to one, or both. Each format changes how much a child talks, how fast they move and what a month costs a family.

The fit question

Ages, subjects and equipment. A school can be right for a 9-year-old and wrong for a 15-year-old, or the other way round.

We are a third school, so we have an interest. To keep this fair we quote each provider only from its own pages, read in full on 9 November 2026; we leave out prices, ratings and reviews entirely; and we do not declare a winner. Pages change, so check anything important on each school's site before deciding.

Kodland, in its own words

What does Kodland's Spanish site say it offers?

A school of programming and design for children and teenagers, taught live by tutors in Spanish, in small groups or one to one, on Kodland's own platform.

Subjects and format

The Spanish homepage describes practical programming, video-game and design content "con tutores en vivo y proyectos reales" (with live tutors and real projects). Its course cards include Roblox game design, Minecraft modding and Python, plus graphic design, drawing and a maths strand that it says reinforces the school curriculum.

On the path from beginner to typed code it says: "De programación por bloques a código en JavaScript o Python" (from block programming to code in JavaScript or Python). It lists two formats, "Grupos pequeños" and "1-1 con tutor".

Ages, rhythm and equipment

The page title says ages 5 to 17. Kodland's own FAQ says its courses are designed for children and teenagers "de 8 a 17 años". Both statements are on its site, so ask which applies to your child's course.

The FAQ says group classes run "una vez a la semana en el horario elegido" (once a week at the chosen time), that classes need a desktop PC or laptop because tablets are not suitable, and that students receive electronic certificates.

What the site is for Peru: the Spanish edition we read does not mention Peru by name. Kodland may run editions in other languages; we reviewed only kodland.org/es and its Spanish FAQ. If Spanish-language teaching is what your child needs, it is a format worth trying in a trial class. Questions about trust and online opinions of Kodland are covered, in Spanish, on Kodland: opiniones y una alternativa en inglés.

Codingal, in its own words

What does Codingal's site say it offers?

Live one-to-one coding and AI classes for children and teenagers, in English, at times the family selects, across a catalogue that runs from block coding to exam and olympiad courses.

Subjects and format

Its homepage headline offers "1:1 live AI & coding classes for kids and teens with expert teachers". The catalogue runs from Scratch and block coding through Python, web, app and Roblox development to data science, a course for the AP Computer Science A exam in Java, and an algorithms course aimed at the IOI.

For parents it describes a first Parent Teacher Meeting after the sixth session and progress reports after each meeting.

Ages, timing and equipment

The homepage says Codingal "supports learners from ages 6-18 and Grades 1-12". Its FAQ also says its classes "start from age 5". As with Kodland, ask which applies to the course you want.

On timing, the FAQ says families can select "any time and any day". On equipment it asks for a laptop or computer with a webcam and a stable connection, and says a tablet works with an external keyboard.

What the site is for Peru: the page is written in English, and we found no mention of Peru on it. Grades are given in the US style (Grades 1 to 12), so a Peruvian parent will need to translate from primaria and secundaria. Roughly, MINEDU's 1° de primaria at age 6 matches Grade 1.

Gaps, and a lesson

What should a Peruvian family ask that neither page answers?

Mostly practical things: the hour in Peru time, the teaching language when a child is stuck, the equipment at home, and how to stop. Keeping notes on every school in the same shape makes the gaps obvious, and that is the idea behind this short Python lesson.

Notes as dictionaries

Write each school's facts as a Python dictionary: one key per question, one value per answer. A second dictionary, blank, holds every question you care about, each set to "not stated". Merging the two should give a full row with the gaps still marked.

Python 3.9 and later merge dictionaries with the | operator. When both sides have the same key, the right-hand side wins. That one rule decides whether your notes come out right.

The bug, and the fix

In the program, wrong = school_a | blank puts the blanks on the right, so every real answer is overwritten by "not stated". The output shows it: wrong: not stated | not stated.

Swapping the order, blank | school_a, keeps the facts and fills only the missing keys. A list comprehension then names the gaps, which become your questions for the trial class. School A and School B here are invented, not Kodland or Codingal.

  1. Predict. Before running it, say which line prints "not stated" twice, and why.
  2. Add a key. Put 'hours': 'not stated' in blank and watch both schools gain a gap.
  3. Count. Print how many questions each school has answered, using len(facts).
  4. Explain. In one English sentence, say what "the right-hand side wins" means for a | b.

This is the kind of lesson our teen students meet early: a small, real problem, one surprising result, and a rule they will reuse for years. Merging settings, configuration files and API responses all follow the same right-hand-wins rule.

How to choose

How should a family in Peru choose between online coding schools?

With one checklist for every school, Kodland, Codingal, us or anyone else, filled in after a trial class rather than from the homepage.

  1. Language. Which language is every lesson in, and what happens when your child does not understand?
  2. Format. Group or one to one, how many children per teacher, and can you switch later?
  3. Hours on your clock. Ask for the class time in Peru time, written down.
  4. Ages. Ask which age range applies to the specific course, not the school as a whole.
  5. Equipment. A computer, a camera, a headset; check what the course truly needs. In INEI's survey for April to June 2026, only 36.4% of homes across Peru owned even one computer.
  6. Thinking, not copying. In the trial, does the teacher ask your child to predict and explain, or mainly to copy?
  7. Progress you can see. How will you know, after a month, what your child can now do alone?
  8. Stopping. How do you pause or stop, and what notice is needed?

Online habits differ by age. INEI's figures for April to June 2026 put internet use at 92.5% among teenagers of 12 to 18, but 68.2% among children of 6 to 11. A teenager can usually manage a live class alone; a younger child often needs an adult nearby for the first few weeks, whichever school you choose.

Kodland, Codingal and our classes: common questions

Are your classes in Spanish?

No. Every class is live, online and in English only, and our mentors in India keep their English clear and paced to each child, accent included. For a child who cannot yet follow a lesson in English, a Spanish-language school is the kinder choice at present.

Which is better, Kodland or Codingal?

We do not rank other schools. From their own pages, Kodland's Spanish site offers small groups or one-to-one in Spanish, and Codingal's English site offers one-to-one in English. The better fit depends on your child's language, age and how they like to learn; a trial at each is the fairest test.

Is Kodland reliable?

We cannot judge another school for you. Check what any school says about itself on its own site, ask for its legal company details and terms, try a trial class, and see what your child can do after a month. Those checks apply to us too.

Why does this page not show their prices?

By choice. Prices change, are often shown differently by country, and comparing them out of context misleads. Each school publishes its own; we show only ours, through the fees section of this page.

Are you connected with Kodland or Codingal?

No. We are an independent online school and have no link with either. We describe them only from their own websites as read on 9 November 2026.

What ages do you teach?

From 6 upwards, including teenagers, university students and adults. Younger children start with Scratch; typed Python usually starts at about 10, and Java at about 13.

When are your classes in Peru time?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. Peru is 10 hours 30 minutes behind India all year, so 19:00 in Lima is 05:30 the next morning in India.

Group or one to one: which should we pick?

Groups of fifteen to twenty suit children who enjoy company and a steady pace. One-to-one suits children who need a slower start in English, have exam goals, or need unusual hours. You can switch later.

Do you give certificates?

Yes, our own certificate of completion at the end of a course. It is not a Peruvian qualification and is not issued or recognised by MINEDU.

How do we pay from Peru?

By card, monthly, in US dollars; your bank converts it into soles. The first class is free and needs no card.
