What Math 30-1 covers, outcome by outcome

Alberta Education describes the course this way in its 2026 to 2027 bulletin: Math 30-1 builds the “critical-thinking skills identified for entry into post-secondary programs that require the study of calculus”, and “algebraic, numerical, and graphical approaches are used to solve problems.” The program of studies groups the course under three general outcomes. The fine print students miss

The general notes in the same document carry rules that cost marks when they are ignored. One is about order: “Stretches and reflections are performed prior to translations unless otherwise stated.” A student who translates first gets the right shape in the wrong place.

Another is about technology. For the transformation, inverse and logarithm outcomes, and for factoring polynomials, Alberta says that “students are expected to meet these outcomes without the use of technology”, even though they may use it to explore. In practice that means the graphing calculator can confirm an answer but cannot be the method.

Seven processes, not just topics

Alberta lists seven mathematical processes that are meant to run through the whole course: Communication [C], Connections [CN], Mental Mathematics and Estimation [ME], Problem Solving [PS], Reasoning [R], Technology [T] and Visualization [V]. Every specific outcome is tagged with some of them.

Communication is the one families underestimate. The bulletin says students are expected to “communicate solutions clearly and effectively when solving both routine and non-routine problems.” On the diploma exam that is not a slogan: it is a quarter of the mark, the written-response part.

What markers say about written responses

The written-response observations are blunter. Markers saw many students who do not grasp “the difference between simplifying or factoring an expression and solving an equation”, or “the difference between verifying and proving a trigonometric identity”. Those are not slips; they are misunderstandings of what the question asked.

Smaller habits cost marks too: students “forget to include the angle argument” (writing sin instead of sin x), drop brackets, and “write expressions in place of equations”. Alberta also warns that “Using abbreviations in written explanations is inappropriate and not acceptable.”

What they say students do well

The commentary is not all bad news. Alberta reports that students “continue to perform well” on coterminal angles, arc length and angles in standard position, that they can “solve exponential equations that can be simplified to a common base”, and that they relate the parameters of a sinusoidal equation to its graph.

That shapes how we spend time. A student already strong on sinusoidal graphs does not need another week of them; the hour goes to the identity substitution or the multi-case counting problem instead.

Worked problems, ours

Two Math 30-1 problems, solved the way markers want to see

Both problems are our own, written for this page, and both sit on a line of Alberta’s commentary above. They are not Alberta Education questions. Each one is laid out as a full written response: every step shown, every restriction stated, every answer checked.

Problem 1: a rational function with a hole. For f(x) = (x2 − 4) / (x2 − x − 2), state the non-permissible values, the point of discontinuity, the asymptotes, and the domain and range.

  1. Factor numerator and denominator. x2 − 4 = (x − 2)(x + 2) and x2 − x − 2 = (x − 2)(x + 1). So f(x) = (x − 2)(x + 2) / ((x − 2)(x + 1)).
  2. Record the non-permissible values before cancelling. The original denominator is zero at x = 2 and x = −1. Both are excluded from the domain, whatever happens next.
  3. Cancel, and name what the cancelled factor leaves behind. The factor (x − 2) cancels, leaving y = (x + 2) / (x + 1) with x ≠ 2. A factor that cancels gives a hole, not an asymptote.
  4. Find the hole’s coordinates. Substitute x = 2 into the simplified form: (2 + 2) / (2 + 1) = 4/3. The point of discontinuity is (2, 4/3).
  5. Find the asymptotes. The factor (x + 1) remains in the denominator, so x = −1 is a vertical asymptote. Numerator and denominator have equal degree with leading coefficients 1 and 1, so y = 1 is the horizontal asymptote.
  6. Write the domain. {x | x ≠ −1, x ≠ 2, x ∈ R}.
  7. Write the range, and check the hole. The simplified form is 1 + 1/(x + 1), which never equals 1. It equals 4/3 only when x + 1 = 3, that is x = 2, which is excluded. So the range is {y | y ≠ 1, y ≠ 4/3, y ∈ R}. Forgetting the second restriction is exactly the slip the commentary describes.

Problem 2: an equation that needs an identity substitution. Solve 2cos2x − sin x − 1 = 0 algebraically for 0 ≤ x < 2π, giving exact values.

  1. Notice the problem. The equation mixes cos2x and sin x, so it cannot be factored as it stands. One function has to go.
  2. Choose the identity that leaves one function. From sin2x + cos2x = 1, replace cos2x with 1 − sin2x: 2(1 − sin2x) − sin x − 1 = 0.
  3. Simplify to a quadratic in sin x. 2 − 2sin2x − sin x − 1 = 0 becomes 2sin2x + sin x − 1 = 0 after multiplying by −1.
  4. Factor and set each factor to zero. (2sin x − 1)(sin x + 1) = 0, so sin x = 1/2 or sin x = −1. Markers specifically remind students to set factors equal to zero rather than stopping at the factored form.
  5. Solve each case on the unit circle. sin x = 1/2 at x = π/6 and x = 5π/6 (quadrants I and II). sin x = −1 at x = 3π/2.
  6. State the solution set and check it. x = π/6, 5π/6 or 3π/2. Substituting x = 3π/2: 2(0)2 − (−1) − 1 = 0. The other two check the same way, and a graph of the left side crosses zero at exactly these three points in the domain.

Why the steps are numbered

Alberta’s directing word algebraically means “Using mathematical procedures that involve variables or symbols to represent values”. A graphing calculator would find the three solutions of problem 2 in seconds, but a response that only reports them has not answered a question that says algebraically.

Writing one step per line also makes the response markable. Alberta scores each written-response part against a general scoring guide, and partial credit is available, so a student who sets up the substitution correctly and then slips still earns something.

What the two problems have in common

In both, the trap is a restriction. In problem 1 it is a value the cancelled factor removed; in problem 2 it is the solutions hiding in a second quadrant. Neither is hard mathematics. Both are skipped by students in a hurry.

So our students finish every solution with the same question: what did I exclude, and did I find everything in the domain? It takes ten seconds and it is the habit we most want them to carry into the exam room.

Practice, ours

Ten Math 30-1 practice questions

We wrote these ten for this page; none is an Alberta Education question and none comes from a released exam. They follow the course from functions to combinatorics. Work them without a calculator unless a question says otherwise, then check the answers in the next section.

  1. Composition. Given f(x) = 2x − 3 and g(x) = x2, evaluate (f ∘ g)(−2) and (g ∘ f)(−2). Are they equal?
  2. Transformations. The point (6, −4) is on the graph of y = f(x). Find its image on the graph of y = −2f(3(x − 1)) + 5.
  3. Invariant points. The graph of y = x2 − 9 is reflected in the x-axis. How many invariant points are there, and what are they?
  4. Logarithm laws. Write 2log5x − log5(x + 1) + log53 as a single logarithm.
  5. Common base. Solve 4x + 1 = 8x − 1 algebraically.
  6. No common base (calculator allowed). Solve 3x = 20, to the nearest hundredth.
  7. Logarithmic equation. Solve log3(x + 6) + log3x = 3 algebraically, and justify any value you reject.
  8. Polynomials. Factor P(x) = x3 − 2x2 − 5x + 6 and state its zeros and y-intercept.
  9. Radians. A circle has radius 12 cm. Find the arc subtended by a central angle of 2π/3, exactly and to the nearest hundredth. Then give the negative angle coterminal with 5π/3 that lies between −2π and 0.
  10. Counting. How many different arrangements of the letters in CANADA are there? How many begin and end with A?

Questions 2, 3, 4 and 7 come straight from the commentary’s list of weak spots, so a student who gets them right has fixed something markers see often. A question you cannot start is information, not failure: note it, and bring it to a class.

Answers, ours

Answers to the ten questions, with the reasoning

Each answer was checked by substitution and in Python. The reasoning matters more than the number, because on the written-response part it is the reasoning that earns the marks.

  1. 5 and 49; not equal. (f ∘ g)(−2) = f(g(−2)) = f(4) = 5. (g ∘ f)(−2) = g(f(−2)) = g(−7) = 49. Composition is not commutative, which is the point of the question.
  2. (3, 13). The mapping rule is (x, y) to (x/3 + 1, −2y + 5): the horizontal stretch by 1/3 and the reflection and vertical stretch come before the translations. x: 6/3 + 1 = 3. y: −2(−4) + 5 = 13.
  3. Two: (−3, 0) and (3, 0). A reflection in the x-axis leaves a point unchanged only where y = 0, so the invariant points are the x-intercepts. Solve x2 − 9 = 0.
  4. log5(3x2 / (x + 1)). Power law first: 2log5x = log5x2. Then the product law joins x2 and 3, and the quotient law puts x + 1 underneath.
  5. x = 5. Write both sides as powers of 2: 22x + 2 = 23x − 3. Equate exponents: 2x + 2 = 3x − 3, so x = 5. Check: 46 = 84 = 4096.
  6. x ≈ 2.73. Take logarithms of both sides: x = log 20 / log 3 = 2.7268..., which rounds to 2.73. Keep every decimal until the final step, as the bulletin instructs.
  7. x = 3. The product law gives log3(x(x + 6)) = 3, so x2 + 6x = 27 and (x + 9)(x − 3) = 0. Reject x = −9: log3(−9) is undefined, since a logarithm takes positive arguments only. Check x = 3: log39 + log33 = 2 + 1 = 3.
  8. (x − 1)(x + 2)(x − 3); zeros −2, 1 and 3; y-intercept 6. P(1) = 1 − 2 − 5 + 6 = 0, so x − 1 is a factor by the factor theorem. Dividing leaves x2 − x − 6 = (x + 2)(x − 3).
  9. 8π cm, about 25.13 cm; and −π/3. Arc length is radius times angle in radians: 12 × 2π/3 = 8π. For the coterminal angle, subtract one full turn: 5π/3 − 2π = −π/3.
  10. 120, then 24. CANADA has six letters with A repeated three times: 6! / 3! = 720 / 6 = 120. Fixing an A at each end leaves C, N, D and A in the middle, all different, so 4! = 24.

Why functions come first

Relations and Functions is more than half of the exam blueprint, and its ideas return everywhere: a sinusoidal graph is a transformed sine function, and an exponential model is a function to be inverted. Students who master mapping notation early find the trigonometry unit shorter.

Logarithms come early for a different reason. They take repetition to become automatic, and a student who meets them in October has months of spaced practice before the exam. A student who meets them in May has days.

What a weekly class looks like

A class starts with five minutes of mixed retrieval from earlier units, then the current school topic taught from the student’s own homework, then one written response laid out in full. The student does the writing; the teacher asks questions.

Every few weeks we set a short mixed set in the diploma style: machine-scored questions first, then one written response. Its mistakes go into a log the student keeps until exam week.

For the exam itself, Alberta’s schedule for 2026 to 2027 sets Mathematics 30-1 at 9 AM on Tuesday 3 November 2026, and on Wednesday 20 January, Thursday 8 April, Thursday 17 June and Thursday 5 August 2027. The exam earns a separate plan for its final weeks, built around the written-response rules, and we start that plan about eight weeks before the student’s sitting. Calculator, Math 31 and the credit

The calculator, the step to Math 31, and who grants the credit

Three questions families ask in the first class, answered from Alberta’s documents.

Which calculator?

The bulletin says the Math 30-1 diploma exam “requires the use of an approved graphing calculator”, cleared and configured before and after the exam, with the approved list in Alberta’s 2026 to 2027 Calculator Information and Rules document. We teach on the model the student will write with.

Where Math 31 fits

Alberta’s Guide to Education, updated 4 September 2026, says “Mathematics 30-1 is a prerequisite or corequisite for Mathematics 31.” Its course chart shows Math 10C to 20-1 to 30-1 as the typical route, and notes that each math course is 5 credits.

Who grants the credit

The credit and the school-awarded 70 per cent come from the student’s own school, and the 30 per cent from Alberta’s diploma exam. Our classes run beside that school course, and we do not grant provincial credits.

Edmonton, Calgary and the rest of Alberta

Classes are live online, so a student in Edmonton, Calgary, Red Deer or a rural school division gets the same teacher and the same plan. What changes is only the clock.

Alberta keeps UTC-6 all year in the IANA time zone database (2026e), so there is no spring or autumn change to plan around. Our teachers work on India time, which is 11 hours 30 minutes ahead of Alberta throughout the year.

When families usually start

The most common start is the first month of the course, when the transformation unit is new and fixable. The second most common is about eight weeks before the diploma date, which still leaves room for the stage 7 review above.

Starting in the last fortnight is possible, but it changes what a teacher can do: by then the work is triage, choosing the two or three skills with the most marks attached and drilling them.

Questions from Alberta families

Math 30-1: common questions

What is Math 30-1 in Alberta?

Mathematics 30-1 is the Grade 12 Alberta math course for students heading to post-secondary programs that require calculus. Its three strands are Relations and Functions, Trigonometry, and Permutations, Combinations and Binomial Theorem, and it ends with a provincial diploma exam.

Do you grant the Math 30-1 credit?

No. We teach alongside your teen's Math 30-1 class and do not grant provincial credits. The credit and the school-awarded mark come from their own school, and the diploma exam portion is set and marked by Alberta Education and Childcare.

How much is the Math 30-1 diploma exam worth?

Alberta says the weighting of diploma exams returned to 30 percent on 1 September 2023: 70 percent of the final mark comes from course work and 30 percent from the diploma exam.

What is on the Math 30-1 diploma exam?

For 2026 to 2027 the bulletin lists 32 machine-scored questions worth 75 percent and 3 written-response questions worth 25 percent. Relations and Functions carries 53 to 58 percent, Trigonometry 27 to 33 percent, and the counting strand 14 to 18 percent.

When is the Math 30-1 diploma exam in 2026 to 2027?

Alberta schedules it at 9 AM on Tuesday 3 November 2026, Wednesday 20 January 2027, Thursday 8 April 2027, Thursday 17 June 2027 and Thursday 5 August 2027. Schools register first-time students.

Which units of Math 30-1 are hardest?

Alberta's own commentary points to logarithm equations that combine several laws, rational functions with a hole, trigonometric equations that need an identity substitution, identity proofs, and counting problems with several cases. Those are the units we spend most time on.

Can I use a calculator in Math 30-1?

Yes, an approved graphing calculator is required on the diploma exam, cleared and configured before and after. But Alberta expects several outcomes, including transformations and logarithm laws, to be met without technology, so steps still have to be shown.

What comes before and after Math 30-1?

Alberta's Guide to Education shows Math 10C, then Math 20-1, then Math 30-1 as the typical route, and says Math 30-1 is a prerequisite or corequisite for Mathematics 31.

Do you tutor Math 30-1 in Edmonton and Calgary?
