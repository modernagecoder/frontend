AI and Python classes for teens in Brampton

Where does a Brampton teenager get serious Python and AI teaching that fits around school?

Modern Age Coders teaches Brampton teens aged 13 to 18 live online: Python first, then machine learning or contest programming, either in a class of fifteen to twenty or one to one. The usual route is Python for Teens followed by AI and Machine Learning for Teens; contest students add CCC preparation and Grade 11 and 12 students the Ontario Computer Science Companion. The first class is free and booked in Eastern time.

Ask a Brampton Grade 10 student what comes after Python basics and the answer is often a shrug. Ontario has more on offer than most families realise: a university-preparation computer science pair, a college-preparation pair that leans into object-oriented programming and interfaces, a place for computer studies among the diploma’s required credits, a national programming contest with a road to the international olympiad, and a provincial skills competition with its own coding event. This page lays each of those out from the official source, explains which AI tools a teenager may use under the companies’ own terms, and walks through a real regression our students build on the City of Brampton’s published energy and emissions records for its own buildings.

Two Ontario routes

University or college preparation: the two computer studies routes open to a Brampton teen

Ontario lists four Grade 11 and 12 computer studies courses, two marked U for university preparation and two marked C for college preparation. The descriptions are the ministry’s, from its curriculum service on 22 October 2026; the notes are ours.

The diploma itself gives computer studies a place. Ontario’s graduation page for students who started Grade 9 in 2024 or later says: “Of the 17 compulsory credits, you must complete 1 from the following group:”, and computer studies is on that list beside business studies, cooperative education and others. So a computer studies course can do double duty: a credit the diploma asks for and a first real programming course. Which route suits a teen is a question of plans after Grade 12, and families should take it to the school’s guidance team, not to us. What we can say is that both routes reward the same habit, writing and testing programs often, and that a student who has written a few hundred lines of Python before Grade 11 starts either course with room to think instead of scrambling.

Choosing U or C

ICS3U and ICS3C both list no prerequisite. The difference is direction: the U pair moves towards algorithms and design, the C pair towards object-oriented programs with interfaces. We teach both kinds of student and adjust the projects.

Before Grade 11

Grade 9 math (MTH1W) already includes coding, and Grade 10 offers ICD2O. Students who arrive in our classes in Grade 9 or 10 usually reach Grade 11 already comfortable with loops, lists and functions.

Planning the order

The ICS3U and ICS3C comparison and the Ontario computer science pathway set the courses side by side for families still deciding.

We do not grant Ontario credits

Our classes run beside a student’s timetable and we do not grant Ontario credits; ICS3U, ICS3C, ICS4U and ICS4C are earned only at school. Modern Age Coders has no tie to the Ontario Ministry of Education or to any Peel school board.

City of Brampton data project

Can a straight line predict a City of Brampton building’s emissions from its energy bills?

The City of Brampton publishes the yearly electricity use, natural gas use and greenhouse gas emissions of the buildings it operates, from offices and libraries to fire stations and arenas. Our students fit a two-input linear model to it. The figures in this section are our own output, not the City’s.

Here is the program. It downloads the whole layer in one request, solves the two least squares equations by hand (no libraries, so the algebra is visible), and prints the fitted factors for each year as well as the errors in the table. We ran it on 22 October 2026 with the output shown in the table and the list below:import json, urllib.request URL = ("https://services3.arcgis.com/rl7ACuZkiFsmDA2g/arcgis/rest/services/" "Environment/FeatureServer/0/query?where=1%3D1&returnGeometry=false" "&outFields=REPORT_YEAR,NATURAL_GAS_M3,ELECTRICITY_KWH,GHG_EMISSIONS_KG" "&resultRecordCount=5000&f=json") rows = [f["attributes"] for f in json.load(urllib.request.urlopen(URL))["features"]] data = [(r["REPORT_YEAR"], r["NATURAL_GAS_M3"] or 0, r["ELECTRICITY_KWH"] or 0, r["GHG_EMISSIONS_KG"]) for r in rows] def fit(points): # least squares for kg = a * gas + b * kwh (two equations, two unknowns) gg = sum(g * g for _, g, e, _ in points) ee = sum(e * e for _, g, e, _ in points) ge = sum(g * e for _, g, e, _ in points) gy = sum(g * y for _, g, e, y in points) ey = sum(e * y for _, g, e, y in points) det = gg * ee - ge * ge return (gy * ee - ey * ge) / det, (ey * gg - gy * ge) / det def miss(points, a, b): return round(sum(abs(a * g + b * e - y) for _, g, e, y in points) / len(points)) train = [p for p in data if p[0] <= 2017] test = [p for p in data if p[0] >= 2018] a, b = fit(train) avg = sum(p[3] for p in train) / len(train) print(len(data), "rows;", len(train), "train (2013-2017),", len(test), "test (2018-2019)") print("kg per m3 of gas:", round(a, 3), " kg per kWh:", round(b, 4)) print("average miss, guessing the mean:", round(sum(abs(avg - p[3]) for p in test) / len(test))) print("average miss, two-input line:", miss(test, a, b)) for year in range(2013, 2020): ya, yb = fit([p for p in data if p[0] == year]) print(year, round(ya, 3), round(yb, 4)) a17, b17 = fit([p for p in data if p[0] == 2017]) print("trained on 2017 only:", miss(test, a17, b17))

1. Ask why it fits so well

A line that misses by about 11,000 kg on buildings that emit hundreds of thousands looks brilliant. The City’s own description explains it: “The software calculates the annual energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions”. The emissions are worked out from the energy use, so the model is mostly rediscovering a formula.

2. Read the coefficients

The gas factor barely moves, between 1.888 and 1.899 kg per cubic metre every year. The electricity factor falls from 0.076 in 2013 to about 0.03 by 2018. The data does not say why; a student should find out what factor the reporting software used before claiming a cause.

3. Watch the rule change

Because the electricity factor dropped, a line trained on 2013 to 2017 overestimates 2018 and 2019. Training on 2017 alone cuts the miss from 10,928 to 8,390 kg. Old data is not always better data when the rule behind it shifts.

Gaps a careful student flags

The item says the City submitted datasets for 2010 to 2019, yet the rows we downloaded run only from 2013 to 2019. Forty-three rows record zero electricity and 123 record zero gas; some buildings may genuinely use no gas, but a building with a gas bill and no electricity at all is more likely a missing record than a fact. A model trained without checking those rows learns from the gaps as well as the data.

The City notes that the reports are prepared under Ontario Regulation 25/23 and that “The City receives utility bills that contain energy consumption data.” Its item page adds: “Last updated: August 30, 2021 with 2018 & 2019 report values.”, so this is a historical file, ideal for practice and not a current account of any building. The dataset carries the licence label CC BY, credited to the City of Brampton; the Creative Commons deed says that licence allows use “for any purpose, even commercially.” Source: City of Brampton, Energy Consumption and Greenhouse Gas Emissions, GeoHub item. The City has not endorsed this page, our courses or our results.

Before a Brampton teen opens any AI tool for schoolwork or a project, we suggest three questions. Is the student old enough under the company’s own terms, and has a parent agreed where the terms require it? Does the school or contest allow it for this task? And could the student explain every line of the result to a teacher without the tool open? The third question matters most. In the energy project above, a tool could produce the least squares code in seconds; it would not notice that the emissions were calculated from the bills, that the electricity factor dropped by more than half, or that 43 rows show no electricity at all. A student who notices those things has learned the subject. One who pasted the code has learned to paste.

The parent decides

OpenAI’s terms give the decision to a parent or guardian until 18. We tell families in writing which lessons could involve the tool, and every lesson is complete without it.

Adult tools stay adult

Anthropic’s terms require users to be 18, so Claude has no place in a teen lesson, and our agentic coding course for adults does not accept students under 18.

School work is the student’s

We explain ideas, review reasoning and set practice. We do not write graded assignments, and we tell a student when a piece of their code looks borrowed rather than understood.

Reasoning first

Why a Brampton teen should be able to fit a line by hand before asking AI to

Because the best moment in the energy project is the suspicion that the result is too good, and that suspicion is human.

Least squares is two equations and two unknowns. A Grade 11 student who solves them in Python, with no library hiding the algebra, understands what a model is: a rule chosen to make the misses as small as possible on the data it was shown. Everything bigger in machine learning, including the systems behind chatbots, grows from that idea.

We teach in that order on purpose. Describe the problem in words, write the steps, predict the output, run it, and argue with the result. AI tools can come later, inside the age rules, as a second reader whose suggestions get checked. Teens taught this way become the people who can tell when a tool has made something up.

On CCC day there is no assistant in the room at all, which is the clearest test of whether the thinking was ever the student’s own. The longer argument is in an essay on why coding still matters in 2026.

FAQ

Brampton parents and teens ask

Answers to the questions Brampton families send most often.

Is there a Modern Age Coders centre in Brampton?

No. Teaching is live and online, so Brampton teens attend from home, in a class of fifteen to twenty or with a private teacher.

Should my teen take ICS3U or ICS3C?

That depends on plans after Grade 12, and the school's guidance team is the right place to decide. Both list no prerequisite; we support students in either course.

Do your courses count as Ontario credits?

No. We teach beside the school course and we do not grant Ontario credits. Credits for ICS3U, ICS3C, ICS4U or ICS4C come only from the school.

What age does OpenAI set for ChatGPT?

Its terms say users must be at least 13, and that anyone under 18 needs a parent's or legal guardian's permission. We only involve it where a parent has agreed.

Can a 16 year old use Claude in your classes?

No. Anthropic's consumer terms set a minimum age of 18, so Claude is not used with any student under 18.

What does it take to reach the CCO?

A top official CCC Senior result: CEMC invites about 20 Senior writers to the CCO each spring, and the CCO is written in C++ only.

Is the CCC Senior paper very hard?

It is designed to spread out strong programmers. In 2026 the Senior mean was 15.56 out of 75, and a writer needed 26, just over a third of the marks, for a Certificate of Distinction.

Can students use AI help during the CCC?

No. The grader rules bar generative AI such as CoPilot or ChatGPT and other internet use, so our contest practice is done without assistants.

When do classes run for Brampton students?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Times are confirmed in Eastern time.

Is the Brampton energy data project real?

Yes. It uses the City of Brampton's published energy and emissions file under its CC BY licence. Students download it and fit the model themselves; the City has not endorsed our courses.
