# EPQ computer science artefact mentoring

A growing number of sixth formers want their Extended Project to be something they built: an app, a website, a game, a machine learning model that classifies images or predicts something from data. Both AQA and Pearson allow it. An artefact is a recognised form of EPQ product, provided it comes with a written research report. But the specifications are just as clear about whose work it must be. At AQA the supervisor signs that the evidence is "the unaided work of the student", and the biggest assessment objective, worth 40 per cent, is the student's own development of the product. So a mentor's job is narrow and real: teach the programming and the method, on separate practice material, so the student can build and evaluate the artefact themselves. This page explains how computing artefacts fit the EPQ, where the marks sit, the authenticity rules, and how our mentoring works.

At a glance: Qualification: Level 3 Extended Project (EPQ); Size: 120 guided learning hours; Artefact report: At least 1000 words at AQA; Biggest objective: AO3 Develop and Realise, 40%; Our role: Teaching method, never the project; Classes: Group of 15 to 20 or 1-on-1; Timing: Weekdays or weekends, flexible; First session: Free. ## Can an EPQ be an app, a website or an AI model?

Yes. AQA accepts an artefact as the project product alongside a research-based written report of at least 1000 words, and Pearson runs a separate Artefact unit whose examples include creating a website. A program or a trained model fits naturally within both.

**Computing artefacts at the two boards we read (specifications as published)**

| Point | AQA EPQ 7993 | Pearson Edexcel Level 3 Extended Project |
|---|---|---|
| Size | 120 guided learning hours | 120 guided learning hours |
| Taught element | About 30 guided learning hours | About 40 guided learning hours recommended |
| Artefact route | An artefact plus a research-based report of at least 1000 words | Unit 4: Artefact, one of four units; examples include creating a website |
| Written-only route | A report of approximately 5000 words | Unit 1: Dissertation |
| Other evidence | Production Log and a presentation to a non-specialist audience | Activity log and a presentation |
| Grades | A* to E | A* to E |

The AQA wording is exact: "Where the chosen product is an artefact there must also be a research based written report of a minimum 1000 words." It adds that artefacts can be evidenced with "photographs of each stage of development as well as the final product". For a computing artefact, the equivalent is screenshots, commit history, test records and short screen recordings at each stage, which also make the development visible to the supervisor. Every project also needs its title and aims approved by the school before the main work starts: AQA requires students to "draft a title and aims of the project for formal approval by the centre".

### Why computing artefacts suit the EPQ

They produce evidence naturally: versions, tests and measurable results.

They suit students heading for computer science, engineering, data science or medicine, where a well-evaluated model shows the habits a degree will want.

### Where they go wrong

A project that becomes a coding tutorial, describing what was built rather than investigating a question.

An ambitious build that is still unfinished in the spring, leaving nothing to evaluate. Both are avoidable with a sharp title and a realistic plan.

Sources: AQA, [Level 3 Extended Project Qualification 7993](https://www.aqa.org.uk/subjects/projects/level-three/projects-7993) specification, version 1.4; Pearson, [Level 3 Extended Project](https://qualifications.pearson.com/en/qualifications/edexcel-project-qualification/level-3.html) specification, Issue 4, November 2023. Both read on 31 October 2026. OCR also offers an Extended Project; we could not read its specification for this build, so it is not described here.

## How is an EPQ artefact marked?

Against four assessment objectives. At AQA, managing the project is 20 per cent, using resources 20 per cent, developing and realising the product 40 per cent, and reviewing it 20 per cent. The artefact itself matters, but well over half the marks reward planning, research and evaluation around it.

**AQA EPQ 7993 assessment objectives and what they mean for a computing artefact (weightings from the specification; the right-hand column is ours)**

| Objective | Weighting | In a computing or AI artefact |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 Manage | 20% | A clear question, aims and a plan, monitored and adjusted in the Production Log |
| AO2 Use resources | 20% | Research into existing methods and tools, critically selected and referenced, including any use of AI tools |
| AO3 Develop and realise | 40% | The student's own design, build, testing and problem solving, with decisions taken critically |
| AO4 Review | 20% | Evaluation against the original aims, own learning, and a presentation to a non-specialist audience |

AQA describes AO3 as the ability to "Select and use a range of skills, including, where appropriate, new technologies and problem-solving, to take decisions critically and achieve planned outcomes." That is the objective most at risk when someone else helps with the build, because it is precisely the student's own skills and decisions that it measures.

The supervisor's checklist for approving a proposal asks whether the title lets the student "plan, research, analyse, evaluate and explain, rather than simply describe and narrate". For computing projects, that one test decides a great deal. "Building a chatbot" describes; "How accurately can a small model trained on a few hundred examples answer questions about one topic, and where does it fail?" investigates.

### Ask a question

Frame the artefact as a test of something: accuracy, usability, speed or fairness, measured against a stated aim.

### Plan for evaluation

Decide before building how success will be measured, and keep test data aside from the start.

### Keep the log honest

Record dead ends and changes of plan. AO1 and AO4 reward the reasoning, not a smooth story.

## Can someone help with my EPQ artefact?

Your supervisor guides you, and you can learn skills from anyone. But AQA requires the supervisor to declare that the evidence is the unaided work of the student, treats submitting work that is not your own as malpractice, and Pearson requires a signed declaration on the Candidate Record Sheet. A mentor may teach; a mentor may not build, write or fix the project.

AQA's specification sets out the position. Supervisors sign "a declaration that the evidence submitted for assessment is the unaided work of the student". Where extra help was received and is acceptable, "the supervisor should award a mark which represents the student's unaided achievement", and "If the supervisor is unable to sign the authentication statement for a particular candidate, then the student's work cannot be accepted for assessment." Students must not "submit work which is not their own" or "submit work typed or word-processed by a third person without acknowledgement", and AQA states: "These actions constitute malpractice, for which a penalty (eg disqualification from the qualification) will be applied."

Pearson's specification asks each learner to sign a declaration on the Candidate Record Sheet, warns that without confirmation "the learner will be marked as absent for the project", and on artificial intelligence says that, "Following the 2023 publication by JCQ of two guidance documents relevant to this qualification, centres are advised to support candidates with referencing and recording of any use of AI in relation to the candidates assessed work". Any AI tool used in the project must be declared and referenced as your school instructs.

**Our mentoring line, drawn from the AQA and Pearson rules**

| Inside our lessons | Outside our lessons, always |
|---|---|
| Teach Python, data handling, machine learning and web skills in lessons | Write, debug, refactor or review the code of the artefact |
| Teach evaluation methods on public practice datasets | Touch, clean or analyse the student's project data |
| Explain what makes a question analytical rather than descriptive, with our own examples | Suggest, draft or rewrite the student's title, aims or plan |
| Teach how to reference sources and record tool use | Write, edit or proofread the report, log or presentation |
| Encourage students to tell their supervisor about outside lessons | Work against a school's instructions on outside help |

### Tell your supervisor

If you take lessons alongside an EPQ, say so in a supervisor meeting and note it in your log. A sentence such as "I am taking general Python lessons outside school, on separate practice exercises" is honest and protects you.

Your supervisor can then judge, as AQA asks, what represents your unaided achievement.

### Age-appropriate tools

Online AI services set their own age rules in their terms of use. We teach with open libraries such as scikit-learn on public datasets, which need no account.

If your project uses any external AI service, check its age terms and your school's policy first.

Sources: AQA EPQ 7993 specification, version 1.4, sections on supervision, authentication and malpractice; Pearson Edexcel Level 3 Extended Project specification, Issue 4, sections on authentication and use of AI. Read on 31 October 2026.

## How do you evaluate a machine learning EPQ artefact?

With data the model has never seen, a comparison against a simple baseline, and more than one measure. Accuracy alone can flatter a model. Here is the method as we teach it, on an example of our own that is not anyone's project.

**Example (ours).** Imagine a model that labels garden photographs as "robin" or "not robin". It is tested on 120 photos kept aside from training: 48 contain a robin and 72 do not. The model finds 40 of the 48 robins (true positives), misses 8 (false negatives), wrongly labels 10 other photos as robins (false positives), and correctly rejects 62 (true negatives).

**Our worked evaluation for the robin example**

| Measure | Calculation | Result | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | (40 + 62) &divide; 120 | 85% | Share of all photos labelled correctly |
| Precision | 40 &divide; (40 + 10) | 80% | When it says robin, how often it is right |
| Recall | 40 &divide; (40 + 8) | About 83% | Of the real robins, how many it found |
| Baseline | Always answer "not robin": 72 &divide; 120 | 60% | The score a model with no skill would get |

The baseline is the line most EPQ evaluations miss. A model at 85 per cent sounds strong until you notice that guessing "not robin" every time scores 60. The honest claim is that the model adds 25 percentage points over doing nothing, finds about five robins in six, and is wrong one time in five when it says robin. That is an evaluation an examiner can follow, and it leads naturally into the review: which photos fooled it, and why?

**Practice code (ours) on a public toy dataset** (Python)
```python
# Practice only (ours): evaluate a classifier on a public toy dataset,
# never on the student's own EPQ data or code.
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix, accuracy_score

X, y = load_iris(return_X_y=True)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=1, stratify=y)

model = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=3, random_state=1).fit(X_train, y_train)
pred = model.predict(X_test)
print(confusion_matrix(y_test, pred))
print("accuracy:", round(accuracy_score(y_test, pred), 3))
```
We teach evaluation on public datasets such as this one. Students then apply the method to their own project themselves.

1. **Split first.** Put test data aside before any training, and never tune the model on it.
2. **Set a baseline.** The simplest possible rule, measured on the same test data.
3. **Report several measures.** Accuracy, precision and recall, or the measures that fit the question.
4. **Look at failures.** Examine the cases the model got wrong; they are the heart of the review.
5. **Tie back to the aims.** Did the artefact achieve what the approved aims said, and what would you change?

**Try this (ours, with answers).** A model is tested on 200 emails, 30 of them spam. It flags 25 emails, of which 20 are spam. (1) What is its precision? (2) Its recall? (3) What accuracy would "never flag anything" achieve? **Answers:** (1) 20 &divide; 25 = 80 per cent; (2) 20 &divide; 30, about 67 per cent; (3) 170 &divide; 200 = 85 per cent, which is why accuracy alone would mislead here.

## When should I start learning the skills for a computing EPQ?

Before the build starts, ideally before the proposal is approved. Learning a language and the evaluation method while also running the project leaves little time for the planning and review that carry 40 per cent of the marks at AQA.

**A typical skills timeline alongside a one-year EPQ (ours; your school sets the real deadlines)**

| Stage | Project work (student and supervisor) | Skills lessons (with us, separate practice) |
|---|---|---|
| Before proposal | Choosing an area, reading around it | Python foundations, data files, simple models or web basics |
| Proposal and approval | Title and aims approved by the school | Evaluation method and baselines on practice datasets |
| Research | Sources, existing tools and methods, referenced | Reading documentation and technical papers, referencing practice |
| Build | The student designs, builds and tests the artefact | Topic lessons only, on unrelated exercises |
| Review and presentation | Evaluation, report, presentation to a non-specialist audience | Explaining technical ideas plainly, practised on our own topics |

## How does your EPQ artefact mentoring work?

It is skills teaching, timed around the project and kept apart from it. Students learn on our exercises and public datasets, then do their own project work with their school supervisor.

1. **A free first session.** We talk about the kind of artefact you are considering and where your programming stands, and agree a skills plan. We do not look at project documents.
2. **Foundations.** Python, data handling and testing, or web development, depending on the artefact type.

5. **Boundaries throughout.** No project code, data, title, report or slides are ever shared with or produced by us.

## What students ask about computing and AI EPQs

### Can my EPQ be a coding project?

Yes. AQA accepts an artefact as the product alongside a research-based written report of at least 1000 words, and Pearson has an Artefact unit whose examples include creating a website. An app, game or machine learning model fits either.

### How long is the written report for an artefact EPQ?

At AQA, an artefact needs a research-based written report of a minimum of 1000 words. A project that is only a written report should be approximately 5000 words.

### How is an EPQ marked?

At AQA against four objectives: Manage 20 per cent, Use Resources 20 per cent, Develop and Realise 40 per cent, and Review 20 per cent. Grades run from A* to E.

### What are good EPQ ideas for computer science?

Strong ideas ask a question the artefact can test, such as how accurately a small model classifies something, or whether an app design improves a task for real users. Your supervisor must approve the title and aims, so we never suggest yours.

### Can a tutor help with my EPQ?

A tutor can teach general skills on separate practice material. AQA requires the supervisor to declare the evidence is the unaided work of the student, and treats submitting work that is not your own as malpractice, so nobody else should write, build or edit the project.

### Can I use AI tools in my EPQ?

Only as your school allows, and any use must be recorded and referenced. Pearson advises centres to support candidates with referencing and recording of any use of AI, following JCQ guidance published in 2023.

### How many hours is an EPQ?

Both AQA and Pearson specify 120 guided learning hours, including a taught skills element: about 30 hours at AQA and about 40 recommended at Pearson.

### Can my EPQ overlap with my A level Computer Science project?

No. AQA says no work assessed for the EPQ may be submitted for another accredited qualification, so the two projects must be different pieces of work.
