# Continental Math League practice and preparation

The League&rsquo;s own history begins: "In 1980 we offered the Continental Mathematics League" to the nation&rsquo;s public and private schools. Students in grades 4 to 9 sit five meets a year, each with six questions in 30 minutes, in one of two divisions defined by how much reading comprehension the questions demand. That makes CML a little different from other contests: careful reading counts as much as arithmetic. This page sets out the 2026 to 2027 League from the League&rsquo;s own site as it read on 31 October 2026, then sets out the read, restate and solve routine our teachers use, worked through on three examples and a ten-problem set we invented.

## What is the Continental Math League and how do meets work?

CML is a league of short meets held at school. Students in grades 2 and 3 sit three meets, students in grades 4 to 9 sit five, and each meet has six questions with a 30 minute limit. A separate Calculus League has four meets of eight questions in 40 minutes.

**The Continental Mathematics League for 2026 to 2027 (cmleague.com League description and FAQ, read 31 October 2026)**

| Level | Meets | Questions per meet | Time per meet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grades 2 and 3 | 3 | 6 | 30 minutes |
| Grades 4 to 9, Euclidean or Pythagorean | 5 | 6 | 30 minutes |
| Calculus League | 4 | 8 | 40 minutes |

**Meet and contest rules (cmleague.com, read 31 October 2026)**

| Rule | What the League says |
|---|---|
| Tools | "Except for the Calculus League, paper and pencil are to be used to solve the questions." Calculus may use calculators approved by ETS |
| Who sits | No restriction on how many students take part |
| Working | Each student works independently within the time limit |
| Running it | The school proctors and grades each meet with answer keys and step-by-step solutions from CML |
| Team score | The top 6 scores at each meet |
| Changing names | Different students can be listed from meet to meet; cumulative scores add up across meets |
| Grade level | Students take part at or above their present grade level |
| Dates | Held on the scheduled day or as close to it as possible |

### Five minutes a question

Six questions in 30 minutes is 5 minutes each, by our arithmetic, which is more time per question than most contests give. The League&rsquo;s design leaves room to read slowly, and its questions call on reading comprehension, so that time is meant to be used.

For grades 4 to 9, five meets of six questions make 30 questions across the season, again our arithmetic. Each one is worth reviewing.

### A team of anyone

Because any number of students can sit and only the top 6 scores count, a school can let a whole class try a meet without harming the team. The FAQ gives a grade 5 example where the six names listed can change at every meet.

For a student, that means CML is low stakes: a chance to try hard problems in class time, not a test that decides anything.

## What is the difference between the Euclidean and Pythagorean divisions?

Both divisions serve grades 4 to 9 and expect grade-level computation. The difference is reading: Euclidean students need average reading comprehension and analytical reasoning, while Pythagorean students need, in the League&rsquo;s words, "above-average reading comprehension and analytical reasoning".

**The two CML divisions (cmleague.com League description and FAQ, read 31 October 2026)**

|  | Euclidean Division | Pythagorean Division |
|---|---|---|
| Grades | 4 to 9 | 4 to 9 |
| Reading comprehension | Average | Above average |
| Analytical reasoning | Average | Above average |
| Computation | Appropriate to grade level | Appropriate to grade level |
| Entry | A team enters one division only | A team enters one division only |
| Students | No student in both divisions | No student in both divisions |

### Which suits my child?

The League&rsquo;s own words point to a test: how does your child handle long, wordy problems? A strong calculator of numbers who rushes through text may score better in Euclidean, while a careful reader who enjoys puzzles fits Pythagorean.

Schools decide team entry, and the FAQ notes that many schools enter one team in each division, in the same or a different grade.

### Moving up a level

CML says it anticipates each student will participate at or above their present grade level, and gives the example that "both 5th and 6th graders may participate at the 6th grade level."

So a strong grade 5 student might sit the grade 6 meets. Ask the teacher who runs your school&rsquo;s team which level and division your child is on before practicing.

Source: Continental Mathematics League [FAQ](https://www.cmleague.com/faq/) and League page, read 31 October 2026.

## How should a student read a CML word problem?

Since reading comprehension is built into the divisions, our method starts with the words: read, restate in your own words, then solve, then answer the exact question asked. The method and its steps are ours.

1. Read once for the story. Who is doing what? Do not touch the numbers yet.
2. Read again for the question. Underline what is being asked: how many, how much more, which day, what fraction.
3. Restate it in one sentence of your own. "There are some apples; a third were eaten and 10 are left; find the start." If you cannot restate it, read again.
4. Solve with a picture or a short list. Bars, a table or a number line, whichever fits.
5. Answer the underlined question. Check units and whether the answer is a sensible size for the story.

Worked example 1 (ours, grade 4): what is really asked. A basket held some apples. Lucy ate 2 and gave away 3. Then she bought twice as many as she had left, and now has 30. How many apples were in the basket at first? Restate: "after eating and giving away, some number remained; that number tripled to 30." Buying twice as many as she had means she now has three times as many, so before buying she had 10. Add back the 5 she ate or gave away: 15 at first. The phrase "twice as many as she had left" is where readers go wrong, treating 30 as only double.

Worked example 2 (ours, grade 5): time, read carefully. A movie started at 6:45 pm and ended 1 hour 55 minutes later. Sam left 30 minutes before the end. How long did Sam watch? Restate: "the movie lasted 115 minutes; Sam missed the last 30." So Sam watched 85 minutes, or 1 hour 25 minutes. The clock times are a distraction; the question never asks when anything ended. Readers who compute 8:40 pm first have done extra work that may hide a slip.

Worked example 3 (ours, grade 6): two conditions. I am thinking of two consecutive odd numbers. Their product is 143. What is their sum? Restate: "two odd numbers in a row multiply to 143; add them." 143 is close to 12 x 12 = 144, so try the odd numbers on either side of 12: 11 x 13 = 143. The sum is 24. Estimating with a square nearby is the key move, and it only appears once the student has restated the two conditions.

### Why restating works

Most lost points in reading-heavy contests come from solving a different problem from the one asked. Restating in one sentence forces the student to decide what the problem is before spending any time on numbers.

Five minutes a question is enough time to do this properly. Students who skip it to save time usually lose more than they save.

### From Euclidean to Pythagorean

A student moving to the Pythagorean division meets longer sentences and more conditions. The method does not change; the restatement just gets more careful.

We practice by giving the same math in a short and a long wording, so students learn that the words, not the numbers, set the difficulty.

## Ten original practice problems for CML students in grades 4 to 6

Written by our teachers, reading-heavy on purpose. They are not CML questions and not drawn from any CML sample or book. Try six in 30 minutes.

1. Nora has 3 times as many stamps as Ivan. If Nora gives Ivan 10 stamps, they have the same number. How many stamps does Ivan have now, before the gift?
2. A train leaves at 9:50 am and arrives at 1:15 pm. How many minutes is the trip?
3. The product of my two digits is 12 and I am a two-digit number greater than 50. What number am I?
4. A class of 28 students voted. Twice as many voted for soccer as for chess, and the rest, 7 students, voted for art. How many voted for soccer?
5. A square and an equilateral triangle each have a perimeter of 24 inches. How much longer is a side of the triangle than a side of the square?
6. Pencils cost 3 for 1 dollar. How many dollars do 18 pencils cost?
7. What is the smallest number that can be divided evenly by 4, 6 and 10?
8. A book has 120 pages. Mei read one third of it on Monday and one quarter of the rest on Tuesday. How many pages are left?
9. Every day a plant doubles in height. On day 10 it is 64 cm tall. On which day was it 8 cm tall?
10. How many minutes are in one fifth of a day?

**Answer key with the restated question (ours; checked in Python)**

| No. | Answer | Restated, then solved |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 | The difference is 20 and is 2 of Ivan&rsquo;s units, so Ivan has 10 and Nora 30 |
| 2 | 205 minutes | 9:50 to 12:50 is 180, then 25 more |
| 3 | 62 | Digit pairs with product 12: 26, 34, 43, 62; only 62 is above 50 |
| 4 | 14 | 21 voted soccer or chess, in 3 units of 7; soccer is 2 units |
| 5 | 2 inches | Triangle sides 8, square sides 6 |
| 6 | 6 dollars | Six groups of 3 |
| 7 | 60 | The least common multiple of 4, 6 and 10 |
| 8 | 60 pages | 40 read Monday, leaving 80; 20 read Tuesday |
| 9 | Day 7 | Halve back from day 10: 32, 16, 8 |
| 10 | 288 minutes | 1,440 minutes in a day, divided by 5 |

### Where readers slip

Problem 1 asks for Ivan&rsquo;s stamps before the gift; students who solve and then add 10 report 20. Problem 8 says "of the rest", not of the book, so a quarter of 80, not of 120.

Problem 3 has two conditions. Students who stop at the first number with digit product 12 answer 26.

## Four stages of a careful reader of math

Our own description of growth through a CML season. It is not a League measure.

**From quick reader to careful solver (our own four steps)**

| Step | Observed in class |
|---|---|
| 1. Slows down | Reads the whole problem before writing |
| 2. Restates | Says the question in their own words |
| 3. Organises | Uses a picture, list or table without prompting |
| 4. Answers exactly | Gives the quantity asked, in the right units |

### For parents

At home, ask your child to tell you a word problem as a story before solving it. It builds stage 2 quickly.
Cumulative scores across five meets show growth better than any single meet.

### For students

Underline the question. Then check, before you write the answer, that it answers the underlined words.
You have five minutes a question. Spend one of them reading.

## Continental Math League: what families ask

### What is the Continental Mathematics League?

It is a school math league that the organiser says began in 1980. Grades 2 and 3 sit three meets and grades 4 to 9 sit five, each with six questions in 30 minutes; there is also a Calculus League.

### What is the difference between Euclidean and Pythagorean in CML?

Both divisions are for grades 4 to 9. The Euclidean division calls for average reading comprehension and analytical reasoning, the Pythagorean for above-average, and no student may be in both.

### When are the Continental Math League meets in 2026 to 2027?

For grades 4 to 9 the League lists 11/5, 12/3, 1/7, 2/4 and 3/4 for the 2026 to 2027 year, which we read as November 5, 2026 to March 4, 2027. Grades 2 and 3 meet on 1/7, 2/4 and 3/4.

### Can you use a calculator in the Continental Math League?

No, except in the Calculus League. The League says paper and pencil are used for all other meets, and calculators approved by ETS may be used for calculus.

### How is a CML team score worked out?

The team score is the sum of the top 6 student scores at each meet. Any number of students can take part, and the names listed can change from meet to meet.

### Where can I find Continental Math League practice problems?

The League posts a sample test for every grade on its site and sells books of past League problems with solutions, which parents may order. The meets themselves are for schools only.

### Can my child join if the school does not take part?

Yes, through what the League calls a Home School entry, which simply means a student taking part when their school does not. Register an account and email the League to set it up.

### Can a 5th grader sit the 6th grade meets?

Yes. CML says students take part at or above their present grade level and gives the example that 5th and 6th graders may both sit the 6th grade level.

### What days and times are your classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.
