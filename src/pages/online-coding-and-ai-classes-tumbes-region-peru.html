Online coding and AI classes in the Tumbes region

Tumbes is Peru's north-western corner: three provinces between the Pacific and the Ecuadorian border, most of their people in or near the capital. Most people here are online, yet the 2025 school evaluation shows pupils struggling with maths. That shapes our advice. Our classes are live, online and taught in English, and for Tumbes learners we pair programming with the reasoning behind it. The region

How big is the Tumbes region, and where do people live?

Small by Peruvian standards. The 2025 census counted 256,691 people in the department of Tumbes, and more than two thirds of them in the province of Tumbes itself.

INEI, Censos Nacionales 2025, tabulated demographic indicators, Cuadro N° 1, people counted plus those INEI estimates were missed, reference day 15 September 2025. The department figure comes from INEI's own table; we do not total the provinces.

Districts in 2025

The district of Tumbes had 114,011 residents in 2025. Corrales had 26,585, Zarumilla 24,909, Aguas Verdes on the border 19,374, Zorritos 14,029 and La Cruz 10,862.

Among the people actually enumerated in the department (250,997), 28,166 were between 6 and 11 and 29,574 between 12 and 17: the ages of primaria and secundaria.

Towns in 2017

INEI's 2017 table of department capitals gives Tumbes city 96,946 people. The same year's list of centros poblados records Zarumilla town at 21,409, San Pedro de los Incas in Corrales at 17,883, La Curva in Aguas Verdes at 13,739 and Zorritos at 7,363.

Those are towns in 2017 and districts in 2025, two different measures eight years apart, so we set them side by side without drawing a growth figure from them.

School numbers are rising, unlike in several Andean regions. INEI's compendium shows public secundaria enrolment in Tumbes climbing from 14.7 thousand in 2015 to 21.4 thousand in 2025, and public primaria from 23.0 thousand to 26.4 thousand. The department counted 4,953 teachers in 2025.

Maths first

Why should Tumbes learners study maths reasoning alongside code?

Because programming is applied reasoning, and the region's 2025 results show that reasoning is where pupils need the most support.

What the numbers say

In 2025 about one Tumbes fourth-grader in six reached the expected level in maths, against nearly one in three across Peru. By second year of secundaria, 5.8 per cent did, less than half the national 12.7 per cent.

These are census-style evaluations of whole year groups, published by MINEDU, not samples. They describe a region, not any one child.

What we do about it

Our Tumbes learners write programs that are maths in action: a loop that counts, a condition that compares, a function that averages and then checks its own answer. Each lesson asks the learner to predict what the code will print before running it.

For pupils who want more, we add structured maths practice in English. Our page on razonamiento matemático online explains the approach for Spanish-speaking parents.

Reading is a strength worth building on. In December 2023 the Gobierno Regional de Tumbes reported that three of its pupils took first place nationally in MINEDU's reading-comprehension contest "El Perú Lee", in which, according to the note, 115,000 pupils from public and private schools took part. A child who reads carefully in one language has a head start in reading code, and English, carefully.

The good news

In 2025 a larger share of Tumbes households had internet at home than across Peru as a whole, 61.3 per cent against 56.8, and 86.3 per cent of residents from age six used the internet.

For a live class that matters: a steady home connection is usually there.

The catch

Computers have not followed. The share of Tumbes households with one was 34.0 per cent in 2015, fell to 20.9 in 2020, and stood at 29.1 in 2025, below where it started.

Phones carry most of the region's internet use. A phone can show a lesson, but a learner writing Python needs a keyboard and a screen big enough for code and output side by side.

If there is one laptop in the house, it is enough. Younger children can do block coding on a tablet; from roughly ten we ask for a laptop or desktop, shared if necessary. The free class checks what you have before anyone pays.

A Tumbes data project

How do you draw a chart of numbers that range from tiny to huge?

The Gobierno Regional de Tumbes publishes every payment it records as open data. We never look at what any payment was for. We use the amounts only as numbers, to teach one of the most useful ideas in data work: some data need bins that grow by powers of ten.

The dataset

The regional government publishes "Gastos según fuentes financiamiento del Gobierno Regional de Tumbes - [Gore Tumbes]" on datosabiertos.gob.pe under the Open Data Commons Attribution License (ODC-By). It was released on 30 April 2024 and last modified on 15 June 2026.

Our copy, gastos-gore-tumbes.csv, has 19,624 rows, one per payment record, and no personal names. Each row names a funding source; the largest groups are canon and royalties (7,010 rows), ordinary resources (6,695) and the regional compensation fund FONCOR (4,925).

A date to notice

Every row carries the same cut-off date, 29 April 2023, yet the request dates run from 16 December 2022 to 7 April 2024. A file can carry a label that its own contents contradict, so we describe what is in it rather than trusting the label.

The column we use is IMPORTE, the amount. The smallest has one digit before the decimal point and the largest has seven. That spread is the whole problem.

Version 1 divides the range from zero to the largest amount into ten equal slices. The result is useless as a picture: 19,088 of 19,624 rows, about 97 per cent, land in the first slice, and seven of the ten bars are empty or nearly so. One or two very large values stretched the scale until everything else was squashed into a single bar. When numbers vary by factors of ten, bin them by order of magnitude instead: one-digit amounts together, two-digit amounts together, and so on.

Now the shape appears: most payments have four digits, with smaller groups either side. But look at the second and third columns. The logarithm method puts 3,996 rows in the three-digit bin and the digit-counting method 3,902. The 94 rows that differ are all amounts of exactly one thousand. math.log(1000, 10) is computed by dividing two natural logarithms, and the tiny rounding error leaves it just under 3, so int() drops it to 2. Counting digits, or using math.log10, which returns exactly 3.0 for 1000, files them correctly. The bars use the digit counts.

  1. Under 8, with coins and notes. Sort play money into piles by how many digits it shows. Which pile is tallest?
  2. Blocks, around 8 to 10. Make a list of twenty numbers between 3 and 30,000. Sort them into bins by digit count, and draw the bars.
  3. First Python, around 10 to 12. Print len(str(n)) for 7, 70, 700 and 7000. Predict each answer first.
  4. Ages 13 and over. Find every whole number from 1 to 10 million where int(math.log(n, 10)) is wrong. Which numbers are they, and why?
  5. Going further. Draw the same power-of-ten bars separately for each funding source. Do the shapes differ?

Schools and training

Is programming taught in Tumbes schools?

No, not as its own subject. Tumbes follows the national curriculum, where technology is a skill spread across all areas.

How school is organised

Peru's General Education Law sets six years of primaria and five of secundaria. Grade follows age on 31 March: six for 1° de primaria, twelve for 1° de secundaria, sixteen for the final year.

Competency 28 of the national curriculum, on working in virtual environments generated by information technology, belongs to every area rather than to one. Of the 35 weekly hours in a regular secundaria timetable, Matemática gets four, and no hour is set aside for programming.

In the region

In August 2025 the regional government announced the reopening of the public teacher-training institute "José Antonio Encinas" in Tumbes after two years of suspension, once MINEDU authorised it.

In August 2023 the regional government also reported progress on the national Territorios Digitales Confiables project, including a "Laboratorio Smart". Neither note mentions programming for school pupils.

Tumbes pupils can enter the same contests as pupils anywhere in Peru. ONEM, MINEDU's maths olympiad, holds its first stage inside schools, and its 2026 edition drew more than 1.7 million secundaria pupils; UTEC's computing olympiads, OPI among them, are open to every secundaria pupil without charge. We are not linked to MINEDU, UTEC, the regional government or any contest, and all our practice problems are written by us. For olympiad-style preparation, see maths olympiad coaching in Peru.

When would a Tumbes learner have class?

When you want. Tumbes uses Peru time, which never changes, and the mentor in India sets the lesson around your day.

Peru keeps UTC minus 5 hours throughout the year with no summer time (tz database zone America/Lima). India keeps UTC plus 5 hours 30 minutes. The difference is always 10 hours 30 minutes, India ahead.

You pick the hour in Tumbes time when you book, and it stays put all year because neither country moves its clocks. Peru's 2026 school year began on 16 March 2026, when 53,631 schools opened their doors to 6,225,000 pupils; the long January to March break is a natural moment for school children to begin, and adults can begin in any week.

Tumbes asks

Questions from Tumbes families about online coding and AI classes

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. All lessons are live online lessons in English, with no Spanish-language groups. Mentors talk slowly, adjust to the learner's pace and accent, and if a learner answers in Spanish they help them say it in English; the teaching stays in English throughout.

My child finds maths hard. Is coding still a good idea?

Often it helps. Code makes each step of a calculation visible, and a program that gives a wrong answer shows exactly where the thinking slipped. We start slowly and add maths practice where it is needed.

Can we join from Zarumilla, Aguas Verdes or Zorritos?

Yes. Every lesson is online, so any town in the region works, as long as the connection holds steady at the chosen hour.

Is a phone enough?

For watching, yes; for programming, no. Typing code needs a keyboard. A shared family laptop is fine, and small children can begin block coding on a tablet.

What time are the classes?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India is always 10 hours 30 minutes ahead of Tumbes; 07:00 in Tumbes is 17:30 in India.

Does school in Tumbes teach programming?

Not as a separate subject. The national curriculum treats digital skills as part of every area, and the standard secundaria week has no programming hours.

Are the classes useful for students at institutes or university?

Yes. Python and data analysis help in engineering, accounting, education and business courses. We teach alongside your studies; your institute or university sets your marks.

Do you cover AI?

Yes, for all ages at the right level. Teenagers and adults see how models learn and where they go wrong; children explore the ideas through games, and no child under 13 uses an adult AI account with us.

How are fees paid?

By card each month, in US dollars, converted to soles by your bank. Someone browsing from India sees rupees instead. The trial lesson is free.

Will there be a certificate?

Courses that include one end with our own certificate of completion. It is not a Peruvian qualification and is not issued or approved by MINEDU, a school or a university.
