### Reading the table

Age matters more than grade for us. A child born in April may be the youngest in their grade and a child born in March the oldest, so two classmates can be almost a year apart. We place children by age and by what they can already do, after a free first class.

The jump from blocks to typing usually comes in 4° or 5° de primaria. It depends on reading and typing more than on cleverness: a child who reads fluently and finds the keys without hunting is ready.

### What the school week holds

The CNEB plan of studies for primaria has 30 teaching periods a week of 45 minutes. Matemática gets 5 in grades 1 and 2 and 4 from grade 3; Inglés gets 2 in the first two grades and 3 from 3° onwards, which is 90 and then 135 minutes a week by our arithmetic.

None of those periods is for computing. The CNEB treats digital skills as a competency shared by every area, number 28, rather than a subject, so a child in a typical primaria never writes a program in class. Bilingual schools in and around San Borja set their own timetables; what each teaches is for the school to say.

## Can a six-year-old really follow a live coding class in English?

Usually, yes, if the class is built for six-year-olds. That means short tasks, a lot of pointing at the screen, very few words, and a mentor who waits.

1. **Hello and a warm-up, five minutes.** The mentor greets each child by name and asks one simple question: "What colour is your cat today?" Children answer however they can; one English word is a good start.
2. **Show, then say, ten minutes.** The mentor drags a block on the shared screen and says only "move", "turn", "say". Children copy it on their own screen. The words come with the action, never alone.
3. **Make it yours, fifteen minutes.** Each child changes something: a colour, a sound, how far the cat moves. The mentor visits screens one by one and asks "What happens if...?"
4. **Show the group, five minutes.** A child or two shows their project. Others clap. Speaking in front of others in English starts with a single sentence.

### If English is new at home

Many San Borja children hear English at school, some barely at all. For a six-year-old with very little English we usually suggest starting one to one: the mentor can slow down as much as needed, use gesture and repetition freely, and nobody else is waiting.

A parent sitting nearby for the first few classes helps too, not to translate, but to make the screen feel safe. After a month, most children prefer to be left alone with their mentor.

### If English is already strong

Children at bilingual schools usually treat the class like any other lesson in English. For them the challenge is the thinking, not the language, and the mentor moves faster through the projects.

Either way, the classes are taught only in English. The mentor understands a Spanish answer and offers the English for it, but does not switch languages. That consistency is what makes the English stick.

## Why choose an online class for a young child in San Borja?

Because for a child of six to nine, the journey is often the hardest part of an after-school activity, and a live class at home removes it completely.

### No journey, no rush

We do not quote traffic times; San Borja parents know the afternoon roads better than any table could. What matters is that a forty-five minute class at home costs forty-five minutes, not an afternoon.

Younger children are also tired after school. A class that starts five minutes after a snack, at a familiar table, gets a fresher child than one that starts after a car ride.

### Still a real class

Online does not mean a video. The mentor sees each child's screen, speaks to each child by name, and notices when someone is lost. Children in the group see and hear each other too.

What you need: a laptop or desktop with a mouse, a camera and a microphone, and a steady connection. A tablet or phone is not enough for building programs. INEI found that 68.2% of Peruvian children of 6 to 11 used the internet in the second quarter of 2026, the lowest share of any school-age group, so for many children this will be their first serious use of a computer.

## What does a lesson look like for the oldest primaria children? Wrapping a story onto a card

This lesson is for children of about 10 to 12 who already type Python. They write a short story and want it to fit on a narrow card, ten letters wide. Splitting text into lines sounds easy, and teaches a surprising amount about counting carefully.

### The task

The story is one sentence about a llama and some cactuses. The child writes a function, wrap(text, width), that takes words one at a time and adds each to the current line if it fits, or starts a new line if it does not. This "fill the line, then move on" method is called greedy wrapping.

Before running it, the child predicts the lines on paper. That prediction is the most important part of the lesson.

### Three surprises

Version 1 prints bars around each line so we can see its edges. The first line starts with a space. The second line, "jumped over", is 11 letters long on a card only 10 wide. And "extraordinarily" sticks out at 15.

The space at the start comes from adding " " before every word, even the first. The line of 11 comes from **forgetting to count the space** between words. The long word is a different problem: no rule can fit 15 letters on a line of 10.

**Version 1: almost a word wrapper** (Python)
```python
story = "The llama jumped over seventeen extraordinarily tall cactuses"

def wrap(text, width):
    lines = []
    line = ""
    for word in text.split():
        if len(line) + len(word) <= width:
            line = line + " " + word
        else:
            lines.append(line)
            line = word
    lines.append(line)
    return lines

for line in wrap(story, 10):
    print("|" + line + "|", len(line))
```
It prints | The llama| 10, |jumped over| 11, |seventeen| 9, |extraordinarily| 15, |tall| 4 and |cactuses| 8.

**Version 2: count the space, then compare with textwrap** (Python)
```python
import textwrap

story = "The llama jumped over seventeen extraordinarily tall cactuses"

def wrap(text, width):
    lines = []
    line = ""
    for word in text.split():
        if line == "":
            line = word
        elif len(line) + 1 + len(word) <= width:
            line = line + " " + word
        else:
            lines.append(line)
            line = word
    if line:
        lines.append(line)
    return lines

for line in wrap(story, 10):
    print("|" + line.ljust(10) + "|", len(line))
print(textwrap.wrap(story, 10))
```
It prints |The llama | 9, |jumped | 6, |over | 4, |seventeen | 9, |extraordinarily| 15, |tall | 4, |cactuses | 8, and then ['The llama', 'jumped', 'over', 'seventeen', 'extraordin', 'arily tall', 'cactuses'].

Version 2 starts each line with the first word on its own and adds 1 for the space before every later word. Now no ordinary line goes past 10, and "jumped" and "over" sit on separate lines because together they need 11. The long word still sticks out, and Version 2 keeps it whole on purpose.

The last line uses Python's own textwrap module for comparison. It makes a different choice: it cuts the long word into "extraordin" and "arily", and then fits "arily tall" on one line. Neither answer is wrong. The child has to decide what a story card should do, and that decision, explained in a sentence, is what we are really teaching.

1. **Ages 6 to 7, with paper strips.** Write a sentence on squared paper, one letter per square, and cut it into strips five squares wide without cutting any word. What do you do with a word of seven letters?
2. **Ages 8 to 9, in Scratch.** Make a sprite say a long sentence in a speech bubble. Then split the sentence into two "say" blocks so it reads better. Where did you choose to split, and why?
3. **Ages 10 to 12, in Python.** Predict the output of Version 1 on paper before running it. Then change the width to 12 and predict again.
4. **Going further.** Add a hyphen when a long word is cut, as books do. Check that no line, hyphen included, is longer than the width.

## Coding classes in San Borja: questions and answers

### Are the classes in Spanish?

No. Every class is live, online and taught only in English. With young children the mentor uses very simple words, gestures and the screen itself, so a child can follow before they understand every word. A child may answer in Spanish; the mentor understands and replies in English.

### Is six too young to start?

No, for the right kind of class. At 6, children solve reasoning puzzles and drag Scratch blocks; they do not type code. Sessions are short and active. Most six-year-olds in 1° de primaria manage well, especially one to one at first.

### When should my child move from blocks to Python?

Usually around 9, in 4° de primaria, once they read fluently and type without hunting for keys. The mentor suggests the move when a child is ready, not on a fixed date.

### When are classes in San Borja time?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India is ten and a half hours ahead of Peru all year, so 10:30 in San Borja is 21:00 in India.

### Does my child need a computer?

Yes: a laptop or desktop with a mouse, a camera and a microphone. Tablets and phones are fine for watching but not for building programs. A shared family computer works if the hour is planned around it.

### Does primary school in Peru teach coding?

Not as a subject. The CNEB plan of studies for primaria has no computing period; digital skills are a competency shared across subjects. Some private and bilingual schools add their own computing classes.

### Will my child use AI chatbots?

No adult chatbot accounts. Claude is for users aged 18 and over, and ChatGPT requires a parent's permission under 18. Children learn how AI works through our own child-safe projects and learn to reason before using any tool.

### Do you have a centre in San Borja?

No. We have no premises in San Borja or anywhere in Peru, and we are not connected with the municipality, MINEDU or any school. All classes are live online with mentors in India. Children who complete a course receive our certificate of completion.

### How are fees paid?

Monthly, by card, in US dollars. A card in soles is converted by your bank, so the figure can vary slightly from month to month. The first class is free and needs no card.
