Online coding and AI classes in the Loreto region

Most of Loreto is reached by river or by air, which makes a class that travels down a cable or through a phone mast more useful here than almost anywhere else in Peru. This page gives the census picture, shows how quickly Loreto homes have come online since 2015 and how far they still have to go, says honestly what a live class needs from a connection, and teaches with soil records written by hand in the forest near Iquitos.

People and places

How many people live in Loreto, and where?

The 2025 national census counted 1,032,771 people in the department of Loreto. They are spread across eight provinces, and Iquitos, in the province of Maynas, is the regional capital.

The towns families name

Iquitos is by far the biggest city. INEI's 2017 census counted 377,609 people in it as one urban area, which takes in the districts around the old centre. 2025 figures for cities have not been released yet.

Beyond Iquitos, INEI's 2017 directory of populated centres gives Yurimaguas 62,903, Requena 22,875, Nauta 19,551, Contamana 17,429 and Caballococha 9,051. Each is a single centre counted on its own, and we print them exactly as listed.

School-age Loreto

The census counted 1,011,102 people in Loreto in person. Of those, 140,975 were 6 to 11 years old and 144,813 were 12 to 17. Loreto is a young region: those two age groups hold a larger share of its people than they do in Peru as a whole, by our reading of the same table.

For a family in Caballococha or Contamana the nearest academy may be days away by boat. A live class arrives at the same moment everywhere, once the connection is there.

In the colegio

Is programming taught in Loreto's schools?

Not as a subject of its own. Loreto's colegios teach the national curriculum, the CNEB, which treats digital skills as something every area should build and leaves no timetabled hour for programming.

Almost everyone is in a public school

In Loreto, private schools are a small slice of enrolment: about eight thousand primaria pupils against more than 164 thousand in public schools. Public secundaria enrolment has grown fast, from 70.0 thousand in 2015 to 125.0 thousand in 2025, and in 2025 some 55.0 thousand secundaria pupils studied in rural schools.

Across Peru, the regular secundaria week has 35 hours and none of them is labelled programming. The curriculum's one technology competency, number 28, is transversal: teachers of every area are asked to weave it in.

What Loreto pupils already do

The regional education authority, the GREL, reported in October 2023 that the regional stage of the national school science fair, Eureka, took place in Iquitos with three areas including technological solutions, and that schools from four UGELs built robotic models for that year's national school arts games.

In September 2023 it described a regional maths contest, COREMAT 2023, with 37 schools taking part, and a partnership in which Fundación Telefónica Perú supplied digital resources to 14 selected schools. Interest is real; regular coding lessons are what is missing.

At national level, MINEDU's Python programme, Fábrica de Programadores, started in July 2025 with 1,000 pupils in the last two years of secundaria in Lima Norte. In September 2026 the ministry announced a plan to bring satellite internet to 55,000 public schools. Both are steps; neither puts a programming teacher in a Loreto classroom this year.

Far more than ten years ago, and still fewer than the national average. INEI's household survey shows Loreto catching up quickly from a very low starting point.

Reading the numbers fairly

In 2015 fewer than ten Loreto homes in every hundred had an internet service. By 2025 the figure was 42.1, more than four times as many, and the share of people aged six and over who use the internet anywhere rose from 21.8 to 67.6 per cent.

Still, about a third of Loreto's people aged six and over were not online in 2025, and fewer than a quarter of homes had a computer. We would rather say that plainly than pretend every family can start tomorrow.

Where that leaves a family

If your home has internet and a laptop or desktop, a live class will usually work. If the only device is a phone, a young child can still try block coding, but an older learner will struggle to type and test code on a small screen.

Some families borrow a laptop from a relative, use a computer at a parent's workplace after hours, or begin with one-to-one lessons at the time of day when their connection is steadiest.

Connection

Can a live class work on a Loreto internet connection?

Often yes, and the only way to know is to try. We do not publish a minimum speed figure because connections in the region vary from hour to hour; instead the free first class is the test.

  1. Book the free class at your usual hour. Choose the time you would really use, so the test reflects normal conditions in your home.
  2. Start with sound, not video. The mentor's voice and the shared screen matter most. Cameras can stay off if the picture freezes.
  3. Share code as text. Programs can be pasted into the chat, so nobody needs to read code from a blurred video frame.
  4. Keep one tab open. Streaming or downloads elsewhere in the house slow the class; pausing them during the hour helps.
  5. Decide together. After the class the mentor tells you honestly whether it worked well enough, and what could change it, before you pay anything.

If a lesson drops, the mentor waits and continues where the learner left off. In one-to-one plans a missed slot can be moved. This is not a promise that every connection will do; it is a promise to tell you the truth after the test.

Teaching language

Will my child be taught in English or Spanish?

In English, always. Mentors teach from India over live video, speak at the learner's pace and accent, and do not move the class into Spanish.

Peru and English

EF's 2025 English Proficiency Index places Peru 52nd of 123 countries with 519 points, in the moderate band. EF does not list a separate score for Loreto.

We assume nothing and plan for learners who are still building their English: short sentences, repeated words, and code on screen that shows what the words mean.

Code helps the English

Programming languages borrow plain English words: if, else, while, print, return. A child who uses them every week learns them the way they learn a game's rules, by doing.

When a learner answers in Spanish, the mentor offers the English back without fuss. Many children understand the class well long before they feel ready to speak much.

A Loreto data project

How acidic are the soils around Iquitos?

The Instituto de Investigaciones de la Amazonía Peruana (IIAP), based in Iquitos, publishes soil pit descriptions written by its field scientists. The pH value is hidden inside a sentence, so the first job is to pull it out, and the second is to check that the words agree with the number.

The dataset

The dataset is "Perfiles de suelo de San Martín, Loreto, Madre de Dios y Ucayali (IIAP, 2005-2017)", published on datosabiertos.gob.pe with an Open Data Commons Attribution licence (ODC-By), so it may be reused, commercially too, with credit to the IIAP. Version 2.1 is dated 15 October 2026. Its description speaks of 143 soil profiles and 537 horizons, the layers seen in each pit.

Loreto contributes 33 profiles, 32 of them collected in 2011, mostly in the districts around Iquitos and Nauta, with 145 horizons between them. We downloaded horizontes_suelo.csv on 9 November 2026.

What one layer looks like

Each horizon has a free-text field written in the field. The first Loreto layer reads, in part: "ultra ácido (pH 3.38)". So a number sits in brackets after the letters pH, and a word label describing the acidity sits just before it.

Our question: across all Loreto layers, how acidic is the soil? On the pH scale 7 is neutral, lower is more acidic, and each whole step is ten times more acidic than the next.

The pattern pH followed by a space, a digit, a point and more digits found a value in every one of the 145 layers. The middle value, 4.19, is strongly acidic soil, typical of the old, heavily rained-on ground of the lowland Amazon. A learner could stop here. But the text holds a second description of the same fact, the word label, and two descriptions of one fact should agree.

Version 2 captures the words just before the bracket as well, groups the layers by label, and prints the lowest and highest pH under each one. If the labels are consistent, each range should sit neatly above the one before it.

Three things jump out. First, a typing slip: two layers say fultra ácido, which is ultra ácido with a stray letter, and a program treats them as a separate category until someone fixes it. Second, label variants: extremadamente ácida with an a, and reacción fuertemente ácida with an extra word. Third, an overlap: the ultra ácido range reaches 4.59, higher than every extremadamente ácido layer, even though ultra is meant to be the more acidic word. Checking that row shows a single layer, horizon 3 of profile LO-029, labelled ultra ácido at pH 4.59; every other ultra ácido layer is below 3.6.

We do not correct the IIAP's records. We report what the program found and let a soil scientist decide whether the word or the number is wrong. That is the habit we want every learner to leave with: a pattern that matches is not the same as a fact that is right.

Data credit: IIAP, soil profiles of San Martín, Loreto, Madre de Dios and Ucayali, reused under ODC-By. The summaries are ours. One layer, in profile LO-013, opens a bracket before its pH and never closes it, so a stricter pattern that demanded the closing bracket would miss it.

  1. Under 8, in the garden. Sort five words, warm, hot, boiling, cool, cold, into order. Then ask: what if someone wrote "boiling" for a cup of tea that was only warm?
  2. Block coding, around 8 to 10. Make a list of pH numbers and a loop that says "acid" below 7, "neutral" at 7 and "alkaline" above it.
  3. First Python, around 10 to 12. Run Version 1, then change the pattern so it insists on the closing bracket, and find the layer it now misses.
  4. Ages 13 and over. Write a dictionary that merges fultra ácido into ultra ácido and the feminine forms into the masculine ones, then print the ranges again.
  5. Going further. List every pair of labels whose ranges overlap, and the layers responsible. Write two sentences to the IIAP describing what you found, without claiming which value is wrong.

Contests

Can Loreto pupils enter the national maths olympiad?

Yes, through their school. The ONEM begins inside each colegio and moves up through the UGEL and regional stages. Modern Age Coders is independent of MINEDU, the ONEM and the GREL.

The ONEM in 2026

The 2026 school stage of the ONEM was held on Wednesday 8 July 2026 in every participating colegio, in three levels by secundaria grade. El Peruano reported that the 2026 olympiad involved more than 1.7 million pupils across Peru.

We never use the organiser's papers. We teach the reasoning contests reward with problems we write.

A practice problem of ours

Soil A has pH 4 and soil B has pH 6. Each step of 1 on the pH scale means ten times the acidity. How many times more acidic is soil A than soil B? (Ours. Answer: 100 times, because two steps mean 10 multiplied by 10.)

It looks like chemistry but it is really about powers of ten, and a two-line Python loop lets a student test the idea on other pairs.

What time would a class be in Iquitos?

Whatever time your family picks. Loreto runs on Peru time, and the mentor in India plans around your chosen slot.

Per the tz database, America/Lima is UTC minus five hours with no daylight saving since 1994, and INACAL keeps Peru's legal time. India is UTC plus five and a half hours. Iquitos and India are therefore ten and a half hours apart all year.

No clocks change on either side, so a slot that works in the rainy season works in the drier months too. Early mornings in Iquitos fall in the Indian evening, which suits many mentors, and they can also be the calmest time on a shared home connection.

Coding and AI classes in Loreto: questions and answers

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. All our classes are live, online and in English only. Mentors speak clearly, follow the learner's pace and accent, and if a child replies in Spanish they simply give the English words in return.

Do you have classes in Iquitos?

Our classes are online, so a learner in Iquitos joins from home over live video, exactly as one in Yurimaguas, Nauta or Requena would. There is no classroom to travel to.

Will the class work on our internet?

We cannot promise that in advance, which is why the free first class is also a connection test. Audio and a shared screen matter most, cameras can be switched off, and the mentor tells you honestly afterwards.

What time are classes in Loreto?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India is ten and a half hours ahead, so 08:30 in Iquitos is 19:00 for the mentor.

Can my child learn on a phone?

A young child can try block coding on a tablet or phone. From about nine, typing and testing code needs a laptop or desktop. Some families borrow one for class hours.

Do Loreto schools teach coding?

No timetabled subject covers it. The national curriculum spreads digital skills across all areas. Loreto pupils do take part in the Eureka science fair and regional maths contests, but regular programming lessons are rare.

Can adults in Loreto take classes?

Yes, in adult groups or one to one. Many begin with data and AI for non-programmers or with Python, and fit lessons around work.

Do you teach AI to children?

We explain how AI works through small projects children build. We follow each tool's age rules: under-13s do not use adult chatbot accounts with us, and teenagers only use tools that allow their age.

How do we pay?

By card, monthly, in US dollars; your bank converts the charge into soles. Visitors from India see the price in rupees. The first class is free and needs no card.

Is there a certificate?

Courses that include one end with a Modern Age Coders certificate of completion. It is our own record of the work done, not a Peruvian qualification, and no Peruvian school authority issues or backs it.
