Courses that teach only prompting produce students who can start anything and finish nothing. ## The five modules

1. The floor: Python and web fundamentals, learned by building. Prevents the failure of code you cannot read and therefore cannot own.
2. Direction: specs, constraints, and precise change requests. Prevents vague prompts compounding into unfixable projects.
3. Verification: reading diffs, testing deliberately, spotting confident nonsense. Prevents shipping whatever ran once on your machine. Every week includes one exercise where something the model produced is subtly wrong, and finding it is the assignment.
4. Shipping: version control, deploys, keys kept secret, real users. Prevents a folder of demos instead of a track record.
5. Agents: terminal coding agents, supervised, with the 18-plus tool floors honestly gated. Teens meet agents through demonstrations and the teen-permitted subset; the full terminal workflow belongs to the college and professional tracks.

## Why the syllabus changed: 2019 vs 2026

The 2019 beginner course spent weeks one to five on syntax drills, a rescue week on environment setup, and a guided identical project. Reasonable then: producing correct syntax was the scarce skill. The 2026 version ships a first build on day one, spends weeks two to four on reading and planted-flaw hunting, weeks five to seven on direction, weeks eight to ten on a chosen project with a real user, and ends by attacking your own app, then defending it at demo day. Judgement is now the scarce skill, so judgement gets the hours. The old skills did not die; they moved into module one and the weekly unassisted work.

## What a graduate can actually do

Ship a real tool in a weekend. Read code they did not write and say where the data lives and what runs on each action, tested cold. Catch a confident error, because a term of planted-flaw hunting built the reflex. Leave a version-controlled paper trail a reviewer can audit. Explain every decision out loud under questioning. And know what they do not know yet, which hiring managers name unprompted.
