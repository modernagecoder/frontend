# Game development for kids in Australia

Most children who ask to make games have already spent hundreds of hours playing them, so they arrive knowing exactly what a good game feels like and nothing about why. That gap is where the learning lives. Making a game means deciding what counts as a win, what the player sees first, what happens when two things touch and how fast anything moves. Each of those is a small programming problem in disguise. This page sets out how Australian kids can go from a first Scratch game in primary school to Python and Pygame around Year 7 and a real engine by Year 9 or 10, which mirrors the order the Australian Curriculum itself uses: visual programs first, then a general-purpose language, then object-oriented code. It also shows one idea worked all the way through, the hitbox, so you can see what a lesson actually contains.

## From a Scratch game to a real engine, by year level

Children learn game development fastest when each stage removes one kind of friction at a time. Scratch removes typing errors, Python removes the blocks, and an engine removes the plumbing so a teenager can think about design.

**A typical progression for Australian students**

| Year level | Tool | What a finished game looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation to Year 2 | Scratch Junior style activities, then Scratch | A character that moves when a key is pressed and makes a sound when it reaches a goal |
| Years 3 and 4 | Scratch | A catch-the-falling-objects game with a score that goes up and a timer that runs out |
| Years 5 and 6 | Scratch, with variables and clones | A platformer with gravity, two levels and a high score the player can beat |
| Years 7 and 8 | Python with Pygame | A top-down maze game written as text, with a game loop the student wrote themselves |
| Years 9 and 10 | Unity with C#, or Godot | A small 2D or 3D game split into scripts and scenes, with enemies that each have their own behaviour |
| Years 11 and 12, and adults | Unity, Godot or Unreal | A finished project with menus, saving and a build that someone else can install |

### Why blocks come first

In Scratch a child cannot make a spelling mistake, because every instruction is a block that snaps into place. That leaves all of their attention for the actual logic: when should the score increase, what should happen when time runs out, why does the cat walk through the wall?

Younger children who start in a text language often spend their first month fighting brackets. Children who start in Scratch spend it making games, and they arrive at Python already knowing what a loop and a variable are for.

### Why blocks are not the end

Scratch has a ceiling. Large projects become hard to read, there is no real way to split a game into files, and the skills do not carry straight into the jobs that use code.

Moving to Python, and later to an engine, is not about leaving childish things behind. It is about being able to build something bigger than one screen can hold, and to read code that somebody else wrote.

Ages are a guide, not a rule. A keen Year 4 student can start Python; a Year 9 student who has never coded usually still benefits from two or three weeks of Scratch first. The free first class is where that decision gets made, with the child at the keyboard rather than by guesswork. ## What a lesson looks like: the hitbox

Almost every game has to answer one question dozens of times a second: are these two things touching? Here is how we teach the answer, from a hand-drawn sketch to working code.

In Scratch, the question is a single block: touching coin? It works, and children love it. But it hides the idea. So once a student is comfortable, we take the block away and ask them to decide for themselves when two rectangles overlap. The rectangle drawn around a sprite for this purpose is called its **hitbox**, and the test is called an axis-aligned bounding box check, because the box edges run along the screen's x and y axes.

**Our worked example: does the player collect the coin?**

| Object | Left edge (x) | Top edge (y) | Width | Height |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Player | 100 | 200 | 40 | 40 |
| Coin | 130 | 220 | 16 | 16 |

### Check across

The player's left edge (100) is left of the coin's right edge (130 + 16 = 146), and the player's right edge (100 + 40 = 140) is right of the coin's left edge (130). So they overlap side to side.

### Check down

The player's top (200) is above the coin's bottom (220 + 16 = 236), and the player's bottom (240) is below the coin's top (220). So they overlap up and down as well.

### The verdict

Both checks pass, so the boxes overlap and the coin is collected. If either check fails, they are not touching, however close they look on screen.

In Python with Pygame, the same test becomes four comparisons joined by and, and a student who has worked it by hand can write it from memory. Pygame also has a built-in method for it, colliderect, but we only introduce it after the student has written their own version and tested it against the example above.

### The catch, in numbers

Boxes are fast but not exact. Our coin is round: a circle 16 pixels across has an area of about 201.06 square pixels, but its 16 by 16 hitbox covers 256. By our arithmetic the box counts about 27.3% more area than the coin really has, all of it in the corners.

That is why a player can sometimes "collect" a coin they visibly missed. Spotting this, and deciding whether it matters, is real game design.

### What students do with it

Older students go on to shrink hitboxes slightly so the game feels fair, to use circles for round objects, and to check only the objects that are nearby so a busy screen does not slow down.

Each of those is a trade-off between speed and accuracy, which is one of the most useful ideas in all of programming, not just games.

## Scratch, Pygame, Roblox, Unity and Godot, compared honestly

Parents often ask which tool is right. The honest answer is that each one is right at a different stage, and some children will use three of them in a single year.

**What each tool is good for**

| Tool | Language | Cost to the family | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scratch | Blocks | Free to use | First games and first ideas, from about age 6 |
| Pygame | Python | Free and open source (pygame 2.6.1 is published under the LGPL) | Learning how a game loop really works, in text |
| Roblox Studio | Luau | See Roblox's current terms | Building worlds other people can join, with a real scripting language |
| Unity | C# | See Unity's current licence terms | Teen and adult 2D and 3D games, with a large community of tutorials |
| Godot | GDScript or C# | Free and open source, released under the MIT licence | A lightweight engine that runs well on modest school laptops |

We avoid recommending tools by brand loyalty. A child who learns why a game loop exists, how collision is detected and how a state such as "paused" or "game over" is stored can move between any of these in a few weeks. That transferable understanding is the point; the tool is the vehicle.

Sources, read 7 October 2026: [Godot Engine licence page](https://godotengine.org/license/) ("free and open source software released under the permissive MIT license"); the Python Package Index entry for pygame (version 2.6.1, licence LGPL). Licence terms for Unity and Roblox change; read the current terms on their own sites before relying on them.

## Four stages from player to game maker

At one or two live lessons a week, most children reach the third stage within a school year. The fourth usually takes a second year.

**From playing games to making them**

| Stage | What your child can do |
|---|---|
| 1. Remix | Change an existing game's speed, sprites and score, and predict what each change will do |
| 2. Build | Make a complete small game from a blank project, with a start, a goal and an ending |
| 3. Explain | Write the same game in Python and explain each line, including the game loop and collisions |
| 4. Design | Plan a larger game on paper, split it into parts, build it in an engine and test it with real players |

## What Australian parents ask about game development

### What is game development for kids?

It is designing and programming a playable game: deciding its rules, making things move, keeping score and detecting when objects touch. For children it usually starts in a visual language such as Scratch and moves to text languages such as Python later.

### What is a hitbox in a game?

A hitbox is an invisible rectangle around an object that a game uses to decide whether two things are touching. It is fast to check, but slightly larger than a round object, which is why a near miss sometimes counts as a hit.

### What age can my child start making games?

Many children start in Scratch at about six or seven, once they can read simple words. Python usually suits children from about nine or ten, and engines such as Unity suit teenagers.

### Should my child learn Scratch or Python first?

For most children under about ten, Scratch first, because it removes typing errors and leaves all their attention for logic. Older beginners can often move to Python within weeks.

### Does this match what my child does at school?

It follows the same order as the Australian Curriculum v9 Digital Technologies content: visual programs in Years 3 to 6, a general-purpose language such as Python in Years 7 and 8, and object-oriented programming by Year 10.

### Is Roblox safe for my child to code in?

Roblox Studio is a development tool, and our lessons focus on building, not chatting with strangers. Parents should still review Roblox's own age guidance and parental controls before setting up an account.

### Will my child just play games in the lessons?

No. They test games, but nearly all lesson time goes into building, fixing and explaining them. Playing each other's finished games is a short and useful part of the end of a project.

### What computer does my child need?

A laptop or desktop with a keyboard and a modern browser is enough for Scratch and Python. Engines such as Unity need more memory, so we check your computer before recommending one.

### When are lessons in Australian time?

After school or at the weekend in your own state's time, agreed in the free first class. Our teachers are in India, which is between two and a half and five and a half hours behind Australian clocks depending on state and season.
