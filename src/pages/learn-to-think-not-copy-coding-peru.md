# Learn to think, not copy: coding classes for Peru

A program copied from a classmate, a video or a chatbot runs exactly as well as one the learner wrote. The difference only shows later, in a room with no internet, a pencil and a clock. ## Why does copying code fail Peruvian students when it matters?

Because the tests that decide medals, places and scholarships in Peru are sat in person, on paper or on a locked machine, and most of them reward a worked answer rather than a lucky choice. Read their own rules and the pattern is plain.

### The olympiads

ONEM is enormous. El Peruano reported on 11 October 2026 that the 2026 edition drew more than 1.7 million secondary students from its first stage, and that 237 finalists met for the national stage at Huampaní. Each school-stage paper in 2026 had two items per CNEB maths competency, and every answer was a value the student had to construct. Entry runs through the school.

The Peruvian Computing Olympiad (OPI), run by UTEC, is free for every secondary student in the country. ## What does it mean to learn to code by thinking instead of copying?

It means four habits that a mentor can see and a parent can check. A learner who has them can sit in front of an empty file and start; a learner who only copies cannot, however many programs they have run.

1. **Predict before running.** The learner says, out loud and in English, what the program will print. Being wrong is useful: the gap between the guess and the output is exactly where the misunderstanding lives.
2. **Trace by hand.** For anything with a loop, the learner writes a table with one column per variable and one row per step. It is slow on purpose. After a few weeks the table moves into their head, which is what an olympiad or an exam needs.
3. **Explain each line.** "This line swaps the two numbers" is an explanation. "This line is from the example" is not. If a line cannot be explained, it is not kept, even when the program works.
4. **Test on cases you chose.** Ordinary inputs, the smallest input, zero, the same value twice, the inputs in reverse order. The learner picks them before seeing whether the code passes.

### What a copier says

"It works." Asked why, they point at the screen. Asked to change one requirement, they search again for a new answer.

### What a thinker says

"It works for these five cases, and it would break if the list were empty, so I added a check." The sentence is longer, and it is theirs.

### What the mentor does

Asks short questions: what will this print, why this line, what if the input is zero. The mentor does not type the answer, even when it would be faster.

Peru's national curriculum names a close cousin of these habits. Among the CNEB's competencies that cut across every subject is "Gestiona su aprendizaje de manera autónoma", managing one's own learning, with three capacities: define learning goals, organise strategic actions to reach them, and monitor and adjust one's own performance along the way. Predicting and then checking a program is that third capacity in miniature.

## What does a "think, don't copy" coding lesson look like?

This lesson is ours, written for this page. Our mentors use it with teenagers from about 13 and with adults in their first months, and the maths is the greatest common divisor that Peruvian pupils meet in primaria. The point is not the answer, which any chatbot gives in a second. The point is being able to show why the answer is right.

The problem, ours: two ribbons are 84 cm and 120 cm long, and we want to cut both into pieces of equal length, as long as possible, with nothing left over. The longest piece is the greatest common divisor of 84 and 120. The mentor first asks for a plan in plain English. Most learners say: start at the smaller number and count down until something divides both.

**Version 1: the honest brute force** (Python)
```python
def gcd_slow(a, b):
    checks = 0
    d = min(a, b)
    while d > 0:
        checks = checks + 1
        if a % d == 0 and b % d == 0:
            return d, checks
        d = d - 1

print(gcd_slow(84, 120))
print(gcd_slow(1071, 462))
```
The function returns two things: the divisor it found and how many numbers it had to try. Before running, the mentor asks: how many checks for 1071 and 462?

**What Version 1 prints** (Output)
```output
(12, 73)
(21, 442)
```
Exact output from our own run with Python 3. The ribbons give 12 cm after 73 tries. The second pair gives 21, but only after 442 tries, because the search walks down one number at a time from 462.

Nothing is wrong with Version 1. It is correct and easy to explain, and that matters: a learner who wrote it understands it. But 442 checks for a three-digit pair is a warning. The mentor then shows Euclid's idea, more than two thousand years old: the greatest common divisor of two numbers is unchanged if you replace the larger one by the remainder of dividing it by the smaller one. Keep doing that until the remainder is zero.

**Version 2: Euclid, with its own trace table** (Python)
```python
def gcd_traced(a, b):
    print(f"{'a':>6} {'b':>6} {'a % b':>6}")
    while b != 0:
        print(f'{a:>6} {b:>6} {a % b:>6}')
        a, b = b, a % b
    return a

print('answer:', gcd_traced(1071, 462))
print('answer:', gcd_traced(462, 1071))
```
The program prints its own trace table: one row per pass of the loop, before the swap on the last line of the loop runs. The second call deliberately passes the numbers in the opposite order.

**What Version 2 prints** (Output)
```output
     a      b  a % b
  1071    462    147
   462    147     21
   147     21      0
answer: 21
     a      b  a % b
   462   1071    462
  1071    462    147
   462    147     21
   147     21      0
answer: 21
```
Exact output from our own run. Three passes instead of 442 checks. With the numbers reversed there is one extra row: 462 % 1071 is 462, so the first pass simply swaps them.

1. **Trace it on paper first.** Before the learner runs Version 2, they fill in the three rows by hand: 1071 divided by 462 leaves 147; 462 divided by 147 leaves 21; 147 divided by 21 leaves 0. The program's table only confirms what they already wrote.
2. **Predict the surprise.** "What happens if the smaller number comes first?" Most learners expect an error or a wrong answer. Tracing one row shows the loop fixes the order by itself, and the learner can say why.
3. **Read the swap.** a, b = b, a % b computes both values on the right before assigning either. Written as two separate lines, a = b then b = a % b, it breaks, and finding out why is a five-minute lesson in its own right.
4. **Choose hard tests.** The learner lists cases before running anything: a prime and a non-prime, a pair in reverse order, and pairs that contain zero.

**Version 3: test against the standard library** (Python)
```python
import math

def gcd(a, b):
    while b != 0:
        a, b = b, a % b
    return a

tests = [(84, 120), (1071, 462), (462, 1071), (17, 5), (12, 0), (0, 12)]
for a, b in tests:
    print(a, b, gcd(a, b), math.gcd(a, b), gcd(a, b) == math.gcd(a, b))
```
Python's math module documents gcd(*integers) as the "Greatest common divisor of the integer arguments". Here it is used as an independent referee, never as a replacement for understanding.

**What Version 3 prints** (Output)
```output
84 120 12 12 True
1071 462 21 21 True
462 1071 21 21 True
17 5 1 1 True
12 0 12 12 True
0 12 12 12 True
```
Exact output from our own run: six cases, six matches, including both orders of 1071 and 462 and the two pairs with a zero.

Then the mentor asks one last question: would Version 1 survive the same tests? A learner who traces gcd_slow(0, 12) by hand sees that d starts at 0, the loop never runs and the function returns nothing at all. Spotting that without running it is the skill we are after. A copied answer could not have told you, because the person who copied it never looked inside.

## Is using AI to write code the same as copying?

Pasting AI output you cannot explain is copying, with a faster source. Using AI to check your reasoning, explain an error or suggest a test you missed is not. The line is the same one the four habits draw: if you cannot predict, trace and explain it, it is not yours yet.

### How we use AI in class

Teenagers and adults meet AI tools in our classes after the basics are in place, never before. The order is fixed: the learner writes or traces their own version first, and only then asks an assistant, and the assistant's answer is treated as a claim to be tested, like Version 3 tests Version 2 above.

The tools themselves have age rules. Claude requires users to be 18; ChatGPT asks under-18s for a parent's permission. Children under 13 in our classes do not use chatbots at all. Our [AI tools age guide](/ai-tools-age-guide) lists what each tool's own terms say.

### Why the competitions ban it

The OPI rules ban generative AI for solving problems, and the ONEM paper is sat on paper in a school classroom. That is not hostility to technology. It is how an organiser finds out what a student can do alone, which is also what a university, an employer and, sooner or later, the student wants to know.

A learner who has spent a year predicting and tracing finds these rules a relief: the room asks for exactly what they practise. A learner who has spent a year pasting finds the same room empty.

## At what age can a child learn to think like a programmer?

From 6, in a form that suits the age. The habit of predicting and checking does not need typing; it needs a question asked before the green flag or the run button is pressed.

### Ages 6 to 8

Scratch blocks. "Where will the cat be after this loop?" The child points on the screen, then presses the flag. Being wrong and laughing about it is part of the lesson.

### Ages 9 to 12

Short Python programs and turtle drawings. Children write their guess in a notebook before running, and keep a list of surprises with one English sentence each.

### Ages 13 and up

Trace tables, test cases and algorithms such as the Euclid lesson above, then data structures. Teenagers who like competitions can aim at ONEM or OPI with problems we write ourselves.

Adults follow the teenage track at their own speed. A professional who has used spreadsheets for years often traces faster than a teenager, and is usually more frustrated by copied code they cannot fix at work. ### Explaining is the exercise

The habit at the centre of this page is explaining your code aloud. Doing it in English doubles the work in a useful way: the learner has to find the words, which forces them to find the idea first. "The remainder becomes the new divisor" is a sentence you cannot say without understanding it.

Python's keywords and error messages are English already. A learner who explains code in English is reading the language the computer answers in.

### Starting with little English

Nobody needs to arrive fluent. Children start with short phrases and pictures; teenagers and adults get a word list per lesson. Mentors repeat in simpler words and write key terms in the chat.

## How can you tell a learner is thinking, not copying?

Five stages we use with learners in Peru. They are ours, not an official scale, and they describe behaviour a parent can watch for.

**Five stages from copying to reasoning (our scale)**

| Stage | What the learner does | What to ask them |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Runs it | Runs code and reports whether it worked | "What did you expect it to do?" |
| 2. Predicts it | Says the output before running, right or wrong | "Why did you expect that?" |
| 3. Traces it | Fills a table of variables step by step for a short loop | "Show me the row where it changes." |
| 4. Breaks it | Chooses inputs that might fail, such as zero or reversed order | "Which input worries you most?" |
| 5. Starts blank | Writes a working solution from an empty file and explains it | "Teach it to me in two minutes." |

### For parents

Pick one question from the right-hand column each week. You do not need to understand the code; listen for whether the answer is about ideas or about where the code came from.
Moving from stage 2 to stage 3 usually takes the longest. Trace tables feel slow, and that slowness is the training.

### For learners

Write your guess before you press run. Keep a list of the times you were wrong; it is the most useful page in your notebook.
When something works on the first try, try to break it before celebrating.

## Learning to think, not copy: questions and answers

### Are the classes in Spanish?

No. Every class is live, online and in English only, taught by mentors in India who speak clearly and adjust to each learner's pace and accent. Explaining code aloud in English is part of the method, and parents can still write to us in Spanish on WhatsApp.

### What does "think, not copy" mean in practice?

Before code counts as the learner's own, they predict its output, trace it step by step, explain every line and test it on cases they chose themselves. If any of the four is missing, the code is treated as borrowed, even when it runs.

### Is it bad to look at example code?

No. Reading good code is how everyone learns. The rule is that an example is read, explained and then closed, and the learner writes their own version from memory. Typing it out line by line while looking is copying slowly.

### Can my child use ChatGPT or other AI tools?

Children under 13 do not use chatbots in our classes. Teenagers use them later, with a parent's permission where the tool's terms ask for it, and only after writing their own version first. Claude's terms require users to be 18, so we use it only with adults.

### Do you prepare students for ONEM or the OPI?

We teach the reasoning those competitions reward, with problems we write ourselves; we never reproduce an organiser's problems. ONEM entry is through the school, and the OPI is organised by UTEC. We are not connected with either and promise no results.

### Will this help with university admission exams?

The habits help with any exam that rewards worked answers, but the admission exams of Peruvian universities are in Spanish and the universities decide admission. We offer maths reasoning practice in English alongside school, not exam preparation or a promise of a place.

### What age can my child start?

From 6, with Scratch and spoken predictions. Typed Python usually starts around 9, and trace tables and algorithms from about 13. Adults of any age can start from zero.

### What time are the classes?

Yes, a laptop or desktop with a keyboard, camera and microphone. A phone is fine for watching but too small for writing and tracing code. Paper and a pencil are useful too.
