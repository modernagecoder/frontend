High school computer science access by state

Can a high school student in your state take a computer science class at their own school? The answer depends heavily on where they live. In the latest national figures, 60% of public high schools offer foundational computer science, ranging from every high school in Arkansas and Maryland to about a third in Alaska and Minnesota. This page gives every state’s count, the share of students who actually enroll, and how access has moved since 2019, with every figure tied to its source year.

In short

In Code.org (2025), 2025 State of AI + CS Education, 14,805 of 24,405 US public high schools, or 60%, offer foundational computer science, but only 871,490 of 14,364,449 high school students, about 6.1%, were enrolled, a figure based on the states that report participation (42 in 2024 to 2025). Arkansas (301 of 301) and Maryland (219 of 219) report every high school offering CS; Alaska (34.2%) and Minnesota (34.4%) report the lowest shares. The top of the table

Arkansas and Maryland report all of their high schools offering a foundational CS course. Nevada (143 of 146), New Hampshire (89 of 95), South Carolina (276 of 300) and Alabama (351 of 382) are all above 90%.

Four of those six, Alabama, Arkansas, Nevada and South Carolina, are also listed in the same report as states with a CS graduation requirement.

The bottom of the table

Alaska (34.2%), Minnesota (34.4%), Kansas (37.9%), Montana (38.4%), Florida (38.8%) and Louisiana (39.3%) all report fewer than four in ten high schools offering CS.

The report flags Alaska specifically: Code.org says it "encountered some challenges collecting data for Alaska this year", so treat its drop with care.

The national line

Nationally, the sheet shows 47% of high schools offering CS in 2019 to 2020, rising to 51%, 53%, 57.7% and then 60% in both 2023 to 2024 and 2024 to 2025. An earlier 45% for 2018 to 2019 rests on only 39 states.

The flat last two years are why the report describes access as having plateaued, except in states with a graduation requirement.

Reading a decline

A falling share does not always mean courses were cut. If a state opens new high schools, or counts schools differently from one year to the next, the share can move without any class disappearing.

Where a figure surprises you, compare the counts as well as the percentage. The access table above gives both.

Highest and lowest

South Carolina (25.7%) and Arkansas (25.1%) report about one high school student in four enrolled in CS. Rhode Island (18.4%), Maryland (16.7%) and North Dakota (16.1%, including cybersecurity) follow.

At the other end, Idaho and Minnesota each report 1.8%, and Florida 2.3%.

Why access and enrollment differ

A single elective section in a school of 2,000 students counts that school as offering CS, yet only a few dozen students may take it. Maryland shows the gap clearly: every high school offers CS, while 16.7% of students are enrolled.

For families, the lesson is that "my school offers CS" is the start of the question. Ask how many sections run, at what level, and whether they fit around other required classes.

Check the numbers

Recompute and rank the shares in Python

Our example for a first data lesson: start from counts, compute each share, and sort. It shows why a published percentage is only as good as the two numbers behind it.

  1. Store pairs, not percentages. Keeping offering and total lets you recompute and catch typing errors.
  2. Use a dictionary comprehension to turn each pair into a share in one line.
  3. Sort with a key. key=lambda kv: kv[1] sorts by the share, not the state name.
  4. Format as a percentage. The :6.1% format multiplies by 100 and adds the percent sign.
  5. Extend it. Add the year as a third value and print it, so no one compares 2023 to 2024 with 2024 to 2025 by accident.

High school CS access: common questions

What share of US high schools offer computer science?

About 60%: 14,805 of 24,405 public high schools, according to Code.org (2025), 2025 State of AI + CS Education. The national figure combines each state's most recent year.

Which states have the highest access?

Arkansas and Maryland report every high school offering CS. Nevada, New Hampshire, South Carolina and Alabama are each above 90% in their most recent year.

Which states have the lowest access?

Alaska (34.2%) and Minnesota (34.4%) report the lowest shares, followed by Kansas, Montana, Florida and Louisiana, all under 40%.

How many students actually take computer science?

Nationally, 871,490 of 14,364,449 high school students, about 6.1%, counting the states that report participation. State shares range from 25.7% in South Carolina to 1.8% in Idaho and Minnesota.

Why do some states show no enrollment figure?

Alaska, Colorado, Delaware, DC, Maine, Michigan, Nevada, New Hampshire and South Dakota had no participation figure in the most recent data. Not reported does not mean zero.

Has access improved over time?

Yes, from 47% in 2019 to 2020 to 60% in the latest two years nationally. Mississippi gained the most in our calculation, 39 percentage points.

My child's school has no CS class. What can we do?

Ask the counselor about state-approved online or dual enrollment courses, ask whether an AP exam can be taken without the school course, and learn live outside school. We teach students in exactly this situation.

When are your classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

Can I reuse these figures?

Cite the original: Code.org (2025). 2025 State of AI + CS Education. Code.org publishes the raw data and states the report licence on its site.

Are you part of Code.org?

No. Modern Age Coders is a separate online school. We quote Code.org's published data and link to it.
