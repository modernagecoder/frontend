Can a Montreal student get live math tutoring in English?

Yes. Modern Age Coders teaches math in English, live online, to Montreal students from elementary school through Secondary V, with a private teacher or alongside fifteen to twenty classmates working at the same stage. Secondary students mostly join High School Mathematics: Algebra to Calculus, taught to their CST, TS or Science option; contest students add Waterloo Math Contests. The first lesson is free.

Montreal students meet a fork in Secondary IV that shapes the rest of their math. The Québec Education Program offers three options in Cycle Two: Cultural, Social and Technical, Technical and Scientific, and Science. The province says all three allow entry to a pre-university CEGEP program, but they differ in how much algebra they ask for. Secondary IV also brings a ministerial exam that counts for half the final mark in the competency it assesses. This page sets out those facts from Québec’s own English documents, then teaches a Secondary IV statistics lesson on the Ville de Montréal’s library figures, works a regression problem by hand, and covers contests and prices. Our lessons are in English; French is the official language of Québec, and we help students read math vocabulary in both.

Quebec secondary math for a Montreal student: options, exam and CEGEP

Montreal schools teach the Québec Education Program. The facts below come from the English version of the program and from Québec.ca, read on 22 and 23 October 2026; quoted wording is the province’s.

Québec.ca says the ministerial examinations in Secondary IV and V count for 50% of the final mark for the competency or competencies evaluated. The schedule labels each mathematics paper “Math. Reasoning”, matching the program’s second competency, Uses mathematical reasoning. A Montreal Secondary IV student therefore has two very different June dates depending on option, and a January sitting for students on a semester timetable.

Three options, one door

The program names them: the Cultural, Social and Technical option, the Technical and Scientific option and the Science option. It adds that all of these options allow students to enroll in a pre-university program. Our CST, TS or Science page compares them in depth.

Secondary V next

Secondary V continues the chosen option with new concepts and a longer project. Our Secondary V math page covers it, including the option changes the program allows.

Then CEGEP

Québec.ca describes a pre-university CEGEP program as one that prepares students for university and requires two years of full-time study, and notes that universities use the R score to analyze applications. Our CEGEP preparation page covers the computer science route.

Statistics runs through all three options, and the program is specific about what students should do with it. Describing the competency Uses mathematical reasoning, it says that in the case of two-variable distributions students interpret the correlation and are able to interpolate or extrapolate using a regression line, and they exercise critical judgment about whether there is a dependent or causal relationship between variables. That is the exact lesson below, on Montreal’s own data.

Montreal data lesson

Do busier Montreal libraries lend more books? Correlation on city data

The Ville de Montréal publishes yearly statistics for its public libraries as open data. Secondary IV students plot visits against loans, measure the correlation, fit a regression line and argue about what it proves. We computed every coefficient and residual shown here ourselves.

The dataset is the Bibliothèques de Montréal statistics on loans, collections and attendance; the City describes it, in French, as tables presenting those statistics for Montreal’s 45 libraries (the English rendering is ours). We used the consolidated workbook covering 2012 to 2020, downloaded on 23 October 2026, and its 2018 sheet. Of 49 numbered rows, 45 libraries across 19 boroughs report visits, loans and floor area; the four we left out are two mobile services and two sites with missing figures.

Across the 45 libraries our correlation between visits and loans is r = 0.825, a strong positive linear relationship, and r squared is 0.681, so about two thirds of the variation in loans lines up with variation in visits. The regression line is loans = 1.1486 × visits + 45,658. At 150,000 visits, an interpolation well inside the data, it predicts about 217,952 loans. The Python reads the 2018 sheet and does the rest:import openpyxl, statistics book = openpyxl.load_workbook("bm_stats-consol.xlsx", data_only=True) branches = [] for row in book["2018"].iter_rows(values_only=True): if isinstance(row[0], int): # numbered library rows only loans, visits, area = row[8], row[19], row[33] if all(isinstance(v, (int, float)) for v in (loans, visits, area)): branches.append((row[4], visits, loans)) visits = [b[1] for b in branches] loans = [b[2] for b in branches] line = statistics.linear_regression(visits, loans) print("libraries:", len(branches)) print("r:", round(statistics.correlation(visits, loans), 3)) print("loans = ", round(line.slope, 4), "x visits +", round(line.intercept))

Residuals tell stories

Benny had the most visits in 2018, 611,789, yet lent about 167,753 fewer items than the line predicts. Ahuntsic lent about 272,282 more. Libraries are used for study, events and computers as well as borrowing, and the residual shows how differently branches are used.

One number to question

Pierrefonds recorded only 20,469 visits in the 2018 sheet but 365,408 in the 2019 sheet. The file does not say why. A careful student flags that point before fitting anything, and checks how much the line moves without it.

A third variable

Visits also correlate with floor area, r = 0.661 in our calculation. Bigger buildings draw more people and hold more books, so size may drive both visits and loans. That is the program’s point about dependent or causal relationships.

Correlation is not a cause

A strong r does not mean that visiting causes borrowing, or the reverse. It says the two rise together across these 45 libraries in 2018. Extending the line to a library with three million visits would be extrapolation far outside the data, and the program asks students to say exactly that when they extrapolate.

A worked problem, ours

Worked problem: a regression line by hand, then a warning

Five invented branches, written by us so that every step can be done without software. The numbers are in thousands.

Question. Five branches report visits x and loans y, in thousands: (50, 90), (100, 160), (150, 190), (200, 260), (250, 300). (a) Find the regression line. (b) Estimate loans at 120 thousand visits. (c) Should the line be used for a library with 600 thousand visits?

Step 1, find the means. The mean of x is 150 and the mean of y is 200. The regression line always passes through the point (150, 200), which is a quick check on any answer.

Step 2, compute the slope. Multiply each x deviation by its y deviation and add: (−100)(−110) + (−50)(−40) + 0(−10) + 50(60) + 100(100) = 26,000. The squared x deviations add to 25,000. The slope is 26,000 divided by 25,000, or 1.04 thousand loans per thousand visits.

Step 3, find the intercept and the line. 200 = 1.04(150) + b gives b = 44, so y = 1.04x + 44. The correlation coefficient for these points is about 0.993, a very strong linear relationship, stronger than the real Montreal libraries, as invented data usually is.

Step 4, interpolate and extrapolate. At 120 thousand visits, inside the data, y = 1.04(120) + 44 = 168.8 thousand loans, a reasonable estimate. At 600 thousand visits the line gives 668 thousand, but 600 is more than twice the largest x in the data. That is extrapolation, and the honest answer to part (c) is that the line may not hold there.

Try it yourself. (1) Remove the point (100, 160) and recompute the slope. (2) Describe the residual of the point (150, 190). (3) In one sentence, explain why the Benny library sits below the Montreal line without anything being wrong with its figures. Your teacher will go over them with you during the free lesson.

The Centre for Education in Mathematics and Computing (CEMC) at the University of Waterloo runs the contests most Canadian schools use. Unusually for a Quebec student, some are open to CEGEP students as well. Dates read on 22 October 2026.

CEMC organises its contests by grade, as most of Canada does, so a Montreal family should ask the school which contest it offers to a given Secondary level. The CEGEP rows are the distinctive ones: CEMC lists CÉGEP students as eligible for both the Euclid and the CSMC. A student in the first year of a pre-university program can therefore keep writing contests after leaving secondary school. Our Euclid preparation page covers that contest in detail.

Through a school

Individuals cannot sign up; CEMC contests are written through a school. A Montreal student whose school or CEGEP stays out can, on CEMC’s advice, ask another school nearby to take them.

Free practice

Ten years of CEMC past contests are free online without registration. Statistics, counting and algebra questions in them suit Secondary IV and V students well.

Math and AI

Why a Montreal student should judge the correlation, not the AI

An AI tool can compute r instantly. It cannot decide what r means for these libraries.

Give an AI tool the library workbook and ask whether more visits cause more loans, and it may answer yes, citing r = 0.825. It will rarely mention floor area, the Pierrefonds figure or the difference between interpolation and extrapolation. Those are exactly the judgments the Québec program asks of a Secondary IV student.

In our lessons a student guesses whether r will be positive or negative before calculating it, and afterwards writes what the value means. AI tools appear only for students old enough under our policy, as a check on work already done. Anything submitted to a school is written by the student alone.

The program asks students to exercise critical judgment about cause and dependency; no tool can exercise it for them. The longer argument is in why judgment matters more than ever with AI.
