## From the Middle Block to Computer Science 120: coding in the New Brunswick curriculum

The department's curriculum site, curriculum.nbed.ca, lays the curriculum out in program blocks and courses. We read it on 22 October 2026. Phrases inside quotation marks are copied from the department; the last column explains them in our own words.

**Coding and computer science in the New Brunswick curriculum framework (curriculum.nbed.ca, Government of New Brunswick; our reading in the last column)**

| Course or block | What the department says | What it asks of a student |
|---|---|---|
| Middle Block Technology, Grades 6 to 8 | Learners &ldquo;use coding and computational thinking to solve problems, and make informed decisions about privacy, cybersecurity, and responsible technology use&rdquo; | Write real programs, and think about what technology does with personal data |
| Middle Block Technology, new expectations | Students &ldquo;critically examine the role of algorithms, artificial intelligence, digital data, and online environments&rdquo; | Ask how an AI system reaches an answer before trusting it |
| Technology 9 | Concepts include &ldquo;Algorithms in coding&rdquo; and &ldquo;Encryption&rdquo;, ending in capstone projects shared in presentations | Build and present a project, with a first look at how data is protected |
| Computer Science 110, Grade 11 | Under computational thinking: &ldquo;Identify small tasks needed to solve large complex problems.&rdquo; | Break a big program into small functions, the core habit of programming |
| Computer Science 120, Grade 12 | Applications in &ldquo;object-oriented programming, recursion, data science, machine learning, artificial intelligence, and gaming&rdquo; | Design and prototype original software, including machine learning |
| Cybersecurity 120 | &ldquo;The focus is entirely defensive&rdquo;, with work in a sandboxed virtual network | Protect systems and analyse weaknesses, never attack real ones |

### Five program blocks

The department divides the curriculum into five program blocks: Early Learning and Childcare, Primary, Elementary, Middle, and High. Coding becomes a named skill in the Middle Block, inside a Technology course that also covers networking, cooking, design and repair.

### Two computer science courses

Computer Science 110 sits in Grade 11 and Computer Science 120 builds on it in Grade 12, where students design and prototype their own software. The department notes an updated online version through the New Brunswick Virtual Learning Centre.

### A cluster of options

Both courses belong to the Digital and Information Technology subcluster, with Technology 9, Cybersecurity and Technical Support 110, Cybersecurity 120, Information Technology 120, Digital Production 120 and Robotics and Automated Processing 120.

Read in order, the courses make a ladder: code in Grades 6 to 8, apply it in Technology 9, decompose problems in Computer Science 110, then build object-oriented programs and machine learning applications in Computer Science 120. The gap is time. A Grade 12 student who meets recursion, object-oriented design and machine learning in one semester has to learn all three at once. Students who reach Computer Science 120 already fluent in Python can use that semester to build something of their own, which is what the course description asks for.

### Alongside the school, never instead of it

Our classes run next to Technology, Computer Science 110 and 120 and the math courses, and we do not grant New Brunswick credits. Credit hours are earned at school. We are not connected with the New Brunswick government, its education department, a school district or the Virtual Learning Centre.

## The mathematics rule a New Brunswick programmer should know

Computer science at university leans on mathematics, and New Brunswick's high school framework sets a minimum. The rule below is the department's, from the Mathematics page of the High School Block.

**New Brunswick high school mathematics requirement (curriculum.nbed.ca, High School Block: Mathematics)**

| Requirement | Department wording |
|---|---|
| Total | &ldquo;Required: 12 credit hours from Mathematics&rdquo; |
| Compulsory course | Geometry, Measurement and Finance 10, worth 4 credit hours |
| The other 8 credit hours | Chosen from options including Number, Relations and Functions 10, Foundations of Mathematics 110 and 120, Pre-Calculus 110, Pre-Calculus 120A and 120B, and Calculus 120 |

For a student thinking about computer science or engineering, the Pre-Calculus sequence and Calculus 120 matter most, because they carry the functions, logarithms and rates of change that programming and machine learning use constantly. We teach that mathematics alongside school with practice we write ourselves, and we connect it to code wherever we can: a logarithm becomes the number of times a binary search halves a list, and a derivative becomes the slope a learning algorithm follows downhill.

### Numbers in code

Grade 10 math is a good moment to meet floating-point numbers properly: why 0.1 plus 0.2 does not print as exactly 0.3, and how rounding works in a finance problem from Geometry, Measurement and Finance 10.

### Functions both ways

A function in Pre-Calculus and a function in Python are close relatives. Students who write one for every formula they learn tend to read both kinds more fluently.

### How we help

Math support follows the order of the student's New Brunswick course. We do not reproduce school or provincial assessment items.

## One place, two official names: a bilingual data project on New Brunswick's place names

Natural Resources Canada publishes the official geographical names for each province, refreshed weekly from the national database. In New Brunswick, some features carry an English and a French official name. Our students find them with Python and then test their own rule. Every count below is ours.

**Our count of the Canadian Geographical Names file for New Brunswick (cgn_nb_csv_eng.csv), downloaded 22 October 2026**

| What we counted | Result |
|---|---|
| Rows (official names) in the file | 11,663 |
| Distinct features (Toponymic Feature ID) | 11,469 |
| Features with exactly two official names | 194 |
| Of those, parks and protected areas (Concise Code PARK) | 166 |
| Pairs where both rows give the Language as Undetermined | 189 |
| Pairs where our rule marks exactly one name as French-looking | 158 |
| Pairs our rule cannot settle, left for a human check | 36 |

Here is the script, which needs only Python 3 and the unzipped CSV in the same folder:

```
import csv

FRENCH_STARTS = ("Zone ", "Parc ", "Rivière", "Lac ", "Ruisseau", "Lieu ", "Réserve", "Unité", "Île", "Baie ", "Pointe", "Cap-")
ACCENTS = set("àâçéèêëîïôûùüÿœ")

def looks_french(name):
    return name.startswith(FRENCH_STARTS) or any(ch in ACCENTS for ch in name.lower())

names_for = {}
with open("cgn_nb_csv_eng.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        names_for.setdefault(row["Toponymic Feature ID"], []).append(row)

pairs = [rows for rows in names_for.values() if len(rows) == 2]
one_french = [p for p in pairs if sum(looks_french(r["Geographical Name"]) for r in p) == 1]
print("features", len(names_for), "with two names", len(pairs))
print("pairs with exactly one French-looking name", len(one_french))
for p in pairs:
    if p not in one_french:
        print("check by hand:", [r["Geographical Name"] for r in p])
```

### 1. Group by the shared ID

Each official name is a row with its own CGNDB ID, but a single feature keeps one Toponymic Feature ID across its names. Grouping rows by that ID turns 11,663 names into 11,469 features and reveals the 194 that have two names, such as Madawaska River and Rivière Madawaska, or Richards Lake and Lac Richards.

### 2. Notice the missing label

The file has a Language column, but on 189 of the 194 pairs it says Undetermined for both names, so it cannot tell a student which name is French. That gap is the problem the script solves: it guesses from the text, using accented letters and a short list of French opening words such as Parc, Lac and Rivière.

### 3. Measure the rule

The rule settles 158 pairs and fails on 36. Grand Falls and Grand-Sault have no accent or listed opening word; New Brunswick and Nouveau-Brunswick likewise. Students list the failures, improve the rule and run it again, which is the honest way to build any classifier, AI included.

### What the counts do not show

Two names in the file means two officially approved names, not a count of how often each is used. Most of the pairs are parks and protected areas, and 61 of them are Protected Natural Areas named on the same decision date, 21 March 2003, so the pairs are not a fair sample of the province's lakes, rivers and communities.

A follow-up for a Grade 12 student: rewrite the rule as a small scoring function that weighs several clues, then compare its errors with the first version. ## New Brunswick asks Grade 6 to 8 students to examine AI critically. That starts with code

A student who has built a simple rule, and counted where it fails, knows more about AI than one who has only used it.

The place-names rule on this page is a tiny classifier. It gets 158 pairs right and 36 wrong, and the wrong ones teach the most: a rule built on accents cannot see that Grand-Sault is French. Every AI system has failures like that, larger and better hidden, and the Middle Block's instruction to examine algorithms critically is a request to look for them.

In our classes students write the rule, test it and fix it before any AI tool is involved. Teenagers then use AI tools within each tool's age limits, and with a parent's consent wherever a tool requires it. Children below 13 never get adult tools from us; they discover how a prediction is made by coding a miniature predictor of their own.

Computer Science 120 lists machine learning and artificial intelligence among its topics; the student who has already measured a model's mistakes will get far more from it. The longer argument is in [is coding still worth learning in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
