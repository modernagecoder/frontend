## Points or per cent?

Two comparisons between Ulster (part of) and Munster, each measured two ways. Counts are published; every share and difference is our arithmetic.

**The same two gaps, as percentage points and as a relative difference**

| Comparison | Ulster (part of) | Munster | Gap in points | Relative gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Journeys of 1.5 hours or more, share of stated | 6,077, about 3.5% | 15,185, about 2.0% | 1.5 points | Ulster share 76% higher |
| Residents born in Ireland | 243,018, about 78.4% | 1,122,711, about 82.8% | 4.5 points | Munster share 5.7% higher |
| Which gap looks bigger |  |  | Birthplace, three times over | Journeys, thirteen times over |

### Why the measures disagree

A percentage point gap is a subtraction; a relative gap is a division. Divide by a small number and the result is large, which is why a gap between two small shares, like long journeys, looks dramatic in relative terms and trivial in points.

### Where the denominators come from

Journey shares use people who stated a journey time: here 188,977 counted, of whom 17,631 did not state one. Birthplace shares use all 310,160 usual residents. Both come straight from the census province tables.

### What a learner writes

Every comparison twice, once in points and once relative, and a sentence saying which one a headline would use and what that choice emphasises.

### The oldest trick in reporting, and the simplest defence

A risk rising from one in a thousand to two in a thousand is up 100%, and also up by a tenth of a percentage point. Both are true. The first sells a story; the second calms one. Health scares, price rises, exam results and advertising all use whichever sounds better for the purpose. The defence costs nothing: whenever you see a percentage change, ask for the two starting numbers, and whenever you see a points change, ask what it was out of. A teenager who has seen the same two census gaps swap places has the reflex for life.

The label on the data matters as well, and here the statistics office gets it exactly right. Its province tables do not call this area Ulster. They call it Ulster (part of), because the historic province also includes six counties outside the State that the Irish census does not cover. We follow that label throughout, and every figure on this page describes Cavan, Donegal and Monaghan only.

## A summary will say something rose by 76% and stop there

The relative figure is the eye-catching one, so it is the one that tends to travel.

Ask for the difference between long journeys here and in Munster and a quick answer may well lead with 76% higher. That is accurate. It is also a description of a gap of a point and a half between two small shares, and nothing in the sentence tells the reader which kind of number they are looking at.

A learner who has worked both versions asks for the base before reacting to any percentage change. It is the least glamorous question in statistics and the one that most reliably stops a misleading figure from being repeated.

Calculating a percentage is trivial for a machine. Knowing which percentage honestly answers the question is a human judgement, and a good reason to learn this properly in 2026. The longer argument is in [our case for learning to code properly](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
