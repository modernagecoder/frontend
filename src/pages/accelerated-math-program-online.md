# Accelerated math program online

An accelerated or compacted math track moves a middle schooler through three years of math in two, so Algebra 1 lands in grade 8, or even grade 7, instead of grade 9. That one decision changes the whole high school ladder, which is why families care about it so much. It also changes the pace in class: topics that used to get a month now get two weeks. This page explains how acceleration works using the published rules of states and districts, shows exactly which topics are compressed, and describes how our live lessons help a student keep up on the faster track or get ready for it.

## What is an accelerated or compacted math program?

It is a course sequence, not a separate curriculum. The same middle school standards are taught, but in fewer years, so a student reaches Algebra 1 a year or more early. The Common Core standards were written to allow this.

### What the standards say about Algebra I in grade 8

A note near the end of the Common Core mathematics document says the standards "do not mandate the sequence of high school courses". It goes on: "many students in the U.S. today take Algebra I in the 8th grade, and in some states this is a requirement."

It then adds the line that makes compaction possible: "The K-7 standards contain the prerequisites to prepare students for Algebra I by 8th grade". In other words, an accelerated track is not skipping content. It is fitting the grade 8 standards in alongside grade 7 so that grade 8 is free for Algebra I.

### Why middle school matters so much

The same note warns against rushing past grades 6 to 8: "some of the highest priority content for college and career readiness comes from Grades 6-8", naming ratio reasoning, fluent work with negative fractions and decimals, and angle, area, surface area and volume problems.

That is the honest tension in acceleration. The middle school topics being compressed are among the most important in the whole sequence, so a student on a faster track needs them taught well, not just faster.

**Three common middle school sequences (our summary of the published pathways quoted on this page)**

| Track | Grade 6 | Grade 7 | Grade 8 | Grade 9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Accelerated by one year | Math 6 | Compacted Math 7 and 8 | Algebra 1 | Geometry |

## Which states require accelerated math?

Most states leave acceleration to districts, but some write it into law. Texas requires a middle school advanced math program with automatic enrollment, and Washington requires districts to automatically place qualifying students into advanced high school courses.

**Two state acceleration laws, from the states' own pages (read 31 October 2026)**

| Point | Texas | Washington |
|---|---|---|
| Law | Senate Bill 2124 (2023), Texas Education Code section 28.029; state rule 19 TAC section 74.2101 | RCW 28A.320.195, academic acceleration for high school students |
| Starts | Districts implement no later than the 2024 to 2025 school year (TEA FAQ) | Policy adopted "By the 2021-22 school year" |
| Who is placed | Sixth graders in the top 40 percent on Grade 5 Math STAAR, or on a local measure | Students who meet or exceed standard on the grade 8 or high school math assessment |
| Placed into | A middle school advanced mathematics program designed to reach Algebra I in grade 8 | "the next most rigorous level of advanced courses or program offered by the high school" |
| Family choice | A parent or guardian may opt the student out | Districts may add criteria that do not create inequities between student groups |

### Texas: the courses and the compaction

The Texas Education Agency says the program's purpose is to "increase the number of students who complete advanced mathematics courses in high school." The state adopted three courses in 19 TAC sections 111.29 to 111.31, ending with Grade 8, Middle School Advanced Mathematics, Algebra I.

TEA publishes crosswalks between these courses and the on-level grades, which it says "provide information about how the TEKS were compacted." For a family, that crosswalk is the clearest map available of which grade 8 topics have moved into grade 6 or 7.

### Texas: what it means for tests

The TEA FAQ says that if an advanced course covers all of grade 6 and grade 7 in one year, students take "the higher level (i.e., grade 7) STAAR mathematics assessment", and a grade 8 student in Algebra I takes the STAAR Algebra I end-of-course test.

It adds a detail many families miss: "Students who take the Algebra 1 EOC must take the ACT or the SAT in high school." For students aiming at Algebra I in grade 7, TEA says districts may go further, but students "must demonstrate proficiency in all the TEKS for grades 6-8 mathematics prior to enrolling in Algebra 1."

Sources: TEA [Middle School Advanced Mathematics Program](https://tea.texas.gov/educators/subject-areas/middle-school-advanced-mathematics-program) and its [FAQ](https://tea.texas.gov/educators/subject-areas/middle-school-advanced-mathematics-program-faq); Washington [RCW 28A.320.195](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=28A.320.195); read 31 October 2026. Other states have their own rules; some state sites could not be read when we checked, so they are not described here.

## How do school districts run compacted math?

Districts design their own tracks, and the published versions differ a lot. Three public school districts show the range: one offers several entry points, one is opening Algebra I in grade 8 to every middle school, and one stresses that readiness comes at different ages.

### Seattle Public Schools: three sixth-grade options

Seattle Public Schools says it offers "three options for math enrollment in sixth grade for exceptional math learners", with eligibility "determined by math MAP (RIT) scores", and states that "Highly Capable (HC) identification is not a requirement."

Its published progressions run from Math 6 then Compacted Math 7/8 then Algebra in grade 8, with AP Calculus by senior year, to Compacted Math 7/8 in grade 6, Algebra in grade 7 and Geometry in grade 8, with AP Calculus by junior year. One route adds a district online summer course before grade 6.

### San Francisco Unified: Algebra I for grade 8

In a 26 March 2026 announcement, SFUSD said its board approved a revised Math Placement Policy and that "Beginning in the 2026-27 school year, Algebra I will be offered at ALL SFUSD middle and K-8 schools".

At most schools this is "Expanded Math", where eligible eighth graders are automatically enrolled in both Math 8 and Algebra I. The district is also piloting "Braided Compression", an accelerated pathway "covering Math 6-8 and Algebra I over three years", at two schools.

Montgomery County Public Schools in Maryland puts the other side of the argument plainly on its mathematics page: "Algebra readiness can occur at different points in a student's maturation." It also says students "are best served by Algebra 1 when they demonstrate prerequisite mathematical understandings, skills, and readiness." Acceleration is a good fit for some students at 11 and for others at 13, and the published pathways leave room for both.

Sources: Seattle Public Schools [Highly Capable Summer Math Program](https://www.seattleschools.org/departments/highly-capable/highly-capable-summer-math-program/) (2026 program, now closed); [SFUSD announcement, 26 March 2026](https://www.sfusd.edu/announcements/2026-03-26-board-education-approves-revised-math-placement-policy); [MCPS mathematics](https://www.montgomeryschoolsmd.org/curriculum/math/); read 31 October 2026. We are not connected with any of these districts; check your own district's current policy.

## What does compacted math leave less time for?

Every grade 7 and grade 8 standard is still taught, so the squeeze shows up as less practice time per topic. These are the topics where we most often see gaps in students who moved up a track, with our own examples at the level the track expects.

**Topics under pressure on a compacted grade 7 and 8 year, with worked examples (topics from the Common Core grade 7 and 8 standards; examples and commentary ours)**

| Topic | Why it gets squeezed | Our example | Answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proportional relationships (grade 7) | Taught quickly to reach linear functions | A printer makes 45 pages in 3 minutes. Write the relationship. | y = 15x, 15 pages per minute |
| Operations with negative fractions (grade 7) | Assumed from grade 6 and rushed | &minus;3/4 &divide; 1/2 | &minus;3/2 |
| Slope and equations of lines (grade 8) | Arrives months earlier than on level | The line through (2, 5) and (6, 13) | Slope 8/4 = 2, so y = 2x + 1 |
| Systems of two equations (grade 8) | Often one short unit | y = 2x + 1 and y = &minus;x + 10 | 3x = 9, so (3, 7) |
| Integer exponents (grade 8) | Rules learned without reasons | 23 &times; 2&minus;5 | 2&minus;2 = 1/4 |
| Pythagorean Theorem (grade 8) | Squeezed by the geometry unit | Legs of 9 and 12 | Hypotenuse 15, since 81 + 144 = 225 |
| Factoring (Algebra 1) | Needs fluent multiplication facts | x2 + 7x + 12 | (x + 3)(x + 4) |

### How the ideas connect

The printer example and the line example are the same idea a year apart. A proportional relationship, y = 15x, is a line through the origin; a linear function, y = 2x + 1, is the same thing shifted up. A student who understood grade 7 well finds slope easy. A student who rushed through it meets slope as a new formula.

That is why our tutors teach a compacted year by linking each grade 8 idea to the grade 7 idea underneath it, rather than as two separate courses pressed together.

### Warning signs a student is struggling

Grades usually hold up for a while, because a strong student can memorize procedures. The early signs are different: avoiding word problems, not being able to explain a step, and sign errors with negative numbers that were not there the year before.

If those appear in the first unit of a compacted course, a few weeks of focused help on the underlying grade 7 topics usually costs less time than falling behind for a whole year.

## Should my child take accelerated math?

It depends on readiness, interest and the alternatives your district offers, and only the family and the school can decide. These are the questions we suggest asking, with a short readiness check written by us.

1. Read your district's published pathway. Find where the track leads in high school and whether there is a way back to on level if needed.
2. Check the placement rule. In Texas, automatic enrollment follows the state rule; elsewhere it is a district decision, often using a test score, grades and teacher input.
3. Look at fluency, not just grades. Can the student work with fractions, negatives and ratios quickly and explain the steps?
4. Ask about interest. Students who enjoy math tend to handle a faster pace well. Students pushed into it often find the second year hard.
5. Plan the support. Decide in advance what happens if the first unit goes badly: extra help, a check-in with the teacher, or a later move.

**A quick readiness check before a compacted grade 7 and 8 year (ours; answers in brackets)**

| Skill | Problem | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Ratio reasoning | 3 notebooks cost the same as 2 binders. How many notebooks match 10 binders? | (15) |
| Fractions | 2/3 &divide; 4/9 | (3/2) |
| Negatives | &minus;7 &minus; (&minus;12) | (5) |
| Expressions | Simplify 3(2x &minus; 4) &minus; 2x | (4x &minus; 12) |
| Equations | 5x + 3 = 2x + 18 | (x = 5) |
| Percent | 15 is what percent of 60? | (25%) |

Our rule of thumb, not a district rule: a student who gets five or six of these right without help, and can explain how, is usually ready for the faster pace. A student who gets three or four is often ready after a few weeks of focused work. This check is ours and is not used by any school for placement.

## Accelerated math: common questions

### What is compacted math?

Compacted math covers more than one year of standards in a single year, for example grade 7 and grade 8 math together. It lets a student take Algebra 1 in grade 8, or earlier, without skipping any middle school content.

### Is accelerated math the same as honors math?

Not always. Honors usually means the same grade-level content in more depth, while accelerated or compacted means content from a later grade arrives sooner. Districts use the words differently, so read your district's course description.

### How do students qualify for accelerated math in Texas?

Under Senate Bill 2124 and 19 TAC section 74.2101, districts must automatically enroll sixth graders who scored in the top 40 percent statewide on Grade 5 Math STAAR, or in the top 40 percent on a local measure. A parent or guardian may opt the student out.

### Does Common Core allow Algebra 1 in 8th grade?

Yes. The standards document says the K-7 standards contain the prerequisites for Algebra I by grade 8 and that the standards are designed to let states continue their policies on Algebra I in grade 8.

### Can an accelerated math tutor help my child get placed?

We can build genuine readiness: fluent fractions, negatives, ratios and early algebra. We do not drill test items, and placement is always the school's decision, so we never promise a placement.

### Is there an accelerated math tutor near me?

Our lessons are live online, so location does not matter. Students join from home and keep the same teacher through the school year.

### What if my child is struggling on the accelerated track?

Start with the grade 7 topics underneath the current unit, since gaps there cause most of the trouble. Talk to the school too; most published pathways have more than one route to calculus.

### Can a student move back to on level math?

Usually yes, but the process belongs to the district. Ask the school counselor how and when changes can be made before the year starts.

### When are your accelerated math classes?
