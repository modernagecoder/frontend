> Coding classes for 5 and 6 year olds in Australia: what the Foundation year curriculum expects, games that teach sequencing at home, and live classes from age 6.

In short

Coding classes for 5 and 6 year olds teach the ideas underneath programming: putting instructions in order, making simple choices and repeating steps. At five, those ideas are easiest to learn through games without a screen. From six, most children can begin a block language such as Scratch in short, guided online lessons. The Australian Curriculum expects the same: by the end of Year 2 students "follow and describe basic algorithms involving a sequence of steps and branching".

Five or six?

## What is realistic at five, and what changes at six

The difference between a five and a six year old is large for coding, mostly because of reading, attention and fine motor control. Neither age is too young for the ideas.

|   | At five | At six |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading | Recognises some words; relies on pictures | Reads short labels such as "move" and "say" |
| Attention | Short bursts with lots of movement | Can follow a short guided lesson with a teacher |
| Mouse and keyboard | Dragging is still tricky for many | Can drag blocks and click accurately enough |
| Format that suits | Games at home with a parent, no screen needed | Short live online lessons, ideally with a parent nearby |
| What they build | Step-by-step instructions for a toy or a friend | A character that moves, speaks and reacts on screen |

### Why we start live classes at six

Our online lessons need a child to recognise a few words on screen, use a mouse with some control and stay with a teacher for a whole lesson. Most children can do that at six; many five year olds cannot yet, and that is entirely normal.

Starting too early can turn coding into a frustration. Waiting a few months, while playing the kind of games below, usually means a child starts at six already confident.

### What five year olds can do now

Five year olds are often superb at the thinking itself: they notice when a story is told out of order, they argue about the right way to make a sandwich, and they love giving instructions to adults who follow them too literally.

All of that is programming in disguise. The arrow-card game further down turns it into something deliberate, with no screen at all.

The name of the first school year varies around Australia (Kindergarten, Prep, Reception, Pre-primary or Transition, depending on where you live), but the curriculum calls it the Foundation year everywhere. Most five and six year olds are in the Foundation year or Year 1. Our page on [online coding classes for Australian kids](/online-coding-classes-for-kids-australia) lists each state's name and starting age, and our guide to [the right age to start coding](/best-age-to-start-coding) looks at readiness more generally.

The Australian Curriculum

## What the Foundation year and Years 1 and 2 expect

Digital Technologies starts in the Foundation year of the Australian Curriculum v9. The early expectations are modest and very sensible, and they describe thinking more than screens.

| When | What ACARA expects |
| --- | --- |
| By the end of Foundation | "students show familiarity with digital systems and use them for a purpose. They represent data using objects, pictures and symbols" |
| Foundation, digital systems (AC9TDIFK01) | "recognise and explore digital systems (hardware and software) for a purpose", for example a "programmable toy" |
| Foundation, privacy (AC9TDIFP01) | "identify some data that is personal and owned by them" |
| By the end of Year 2 | students "follow and describe basic algorithms involving a sequence of steps and branching" |

### A sun on a weather map

ACARA's description of the Foundation year gives a lovely example of early computational thinking: representing an idea "with a symbol, object or picture that is understood by others, such as a sun indicating fine conditions in a weather forecast".

That is the same idea as a block in Scratch: a picture that everyone agrees means "move forward" or "say hello".

### Socks before shoes

For Years 1 and 2, ACARA suggests children work out when the order of steps matters, with the example that "socks must be put on before shoes but a jumper can be put on before or after shoes".

Spotting which steps can swap and which cannot is a real programming skill. Professional programmers spend whole careers on the grown-up version of it.

Try this at home

## The arrow-card game: a first program with no screen

This is the unplugged activity we recommend most for five year olds, and we use a version of it in first lessons with six year olds too. You need paper, a pencil and a small toy.

### 1. Draw a grid

Draw a 4 by 4 grid of squares, big enough for the toy. Put the toy in the bottom-left square and draw a star in the top-right square.

### 2. Make cards

Cut out small cards and draw one arrow on each: up or right to begin with. Each card means "move one square that way".

### 3. Program the toy

Your child lays cards in a row, then "runs" the program by moving the toy one card at a time. If it misses the star, they find the wrong card and fix it.

### The maths hiding in the game

To reach the star, the toy must move up 3 squares and right 3 squares, so every shortest program uses exactly six cards. How many different six-card programs work? By our count, 20, because a program is just a choice of which 3 of the 6 cards point up (six choose three).

Five year olds will not calculate that, but they will discover that "up, up, up, right, right, right" and "right, up, right, up, right, up" both work, which is the real lesson: there is more than one correct program.

### Making it harder, slowly

Add a "wall" square the toy cannot enter, and some of the 20 programs stop working. Add left and down arrows. Let your child design a grid for you to solve.

For six year olds, add a "repeat" card: a card that says "do the next card three times". Suddenly six cards can become two, and the child has invented a loop.

Watch for the moment a child says "that one's wrong" and points at a single card. That is debugging, and it is the most important habit in programming. Praise the fix more than the first attempt.

From six

## What a first live lesson looks like for a six year old

Our first lessons for six year olds are built around one small success at a time, with a teacher guiding every step and a parent welcome to sit alongside.

| Week | The child makes | The idea underneath |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A cat that walks across the screen when the green flag is clicked | A program runs in order, from the top |
| 2 | The cat says hello, then waits, then says goodbye | Steps happen one after another; waiting is a step too |
| 3 | The cat dances by repeating a few moves | Repeating saves blocks: the first loop |
| 4 | A second character answers the first | Two characters can each have their own program |

### Short and lively

Lessons for this age switch between watching, trying and talking often, because six year olds learn by doing, not by listening for long.

### A grown-up nearby

For six year olds we ask that a parent or carer is in the room, especially for the first few lessons, to help with the mouse if needed.

### Celebrating small wins

Each lesson ends with something on screen that moves, so the child leaves proud of what they made.

Scratch is free and runs in a web browser, so there is nothing to buy. A laptop or desktop with a mouse is easier than a tablet for little hands. Once a child is comfortable, our guide to [coding at seven and eight](/coding-classes-for-7-and-8-year-olds-australia) shows what comes next.

Screen time

## Coding and screen time for young children

Parents of five and six year olds often worry about adding more screen time. It is a fair concern, and it is one reason the unplugged games matter so much at this age.

### Making, not watching

An hour of coding is a different kind of screen time from an hour of videos: the child is deciding, testing and fixing, and nothing happens unless they make it happen.

Even so, at five and six the right balance is plenty of off-screen play around a short lesson.

### Privacy from the first day

The Foundation curriculum asks children to "identify some data that is personal and owned by them". We follow the same principle: young children never need to share personal details in a lesson, and accounts are set up by a parent.

If a class uses a username, it is chosen with a parent and contains no surname or school name.

Stages

## Four early stages, from cards to Scratch

Five year olds usually spend months on the first two stages at home; six year olds in live lessons reach the third within a term.

| Stage | What your child can do |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Orders steps | Puts picture cards for a familiar routine in the right order and explains why |
| 2. Programs a toy | Lays out arrow cards that move a toy to a target, then fixes a wrong card |
| 3. First Scratch | Snaps together blocks so a character moves and speaks when the green flag is clicked |
| 4. First loop | Uses a repeat block instead of copying the same blocks again and again |

### Does my child need to read?

For the arrow-card game, no. For Scratch lessons at six, only a little: blocks have short labels such as "move" and "say", and the teacher reads anything longer aloud.

Coding often gives reluctant readers a reason to decode words, because the words make something happen.

### Fitting into the school week

Six year olds are often tired after school, so many families choose a weekend lesson. Billing is monthly, so it is easy to stop over the holidays.

Questions from parents

## What parents of 5 and 6 year olds ask

### Can a 5 year old learn coding?

Yes, the ideas: ordering steps, making simple choices and repeating actions. At five these are learned most easily through games without a screen, such as programming a toy with arrow cards. Typing and screen-based coding usually come a little later.

### Are there coding classes for 5 year olds?

Our live online classes start at six, because they need a little reading and mouse control. For five year olds we suggest the unplugged games on this page and a first class once they turn six.

### What do coding classes for 6 year olds involve?

Short, guided lessons in a block language such as Scratch, where children snap blocks together so a character moves, talks and reacts. Each lesson ends with something the child made and can show.

### What is unplugged coding?

Unplugged coding teaches programming ideas without a computer: putting instructions in order, following them exactly and fixing mistakes, using cards, toys, drawings or games.

### Does coding count as screen time?

It uses a screen, but it is active making rather than watching. For five and six year olds we still recommend short lessons and plenty of off-screen play around them.

### Is coding part of the Foundation year at school?

Digital Technologies starts in the Foundation year of the Australian Curriculum v9, focused on recognising digital systems and representing ideas with objects, pictures and symbols. Sequencing steps follows in Years 1 and 2.

### Should my child use a tablet or a computer?

A laptop or desktop with a mouse is easier for Scratch. Tablets are fine for picture-based apps, but dragging small blocks with a finger frustrates many young children.

### Do I need to know how to code to help?

Not at all. Playing the arrow-card game, asking your child to explain their program and celebrating fixed mistakes help more than any technical knowledge.

### How long should lessons be for a 6 year old?

Shorter and livelier than for older children, with frequent changes of activity. The teacher adjusts to your child's attention on the day.

### What does it cost?

The first lesson is free and asks for no card. Group places and one-to-one lessons each have a monthly fee, listed under Fees above, with no sign-up charge.
