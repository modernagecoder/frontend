# Fryer, Galois and Hypatia contests guide, 2027

The Fryer, Galois and Hypatia Contests are the University of Waterloo's full-solution papers for Grades 9, 10 and 11, written on the same April morning by tens of thousands of students in and beyond Canada. Each paper is short, four questions in seventy-five minutes, but every question is a small staircase: quick boxed answers at the bottom, a written argument at the top. This guide is built from CEMC's contest page for 2027, the covers of the 2025 booklets and CEMC's 2026 results booklet. It explains how one 10-mark question is put together, how far students climbed on each question in 2026, a rubric of ours for marking a written part, one complete practice question of ours for each contest, and a plan from November to Wednesday, April 7, 2027.

CEMC answers a common question directly. A Grade 9 student taking a Grade 10 math course is still eligible for the Fryer, the contest for their own grade.

## How one 10-mark question is put together

The three 2025 booklet covers are identical apart from the contest name and grade. Read together, their rules describe the inside of a single question better than any summary.

**From the covers of the 2025 Fryer, Galois and Hypatia booklets (rules only, no problems)**

| Rule on the cover | What it means for a student |
|---|---|
| "Number of questions: 4" and "Each question is worth 10 marks" | Four equal questions; the paper is out of 40 |
| Short answer parts are "worth 2 or 3 marks each" | The early parts of a question are small, quick and boxed |
| A correct boxed answer earns the part in full | A boxed part needs the answer, not an essay |
| Part marks on a boxed part need relevant working in the space given | A line of working protects a boxed part from a slip |
| Full solution parts carry whatever the boxed parts leave of the 10 | The written parts carry most of each question |
| Presentation is marked: a right answer, badly written, falls short of full marks | A right answer with a missing reason is an incomplete answer |

Put those rules together and a question looks like a staircase of three or four steps. The bottom steps are short and boxed, worth 2 or 3 marks each. The top step, sometimes two, asks for a written argument and carries the rest. CEMC's own overview describes the papers as problems that "progressively lead to more complex questions", and in our experience that is literal: the earlier parts usually hand the student a fact or a method that the last part needs. A student who treats each part as a separate puzzle works harder than one who asks, at every step, what the previous part just gave them.

### Reading a question from the top

Before writing anything, read the last part first. Knowing where the question is going makes the early parts look like preparation, which is what they usually are.

Then do the boxed parts quickly and cleanly, keeping a note of any result that seems likely to come back.

### Seventy-five minutes for four

Our budget: questions 1 and 2 in about 30 minutes together, then 20 minutes each on questions 3 and 4, with 5 minutes left to reread every written part.

This budget is ours, not CEMC's. Its purpose is to stop a student finishing question 3 perfectly and never reading question 4, whose first parts are often the easiest marks left on the paper.

## Three full questions of ours, one for each paper, solved completely

Each question below was written by our teachers for our students. None comes from a CEMC paper or is modelled on one. Each has the same staircase shape as an April question, and each is followed by a note on how its parts feed one another. All answers were checked in Python.

**Fryer-style · our question and our complete solution** (text)
```text
Fryer-style question (ours). A school bake sale packs cookies
in boxes of 6 and boxes of 10. A customer buys whole boxes only.

(a) [2 marks, short answer] Show how to buy exactly 46 cookies.
(b) [2 marks, short answer] In how many ways can exactly 60 cookies
    be bought?
(c) [2 marks, full solution] Explain why an odd number of cookies
    can never be bought.
(d) [4 marks, full solution] Determine, with justification, the
    largest even number of cookies that cannot be bought.

(a) One box of 6 and four boxes of 10: 6 + 40 = 46.

(b) We need 6a + 10b = 60, that is 3a + 5b = 30, with a, b >= 0.
    b = 0 gives a = 10; b = 3 gives a = 5; b = 6 gives a = 0.
    Other values of b from 0 to 6 leave 30 - 5b not a multiple
    of 3. So there are 3 ways.

(c) 6a + 10b = 2(3a + 5b), which is even for every a and b.
    So an odd number of cookies is impossible.

(d) 14 cannot be bought. With no 10-box we need 6a = 14,
    impossible. With one 10-box we need 6a = 4, impossible.
    Two or more 10-boxes already give more than 14.

    Every even number from 16 up can be bought.
    16 = 6 + 10,   18 = 6 + 6 + 6,   20 = 10 + 10.
    Adding one more box of 6 to any of these gives the next
    three even numbers, 22, 24 and 26, and repeating this
    reaches every even number from 16 upward.

    Therefore the largest even number of cookies that cannot
    be bought is 14.
```
Part (c) is the key to part (d): once every purchase is known to be even, the search in (d) only has to look at even numbers. Part (b) quietly practises the same equation that (d) uses. The mark split shown is ours, chosen to resemble the cover's rules; CEMC sets its own split on each paper.

**Galois-style · our question and our complete solution** (text)
```text
Galois-style question (ours). The parabola y = x^2 - 4x + k,
where k is a real number.

(a) [2 marks] If k = 3, what are the x-intercepts?
(b) [3 marks] For what value of k does the parabola touch
    the x-axis at exactly one point?
(c) [5 marks, full solution] The line y = 2x - 5 meets the
    parabola at two points whose x-coordinates differ by 4.
    Determine k and the two points.

(a) x^2 - 4x + 3 = (x - 1)(x - 3), so the intercepts are x = 1, x = 3.

(b) x^2 - 4x + k = (x - 2)^2 + (k - 4). This touches the x-axis
    exactly once when k - 4 = 0, so k = 4.

(c) At an intersection, x^2 - 4x + k = 2x - 5,
    so x^2 - 6x + (k + 5) = 0.
    By the quadratic formula,
        x = (6 +/- sqrt(36 - 4(k + 5))) / 2 = 3 +/- sqrt(4 - k).
    The two x-coordinates differ by 2 sqrt(4 - k).
    Setting 2 sqrt(4 - k) = 4 gives sqrt(4 - k) = 2, so k = 0.
    Then x = 5 or x = 1, and on the line y = 2x - 5 these give
    the points (5, 5) and (1, -3).
    Check on the parabola y = x^2 - 4x: 25 - 20 = 5 and 1 - 4 = -3.

    Therefore k = 0, and the points are (5, 5) and (1, -3).
```
Part (b) completes the square and part (c) uses the quadratic formula, which is completing the square in general form. A student who sees that link solves (c) calmly. The check on the parabola at the end costs ten seconds and catches arithmetic slips.

**Hypatia-style · our question and our complete solution** (text)
```text
Hypatia-style question (ours). For a two-digit positive integer n,
R(n) is the number formed by reversing its digits,
so R(52) = 25 and R(40) = 4.

(a) [2 marks] What is 52 - R(52)?
(b) [3 marks, full solution] Prove that n - R(n) is a multiple
    of 9 for every two-digit n.
(c) [5 marks, full solution] Determine how many two-digit n
    make n - R(n) a positive perfect square.

(a) 52 - 25 = 27.

(b) Write n = 10a + b, where a is the tens digit (1 to 9) and
    b is the units digit (0 to 9). Then R(n) = 10b + a, and
        n - R(n) = 9a - 9b = 9(a - b),
    which is a multiple of 9.

(c) By (b), we need 9(a - b) to be a positive perfect square.
    If 9m = s^2 for a positive integer m, then 3 divides s, so
    s = 3t and m = t^2. Conversely 9t^2 = (3t)^2. So 9(a - b) is a
    perfect square exactly when a - b is a perfect square.
    Since 1 <= a - b <= 9, a - b is 1, 4 or 9.
      a - b = 1: a = 1 to 9, so 9 numbers (10, 21, ..., 98).
      a - b = 4: a = 4 to 9, so 6 numbers (40, 51, ..., 95).
      a - b = 9: only a = 9, b = 0, so 1 number (90).

    Therefore 9 + 6 + 1 = 16 two-digit numbers work.
```
Part (b) does the algebra that turns part (c) from a search through 90 numbers into a question about the digit difference a - b. The small lemma about 9m being a square is the line many students skip; without it the count is a guess.

### A common thread

In all three, the written part is easier than it looks once the earlier parts are taken seriously. The paper is designed that way, and students who expect it find question 4 less frightening.

Try each question under time: 20 minutes for the Fryer-style one, 18 for the Galois-style and 18 for the Hypatia-style, then compare with ours line by line.

### If a student is stuck on the last part

Write down what the earlier parts proved, in a sentence each. On our three questions, that sentence is most of the final answer.

Then try a small case on scrap paper. CEMC's covers ask for rough work to stay out of the finished solution, so the small case belongs on the rough page, not the answer booklet.

## Our rubric for a written part, using the cookie question

This is the rubric our teachers use in class for part (d) of the Fryer-style question above. It is ours, not CEMC's marking scheme, but it follows the three qualities CEMC names on every cover: completeness, clarity and presentation.

**Our 4-mark rubric for part (d) of the Fryer-style question (ours)**

| Mark | Earned for | Commonly lost because |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Showing 14 cannot be bought, case by case | The student says "14 does not work" with no cases |
| 1 | Showing 16, 18 and 20 can be bought | Only one or two of the three are shown |
| 1 | Explaining why every larger even number then works | The student lists 22, 24, 26, 28 and writes "and so on" |
| 1 | A closing sentence that answers the question | The answer 14 appears only inside the working |

### What the rubric teaches

A largest-impossible question always has two halves: the number really is impossible, and everything above it really is possible. Students who prove only one half lose half the marks.

The phrase "and so on" is the most expensive two words on an FGH paper. It asks the marker to finish the argument, and markers do not.

### Using it at home

Ask your child to mark their own attempt against the four rows before reading our solution. Self-marking against a rubric is a skill in itself, and it transfers to every written contest.

In our classes, students swap attempts and mark each other's work with the same table before the teacher marks both.

## Short boxed questions to start a practice session

These are our practice problems, not CEMC problems, at the level of the first boxed part of an April question. Each should take two or three minutes.

Warm-up 1. Our practice problem, not a CEMC problem. What is the smaller angle between the hour hand and the minute hand of a clock at 3:30?

Solution. At 3:30 the minute hand points at the 6, which is 180 degrees from 12. The hour hand has moved halfway from the 3 to the 4: the 3 is at 90 degrees and each hour mark is 30 degrees, so the hour hand is at 90 + 15 = 105 degrees. The angle between them is 180 - 105 = 75 degrees. Answers of 90 forget that the hour hand moves.

Warm-up 2. Our practice problem, not a CEMC problem. How many of the integers from 1 to 100 are perfect squares or perfect cubes, or both?

Solution. There are 10 squares (1 to 100) and 4 cubes (1, 8, 27, 64). Two numbers, 1 and 64, are both, so they were counted twice. The total is 10 + 4 - 2 = 12.

Warm-up 3. Our practice problem, not a CEMC problem. The mean of five consecutive odd integers is 21. What is the largest of them?

Solution. Five consecutive odd integers are spaced 2 apart, so their mean is the middle one, 21. The five are 17, 19, 21, 23 and 25, and the largest is 25. No equation is needed once the symmetry is seen.

Warm-ups like these are where a 2-mark box is won or lost. In class we set three at the start of every session, timed, before any written work, so that the habit of exact, quick boxed answers is practised as often as the longer writing.

## From November 2026 to the April 7, 2027 papers, in five stages

Our plan for a Grade 9 to 11 student starting in November, with two live classes a week and one written part handed in every week from the first class.

## Four steps up an FGH staircase

Our stages, used in the first class to place a student.

**From the first box to the final part (ours)**

| Stage | What a paper shows |
|---|---|
| 1. First steps | Boxed parts of questions 1 and 2 correct; written parts left as a bare answer |
| 2. Two questions | Questions 1 and 2 complete, with written parts that give reasons |
| 3. Lower steps | Boxed parts of questions 3 and 4 collected as well |
| 4. Top step | A written final part on question 3 or 4, complete and clearly argued |

### For parents

Most capable students begin at stage 1, because school math rarely asks a Grade 9 student to explain why something must be true. Moving to stage 2 is usually a matter of weeks; stages 3 and 4 take months of steady writing.
The FGH papers are a gentle introduction to proof. Students who learn it here meet the Euclid and university mathematics with an advantage.

### For students

Before the last part of any question, write down what the earlier parts gave you.
Never write "and so on". Say exactly why the pattern continues.

## Fryer, Galois and Hypatia: frequently asked

### What are the Fryer, Galois and Hypatia Contests?

They are CEMC's full-solution math contests from the University of Waterloo: the Fryer for Grade 9, the Galois for Grade 10 and the Hypatia for Grade 11. Each has four questions, takes 75 minutes and is scored out of 40.

### When are the Fryer, Galois and Hypatia Contests in 2027?

CEMC lists Wednesday, April 7, 2027 in North and South America and Thursday, April 8, 2027 elsewhere. Schools must order by Thursday, March 11, 2027.

### How is each question marked?

Every question is worth 10 marks. On the 2025 papers, short answer parts were worth 2 or 3 marks each, with full marks for a correct boxed answer, and full solution parts carried the rest of the question, marked for completeness, clarity and style.

### Can my child write more than one of the three?

No. CEMC allows only one of the Fryer, Galois and Hypatia Contests per student in a school year. A student working ahead may choose a contest above their grade instead of their own.

### What were the 2026 averages?

CEMC lists 2026 means of 21.7 for the Fryer, 21.0 for the Galois and 22.8 for the Hypatia, each out of 40, with Certificate of Distinction cutoffs of 28, 26 and 28. Question 4 averaged 1.1 out of 10 on the Galois and 1.3 on the Hypatia.

### How is FGH different from Pascal, Cayley and Fermat?

Pascal, Cayley and Fermat are 60-minute multiple-choice papers in February. The Fryer, Galois and Hypatia are written papers in April, where most marks come from explaining a solution. Many students write one of each in the same school year.

### How do we enter?

Through the school. CEMC takes orders only from schools, and students write at school under supervision. If your school is not taking part, CEMC suggests asking another local school whether your child can write with its students.

### Are calculators permitted?

Some are. CEMC permits calculators that cannot go online, talk to other devices, hold stored notes or programs, or run algebra or geometry software. A written part still has to show the reasoning on paper.

### Does your course give certificates or guarantee results?

Certificates come from CEMC, based on the paper. We teach the mathematics and the writing, and we make no promise of a score, certificate or medal.
