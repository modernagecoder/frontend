## Can a tutor help with Art of Problem Solving books?

Yes. Many of our students keep their AoPS book as the main text and use our classes to talk through the problems they are stuck on, check their write-ups and fill gaps. We never do the problems for them.

**How a session alongside an AoPS-style book runs (our method)**

| Part of the hour | What happens |
|---|---|
| First 10 minutes | The student shows a problem they could not finish and explains how far they got |
| Next 20 minutes | Hints, one at a time, until the student finds the idea themselves |
| Next 20 minutes | Our own problem using the same idea, solved without hints |
| Last 10 minutes | The student writes a clean solution and names the idea in one sentence |

### What we will and will not do

We give hints, ask questions and teach the idea behind a problem. We do not hand over worked solutions to book exercises, because finding them is the whole value of a problem-solving book.

We also never copy pages or problems from any AoPS book into our materials. Our practice problems are written by our teachers.

## How do you break a hard problem into familiar parts?

AoPS puts its own method in one line: it "trains students to approach hard problems by breaking them down into familiar parts." Here is how we teach that idea, with three worked examples written by us.

1. Name what is unfamiliar. What makes this problem feel new? Usually it is size (huge numbers), shape (an odd figure) or wording.
2. Find the familiar core. Strip it down: a smaller case, a simpler shape, a known formula hiding inside.
3. Solve the small version. Get a sure answer for the easy case and look at how you got it.
4. Rebuild. Apply the same steps to the real problem.
5. Check with a second view. A different method, or a case you can count by hand.

Example 1 (ours): a big sum. What is 1 + 2 + 3 + ... + 50? Unfamiliar: fifty terms. Familiar core: 1 + 2 + 3 + 4, which is 10, and also (1 + 4) + (2 + 3), two pairs of 5. So pair the ends: 1 + 50, 2 + 49, and so on, 25 pairs of 51, total 1,275. Check with a small case: 1 to 4 gives 2 pairs of 5, which is 10. Correct.

Example 2 (ours): an odd shape. How many diagonals does a hexagon have? Unfamiliar: drawing them all gets messy. Familiar core: each corner connects to every other corner except itself and its two neighbors, so each of the 6 corners sends out 3 diagonals. That counts 18 ends, but each diagonal has two ends, so there are 9. Check by drawing a square: each corner sends 1, 4 ends, 2 diagonals. Correct.

Example 3 (ours): wording that hides algebra. The sum of three consecutive whole numbers is 87. What is the largest? Unfamiliar: three unknowns. Familiar core: the middle number is the average, so it is 87 / 3 = 29, and the numbers are 28, 29 and 30. The largest is 30. Check: 28 + 29 + 30 = 87. The familiar part was "average of evenly spaced numbers is the middle one".

### Why this transfers

The three examples share no topic: a sum, a shape and a sentence. They share a move, and that move is what lets a student face a problem they have never seen on a contest.

This is why problem-solving programs of every kind, AoPS included, focus on method more than on any list of topics.

### How we practice it

Before solving, students must say what the familiar core is. Only then may they calculate.

At the end, they write the core in one sentence. Over a term those sentences become their own toolkit.

## Ten original problem-solving practice problems

Written by our teachers at about the AMC 8 to early AMC 10 level. They are not from any AoPS book, class or Alcumus, and not from any contest. Find the familiar core first.

1. What is the sum of the odd numbers from 1 to 99?
2. How many diagonals does an octagon have?
3. Five consecutive whole numbers add to 115. What is the smallest?
4. How many trailing zeros does 25 x 24 x 23 x ... x 2 x 1 have?
5. What is the ones digit of 7 multiplied by itself 2026 times?
6. How many paths of length 4 go from the bottom-left to the top-right corner of a 2 by 2 grid of squares, moving only right or up along grid lines?
7. A rectangle has area 48 and perimeter 28. What are its side lengths?
8. What is 99 squared, without a calculator?
9. How many positive whole numbers less than 100 are divisible by 6 but not by 4?
10. The mean of a list of 10 numbers is 12. Adding one number makes the mean 13. What number was added?

**Answers and the familiar core of each (ours; checked in Python)**

| No. | Answer | Familiar core |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2,500 | The first n odd numbers add to n squared; here n is 50 |
| 2 | 20 | Each of 8 corners sends 5 diagonals; 40 ends, halved |
| 3 | 21 | The middle number is 115 / 5 = 23 |
| 4 | 6 | Count factors of 5: 5, 10, 15, 20 give one each, 25 gives two |
| 5 | 9 | Ones digits cycle 7, 9, 3, 1; 2026 leaves remainder 2 on division by 4 |
| 6 | 6 | Choose which 2 of the 4 moves are right |
| 7 | 6 and 8 | Two numbers with sum 14 and product 48 |
| 8 | 9,801 | (100 minus 1) squared = 10,000 minus 200 + 1 |
| 9 | 8 | 16 multiples of 6 below 100, minus the 8 multiples of 12 |
| 10 | 23 | Totals 120 and 143; the difference |

### Where students slip

Problem 4 is usually answered 5, forgetting that 25 holds two factors of 5. Problem 9 tempts students to subtract multiples of 4 rather than of 12, the numbers divisible by both 6 and 4.

Problem 6 can be done by drawing every path, which is a fine check; the counting idea makes the 3 by 3 version easy too.

### Then harder material

Students who finish these comfortably are ready for full contest-length sets. We set those from our own problem bank in class.

Students using AoPS books can bring the chapter they are on, and we will match the practice to it.

## Four stages of a problem solver

Our own description of how students grow on hard problems, whichever program they use.

**From stuck to fluent (our stages)**

| Stage | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1. Tries | Writes down a small case instead of waiting |
| 2. Simplifies | Finds a familiar core without prompting |
| 3. Proves | Explains why an answer must be right |
| 4. Extends | Asks what happens with a bigger or different version |

### For parents

Struggle is part of the method. A child stuck for ten minutes on a good problem is not failing; they are doing the work.
Ask your child to explain one problem a week to you. If they can, the idea has stuck.

### For students

When a problem looks huge, try it with small numbers first. The pattern often appears by the third case.
Write the familiar core in your notebook after every solved problem.
