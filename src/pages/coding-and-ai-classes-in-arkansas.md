# Coding and AI classes in Arkansas

**Do Arkansas students need computer science to graduate?** Yes. Under Act 414 of 2021, every Arkansas student starting with the 9th grade class of 2022-2023 must earn one full high school computer science credit, which can be earned in grades 8 to 12. Modern Age Coders teaches live online classes that run alongside that credit, from middle school game coding to Python data work, cybersecurity and AP Computer Science Principles, in batches or 1-on-1.

Arkansas built its computer science policy in layers. A 2015 law made every public high school offer at least one course; a 2021 law turned that offer into a graduation credit for every student and required a certified computer science teacher in every high school. The result shows in the published numbers: the share of Arkansas high schools with a course reached 100%, and a quarter of high school students were enrolled in 2024 to 2025. Below we set out the rules as the Arkansas Department of Education publishes them, the state's course pathways, the figures, and a data project that ranks the trails in the state's own open data.

## What Act 414 of 2021 requires, as the department explains it

From the Arkansas Department of Education Commissioner's Memo COM-21-105, dated March 29, 2021, read on 17 October 2026.

**The Computer Science Education Advancement Act of 2021 (Act 414), summarized from ADE memo COM-21-105**

| Rule | What the memo says |
|---|---|
| Signed into law | March 23, 2021, as an extension of Act 187 of 2015, which required every high school to make a computer science course available |
| Graduation credit | Every student, beginning with the 9th grade class of 2022-2023, must earn one full high school computer science credit to graduate |
| When it can be earned | Any time in grades 8 to 12; the credit is found on the transcript by course codes beginning 465 or 565 |
| Flex credit | A computer science credit can count toward the existing fourth math, third science or career focus credit requirements |
| Teachers | Each high school must employ a computer science certified teacher by the 2023-2024 school year |
| Eighth grade | On March 11, 2021 the State Board approved nine computer science and computing courses that districts may teach to 8th graders for high school credit |

### Not an extra credit

The memo says the requirement does not necessarily increase the number of credits a diploma needs, because a computer science course can also fill a math, science or career focus slot.

### The 2015 foundation

State law, A.C.A. 6-16-146, has required every public and public charter high school to offer at least one computer science course since the 2015 to 2016 school year, in class, blended or online.

### Four ways to certify

The memo lists licensure codes 528, 5013, 5014 and 5016 as meeting the teacher requirement, and the department funds seven statewide specialists to train teachers.

So the first Arkansas students who had to hold the credit entered ninth grade in fall 2022 and finished high school in spring 2026. Families with younger children can plan for it from middle school, since a credit-bearing course taken in eighth grade counts.

## Ranking Arkansas trails: why "the longest" depends on what you count

The Arkansas GIS Office publishes a statewide trails layer, compiled for the state parks and natural heritage agencies. Students measure every line and rank them, then rank them a second way and compare.

**Longest single records in the Arkansas Trails layer (our haversine lengths in miles, from a download on 18 October 2026)**

| Rank | Trail record | Our length |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ouachita Trail (Talimena to Queen Wilhelmina) | 156.08 |
| 2 | Delta Heritage Trail | 86.09 |
| 3 | Ouachita Trail (Hwy 7 to Hwy 9) | 30.06 |
| 4 | Ouachita Trail (Hwy 9 to Pinnacle Mountain) | 27.74 |
| 5 | Hidden Diversity Multi-Use Trail | 20.43 |

### 1. Get every record

The service hands out 200 records per request, but its count query says 384. Students page through with an offset until they hold all 384, then check for repeats. Ranking a partial download ranks the wrong list.

### 2. Measure, then sort

Each trail is a polyline of coordinates. Summing haversine distances between consecutive points gives a length; sorting descending and slicing the first five gives a top-five list, and 308 of the 384 records turn out to be under one mile.

### 3. Rank again by name

Group by trail name first and the order changes. River Corridor Trail is 12.46 miles as one record but 24.41 miles across 6 records, and Water Trail adds up to 23.19 miles from 23 short pieces.

### Our miles, not the state's

These lengths are ours, measured from the layer's geometry on one day. The layer carries no licence text and says its completeness depends on the resources available when it was produced, so the figures are a teaching result, not official trail mileage. The Ouachita Trail also appears as several records, so "longest trail" and "longest record" are different questions.

A third exercise hides in the surface_type column, which mixes words such as Natural, Gravel and Asphalt with bare digits such as 2 and 6: 199 records simply say "2". Without the codebook, a student cannot rank trails by surface, and saying so is the right answer. That is the Data Analytics and Machine Learning pathway in miniature: measure carefully, rank honestly, and refuse to invent meanings.

## Arkansas teaches AI as a course. Here is what using it well looks like.

The state lists Artificial Intelligence in its Data Analytics and Machine Learning pathway. Judgment is the part a tool cannot supply.

Ask an AI assistant for "the longest trail in Arkansas" from this layer and it may sort the records and name the first one, never noticing that one trail is split across several records or that its lengths depend on the projection used to measure them.

A student who has written the ranking code knows to ask what is being ranked and how it was measured. That habit transfers to every AI output they will see: check the unit, check the source, check whether the question asked is the question answered.

Arkansas made sure every student meets computer science; the students who get the most from it learn to question a confident answer. The longer argument is in [why we still teach coding in an AI world](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).

## Arkansas computer science questions

### Which Arkansas students need a computer science credit?

According to ADE memo COM-21-105, every student beginning with the 9th grade class of 2022-2023 must earn one full high school computer science credit to graduate.

### Can an eighth grader earn the credit?

Yes. The memo says the credit may be earned in grades 8 to 12, and the State Board approved nine courses that districts may teach to 8th graders for high school credit.

### Does the credit add to the total a diploma needs?

Not necessarily. A computer science flex credit can count toward the fourth math, third science or career focus requirement, so it can fill an existing slot.

### Do all Arkansas high schools offer computer science?

Code.org (2025) reports 301 of 301 Arkansas high schools offering it in 2024 to 2025, and state law has required at least one course in every public high school since 2015 to 2016.

### How many Arkansas students take computer science?

For 2024 to 2025 the Code.org sheet reports 37,330 of 148,457 high school students, or 25.15%, up from 7.2% in 2020 to 2021.

### Will your classes count as the Arkansas credit?

No. The credit comes from state-approved school courses with codes beginning 465 or 565. Our classes add depth and practice alongside them.
