Student hackathons in Australia

A hackathon squeezes a whole software project into a weekend: form a team, pick a problem, build something that works, and show it to judges before the clock runs out. Lists of "student hackathons" online are often out of date, and many events have age or enrolment rules that rule out school students. This page sets out what the main Australian events say about themselves, who can actually enter, what school-age students can do instead, and the single build habit that most often decides whether a team has a demo at the end. Every detail was checked at the organiser on 7 October 2026. We are independent of all of them.

UNIHACK lists four judging criteria: polish, design and execution; technical difficulty; originality and creativity; and what it calls the wow factor. It asks whether the project actually works and whether it does more than put a pretty front end on an existing service.

UNIHACK says everyone is welcome whether just starting or experienced, and that graduates who have finished but not yet had a graduation ceremony are still eligible.

GovHack encourages mixed teams: entrepreneurs, developers, designers, digital media creators, artists, storytellers, researchers and open data enthusiasts. Categories are announced on the Friday night and teams work through the weekend with official data sets.

On its home page GovHack says it is taking time to refresh the event after sixteen years, which is why no 2026 event is listed. Students interested in open data can still explore government data portals in the meantime.

Universities and schools also run their own one-day coding events and holiday programs. They change often, so check each host's own page for age limits, supervision and dates.

For a student under 18, an event's rules on age, supervision and parental consent matter as much as its prize list. Read the code of conduct before registering; UNIHACK and GovHack both publish one.

A school-age student who learns to build a small working thing in a short time will be well placed for UNIHACK or GovHack later, once old enough or enrolled.

Worked lesson

The walking skeleton: something that works, first

The most common way to lose a hackathon is to build three impressive parts that never join up. A walking skeleton avoids that: the thinnest possible version of the whole project, working end to end, built before anything else. The two teams below are invented to show the idea.

What a skeleton is

Every layer of the project exists in its simplest form and is connected: a screen that takes input, code that does one real step with it, and a visible result. It is ugly but it runs.

Why first

Joining parts is where hidden problems live: data in the wrong format, a service that rejects requests, a page that cannot reach the code. A skeleton finds them in hour five, not hour forty.

Then thicken

Once the skeleton walks, every hour adds muscle to a working body. If time runs out, the team still has a demo; it is just thinner than planned.

Two invented teams in a 48-hour hackathon building an app that suggests a study timetable
Hours inTeam A: build parts separatelyTeam B: skeleton first
0 to 4Idea chosen; each member starts their own partIdea chosen; one-sentence demo script written
4 to 8Login screen, database and scheduling code under waySkeleton: type one subject, get one suggested slot on screen
8 to 30Each part grows, tested aloneReal scheduling rules added to the working path
30 to 40First attempt to join the parts; formats do not matchSecond and third subjects, a calendar view, clean-up
40 to 46Fixing the joins; no time to rehearseFeature freeze, testing, pitch rehearsed twice
46 to 48Demo crashes on one pathDemo runs the scripted path smoothly

Team A was not less talented; it simply discovered its biggest risk last. Team B wrote the demo script at the start, which told it exactly which path through the app had to work, and built that path before adding anything else. The same approach works at school scale: in a six-hour classroom build, the skeleton should run within the first hour. A useful rule of thumb is a feature freeze two hours before the end of a 48-hour event, after which only fixes and rehearsal are allowed.

Practising it before an event

Run a two-hour mini hackathon at home: pick a tiny idea, write a one-sentence demo, and build the skeleton in the first thirty minutes. Repeat monthly with different ideas until starting this way feels natural.

Keep a short list of starters a team knows how to use quickly, such as a basic web page that calls a function, so the skeleton does not burn the first hours.

What judges see

Criteria such as UNIHACK's "does it actually work" reward the skeleton habit directly. A modest project that runs during judging usually lands better than an ambitious one that does not.

The habit also suits project competitions like Young ICT Explorers, where judges welcome prototypes but need to see a working core.

Getting ready

How we prepare students for team builds

Hackathon readiness is a mix of coding fluency, teamwork and calm under time pressure. Each can be practised.

Lessons cover quick prototyping in Python and JavaScript, calling a public data source or API, building a simple web or mobile front end, splitting work across a team without losing track, and pitching a project in a few minutes.

Students practise in short timed builds with the skeleton rule from the first minute, then review what slowed them down.

We do not build projects for teams at events, and we follow each event's rules on outside help. Hackathon work belongs to the team that builds it during the event.

We make no promise of a prize or a placing. We teach students to ship something that works, which is the part they control.

Questions about hackathons

What students and parents ask

What is a hackathon?

An event where teams build a working software project from scratch in a fixed time, often a weekend, and present it to judges at the end.

What is a walking skeleton?

The thinnest version of a whole project that works end to end, from input to visible result, built before any other feature so the team always has something to demonstrate.

Can school students enter UNIHACK?

No. UNIHACK says entrants must be 18 or older and studying at a university or TAFE in Australia or New Zealand at the time of the event.

When is the next UNIHACK?

UNIHACK lists March 12 to 14, 2027 for its next event, a 48-hour hybrid hackathon, with pre-registration open.

Is GovHack running in 2026?

GovHack's site says it is refreshing the event after sixteen years and will share what is coming in 2027, so no 2026 event is listed.

What can under-18s do instead?

Project competitions and workshops written for school years, such as Young ICT Explorers, RoboCup Junior Australia, FIRST LEGO League and Girls' Programming Network workshops.

Do you need to be an expert coder?

No. Both UNIHACK and GovHack welcome mixed experience and mixed skills, including design and storytelling. Being able to build a small thing quickly helps most.

Will you help my team during the event?

No. We prepare students beforehand. During an event, the work belongs to the team and each event's rules on outside help apply.

How long does preparation take?

Our eight-week build-up suits a first event: a starter project, using an API, team habits, a mini hackathon and pitch practice.

What should a team decide first at a hackathon?

The demo: one sentence describing exactly what will be shown working at the end. Everything else follows from that path.
