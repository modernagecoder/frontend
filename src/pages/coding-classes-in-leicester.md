## Majority and largest are not the same predicate

Every market page here works through one analytical trap. Leicester's is a logic error that also teaches something real about writing functions.

The learner gets the 2021 column from the table above and one instruction: write a function that answers whether any group holds a majority. Most people write something that finds the biggest number and returns its name.

That function answers a different question, and on this data the two answers disagree.

The trap

One predicate needs a single number, the other needs the whole list

Majority means more than half. It is a test you can run on one value with nothing else in front of you: is 43.40 greater than 50? No. Done. Plurality, which is what most people mean when they say largest, cannot be tested that way at all. You have to see every other value to know whether the one in your hand is the biggest. One predicate is local, the other is global, and a learner who has felt that difference while writing two functions will never conflate them again.

On Leicester, Asian or Asian British at 43.40 per cent is the plurality and there is no majority, so a program that reports the largest group as though it had answered the majority question is confidently wrong. The same shape of error shows up everywhere. A winning candidate is not necessarily a majority. A most-common cause is not necessarily most causes. The best-selling item may be under a fifth of sales.

Then a second pass, on the same table. The learner is asked which sentence about the White population between 2011 and 2021 is correct: the share fell, the count fell, or the city grew. All three, and each was written up somewhere as the story. The output they end up producing prints all three side by side and refuses to pick, because picking one is editorial rather than arithmetic.

Every figure in this exercise is published census data, quoted above with its source. No individual, school or neighbourhood is described, and nothing about any group is inferred beyond what the arithmetic supports.

## Does coding still matter when AI can write it?

Try the exact question from the project on any assistant. Give it Leicester's figures and ask which group is the majority. A good one will catch it and tell you none of them is. A less careful one will name the largest group, because the question sounded like it wanted a name and a name was available.

You cannot tell which you have got without already knowing the answer. That is the whole problem with fluent output, and it does not improve as the fluency improves. It gets harder.

What remains for a person is knowing that majority and largest are separate tests, that one needs the whole list, and that a table with shares and counts and a total in it can support three contradictory headlines. Those are habits of precision. They are taught by writing small programs about real numbers and being wrong in front of somebody who notices, which is what a class is for.
