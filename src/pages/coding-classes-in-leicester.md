**One city, three true sentences, three different stories.** The White share fell by more than nine percentage points. The White count fell by roughly sixteen thousand. The city as a whole grew by nearly thirty-nine thousand people. Every one is correct and each supports a different headline, which is why the honest way to read a table like this is to look at shares, counts and total together and refuse to write a sentence until all three agree with it.

The most quoted fact about Leicester is that no ethnic group holds a majority. True: the largest group, Asian or Asian British, is 43.40 per cent, and a majority needs more than half. Leicester is one of a small number of English cities in that position, alongside Birmingham, London and Manchester.

## The Leicester student data project

Every market page here works through one analytical trap. Leicester's is a logic error that also teaches something real about writing functions: **majority and largest are not the same predicate.**

The learner gets the 2021 column and one instruction: write a function that answers whether any group holds a majority. Most people write something that finds the biggest number and returns its name. That function answers a different question, and on this data the two answers disagree.

**Majority means more than half.** It is a test you can run on one value with nothing else in front of you: is 43.40 greater than 50? No. Done.

**Plurality**, which is what most people mean by largest, cannot be tested that way at all. You have to see every other value to know whether the one in your hand is the biggest.

One predicate is local, the other is global, and a learner who has felt that difference while writing two functions will never conflate them again. On Leicester, Asian or Asian British at 43.40 per cent is the plurality and there is no majority, so a program reporting the largest group as though it had answered the majority question is confidently wrong.

The same shape of error is everywhere. A winning candidate is not necessarily a majority. A most-common cause is not necessarily most causes. The best-selling item may be under a fifth of sales.

Then a second pass on the same table: which sentence about the White population between 2011 and 2021 is correct, that the share fell, the count fell, or the city grew? All three, and each was written up somewhere as the story. The program they end up producing prints all three side by side and refuses to pick, because picking one is editorial rather than arithmetic.

Every figure is published census data, quoted above with its source. No individual, school or neighbourhood is described, and nothing about any group is inferred beyond what the arithmetic supports.

## Does coding still matter when AI can write it?

Try the exact question from the project on any assistant. Give it Leicester's figures and ask which group is the majority. A good one will catch it and say none of them is. A less careful one will name the largest group, because the question sounded like it wanted a name and a name was available.

**You cannot tell which you have got without already knowing the answer.** That is the whole problem with fluent output, and it does not improve as the fluency improves. It gets harder.

What remains for a person is knowing that majority and largest are separate tests, that one needs the whole list, and that a table with shares and counts and a total in it can support three contradictory headlines.
