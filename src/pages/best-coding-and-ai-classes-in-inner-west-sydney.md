### October as the base

Five of the ten stops sit below 100 in August 2026. Dulwich Hill reads 88.6, a fall of about 11%.

### November as the base

November 2024 was the quietest month at every one of the ten stops. From that base, all ten are above 100, nine of them above 118, and Dulwich Hill reads 123.0.

### Same data

Nothing in the counts changed between the two columns. Only the month set to 100 moved, by four weeks.

**Three ways to measure October 2024 to August 2026 (our calculation)**

| Stop | Fixed base | Monthly ratios multiplied together | Average monthly change, compounded |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hawthorne | 109.5 | 109.5 | 130.5 |
| Marion | 102.9 | 102.9 | 118.4 |
| Dulwich Grove | 90.6 | 90.6 | 101.5 |
| Dulwich Hill | 88.6 | 88.6 | 102.4 |

### Why averaging growth goes wrong

Multiplying the 22 monthly ratios always lands exactly on the fixed-base figure, because the middle months cancel. Averaging the percentage changes first does not: a 20% fall followed by a 20% rise averages to no change at all, yet leaves the count 4% lower than it started. That is how Dulwich Hill appears to grow when it actually fell.

The counts are tapped entries only, so trips with no tap are missing, and the indices, comparisons and the choice of stops are ours. We have not checked which council each stop sits in; they are simply the western end of the L1 line.

## Index numbers in a handful of lines

Learners write the calculation themselves before reaching for a library, so they can see each choice the library would otherwise hide.

**The calculations, written out**

| Idea | Python | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed-base index | [100 * v / counts[base] for v in counts] | A base month that is unusually high or low |
| Chained index | running product of counts[i] / counts[i - 1] | A missing month breaks the chain |
| Averaged growth | mean of the monthly percentage changes | Never compound it to report total growth |

Price indices, school results and company reports all use base periods. The question a careful reader asks is always the same: why this base, and what does the picture look like from another one?

## An AI chart tool picks a base month for you. Does an Inner West student need to care?

Yes, because the tool will not tell you what the other months would have said.

Ask an AI assistant to "show growth at Dulwich Hill" and it will often start the chart from the first month in the file, or from whichever month makes a tidy line. Either choice is defensible. Neither is mentioned in the answer, and the headline can flip from a fall to a rise.

A learner who has built both indices by hand knows to ask which month is 100, to try another, and to multiply ratios rather than average them. Those questions stop a misleading chart before it reaches a report.

In the Inner West or anywhere else, the student who checks the base month is the one whose charts can be trusted. The longer argument is in [why teenagers should still learn to code in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).

### What is an index number?

A way to compare values by setting one period to 100. If a stop had 8,373 entries in the base month and 9,169 later, the later index is 100 times 9,169 divided by 8,373, which is 109.5.

### Why does the base month matter so much?

Because every later value is divided by it. November 2024 was the quietest month at all ten L1 stops we studied, so using it as the base makes every stop look as if it grew strongly.

### Is a chained index different from a fixed-base one?

For a single series with no gaps, no. Multiplying each month's ratio to the month before gives exactly the fixed-base figure. Averaging the monthly percentage changes is what gives a different, wrong answer.
