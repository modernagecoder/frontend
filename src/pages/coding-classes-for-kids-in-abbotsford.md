## Sense, think, act: BC&rsquo;s robotics and digital literacy content for Abbotsford children

Abbotsford schools teach the British Columbia curriculum. In Grades 6 and 7, Applied Design, Skills, and Technologies (ADST) is taught through modules, at least three a year, chosen by the school. Two modules from the ADST 6-7 page, read on curriculum.gov.bc.ca on 22 October 2026, say a good deal about coding. Quotes are the curriculum&rsquo;s; the right column is ours.

- **Robotics**: A robot is &ldquo;a machine capable of carrying out a complex series of actions automatically&rdquo;, and the module includes &ldquo;programming and logic for robotics components&rdquo;. Our answer: Automatically is the key word. We show children that the robot only seems clever because someone wrote every rule it follows, then let them write the rules.
- **Robotics: sense**: The sense parts are &ldquo;the parts of the robot that allow it to gather information about its environment that guides its behaviour&rdquo;. Our answer: In Scratch, a sprite senses with &ldquo;touching colour&rdquo; and key presses. Children build a sprite that stops at a wall, the simplest sense-and-react loop there is.
- **Robotics: think and act**: Think is &ldquo;the part of the robot that determines the robot&rsquo;s behaviour&rdquo;; act is &ldquo;the parts of the robot that do the work&rdquo;. Our answer: We split every project into the same three boxes on paper: what it notices, what it decides, what it does. Most bugs live in the middle box.
- **Digital Literacy**: Learning &ldquo;how search results are selected and ranked&rdquo;, judged for &ldquo;accuracy, timeliness, appropriateness, credibility, and bias&rdquo;. Our answer: Children who have sorted data themselves understand ranking from the inside. Our parks project below sorts and ranks, and we ask who decided what counts as first.

The same ADST page lists visual programming such as Scratch and simple algorithms under its Computational Thinking module, so an Abbotsford child might meet coding through robots, through computational thinking, or not at all in a given year, depending on the modules the school chooses. We have found no primary source for coding in BC Kindergarten to Grade 5 and claim nothing about those grades. Teenagers have Computer Studies 10 and Computer Programming 11 and 12 ahead of them, which our [coding classes in British Columbia](/coding-classes-in-british-columbia) page explains.

## Which Abbotsford neighbourhoods have the most park records? A top five from the City&rsquo;s file

The City of Abbotsford publishes its parks as open data, a file of every park and recreation area with a name, a type and a neighbourhood. Our older kids count records by neighbourhood, sort the counts and print the top five. Every figure below is ours, from the file downloaded on 22 October 2026.

**Park records per neighbourhood in the City of Abbotsford Parks file (column DCC Park Neighbourhood), our count, top five of 21 named neighbourhoods**

| Rank | Neighbourhood as written in the file | Park records |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | TOWNLINE HILL | 21 |
| 2 | UPPER TEN OAKS | 19 |
| 3 | LOWER SUMAS MOUNTAIN | 18 |
| 4 (tied) | MATSQUI PRAIRIE | 15 |
| 4 (tied) | WEST CLEARBROOK | 15 |

The file has 238 records. 43 have no neighbourhood, and they hide a trap: 36 cells are truly empty, but 7 contain a single space. A program that checks only for empty text counts the spaces as a neighbourhood whose name is a space. Calling strip() first removes the space, so all 43 are counted as blank. Here is the program a 12-year-old writes in our class:

```
import csv

counts = {}
blank = 0
with open("abbotsford-parks.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as f:
    for park in csv.DictReader(f):
        hood = park["DCC Park Neighbourhood"].strip()
        if hood == "":
            blank += 1
        else:
            counts[hood] = counts.get(hood, 0) + 1

top = sorted(counts.items(), key=lambda pair: pair[1], reverse=True)
for hood, n in top[:5]:
    print(hood, n)
print("no neighbourhood:", blank)
```

### Sorting by the count

counts.items() gives pairs such as (TOWNLINE HILL, 21). The key function tells sorted to compare the second item of each pair, the number, and reverse puts the biggest first.

### Slicing the top

top[:5] keeps the first five pairs. Two neighbourhoods tie on 15, and children notice that the order between them comes from the file, not from any rule. Ties deserve a mention.

### Records are not parks

Count what the file counts. 42 records are named UNDEVELOPED GREEN SPACE and 30 have no name at all, and there are 181 distinct Park ID values among the 238 records, so a record is not always a separate park.

### Check the units with a second column

One column is labelled Area (m2), square metres. A second column, Shape__Area, holds the area of the drawn shape. For 218 records the drawn area is about 10,000 times the labelled value, which is what you would see if the labelled numbers were hectares, since one hectare is 10,000 square metres. Our reading is that the label is wrong and the values are hectares; 15 records do not fit the pattern. Comparing one column against another is a habit worth more than any single answer.

By type, the file holds 129 open space records, 64 neighbourhood parks, 25 community parks, 15 regional parks and 5 city-wide parks. Some columns record staff account names, and some park names honour people, so in class we print neighbourhoods, types and counts only. The City describes the layer as a &ldquo;collection of all known naturally designated parks and recreation areas in the City of Abbotsford&rdquo;. With Abbotsford at UTC-7 and India at UTC+5:30, India is 12 and a half hours ahead. A 4:00 pm class in Abbotsford is 4:30 am the next day in India, in every month of the year (our arithmetic).

## With AI everywhere, why should an Abbotsford child learn to code?

Because sense, think and act describes AI systems too, and the think part is where mistakes hide.

A program that skipped strip() would have reported a neighbourhood whose name is a space, and looked sure of itself. An AI system can be just as confident about a wrong answer. Children who have found such a bug with their own eyes learn to ask how an answer was produced before they trust it, which is the digital literacy BC asks for.

We keep adult chatbots out of lessons for children under 13. Instead, teacher-led activities show how AI learns from examples and why it fails. Most of each class is the child&rsquo;s own thinking: splitting a problem into steps, trying one, and testing it.

BC puts judging search results for accuracy and bias into Grade 6 and 7 technology, and that judgement grows fastest in children who build things themselves. The longer argument is in [why coding still pays off for kids](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
