# OCR FSMQ Additional Maths 6993 help

OCR's Additional Maths is an unusual qualification: a Level 3 maths exam, the same level as A level, that most students sit in Year 11 alongside GCSE. It is one paper of two hours, it covers differentiation, integration, logarithms and the binomial expansion, and it is graded A to E. Schools offer it to their strongest GCSE students, often in an after-school slot, which is why families looking for help with it find so little. This page sets out the paper and the June 2027 date from OCR, explains the seven topic sections and their codes, describes the "show detailed reasoning" rule that decides whether a calculator answer earns marks, and works three original problems at the level of the paper.

## How is OCR Additional Maths 6993 assessed?

By one written paper, which makes up all of the qualification. The table follows OCR's specification, version 2.0 (June 2026), and its final June 2027 timetable.

**OCR Level 3 FSMQ: Additional Maths 6993 at a glance (OCR specification v2.0 and June 2027 final timetable)**

| Feature | What OCR says |
|---|---|
| Level | Level 3, the same level as AS and A level |
| Assessment | Paper 1 only: one two-hour written exam, with no non-examination assessment |
| Marks | 100, which is 100% of the qualification |
| Calculator | Scientific or graphical calculator permitted |
| Formulae | Given on page 2 of the integrated answer booklet |
| Grades | A, B, C, D, E, where A is the highest; below E is Unclassified |
| Series | One a year, in May/June |
| June 2027 | Friday 18 June 2027, afternoon, 2 hours |
| Teaching time | 30 guided learning hours; total qualification time 60 hours |
| UCAS | Up to 10 UCAS points in the new tariff |
| Qualification number | QN 100/2548/0 |

A few of those rows deserve a second look. Thirty guided learning hours is very little teaching time: OCR adds that this "is only a guide" and that centres "with particularly able candidates could deliver this course considerably faster". The other 30 hours of the total qualification time are independent study, so a student who has one after-school lesson a week is expected to do about as much again at home. And because there is only one series a year, a student who misses or wants to improve on June 2027 waits until the next summer; OCR says "Learners can retake the qualification as many times as they wish."

The paper is the last major maths exam of the summer for most students who take it. In June 2027, GCSE Maths papers fall on 14 May, 27 May and 14 June, and the FSMQ on the afternoon of 18 June, four days after the final GCSE maths paper. That order is helpful: GCSE Higher algebra is fresh, and the FSMQ builds directly on it.

## What topics are in OCR FSMQ Additional Maths?

Seven sections, each with its own two-letter code: algebra underneath, four pure topics with applied twists, and two numerical topics. All of it goes beyond GCSE Higher.

OCR describes the structure in one sentence: the course "consists of four main 'pure' mathematics topics, each of which contains an 'applied' dimension, and two numerical topics, all underpinned by an Algebra section." Every content statement carries a reference such as AL5 or CA11, and an italic statement in square brackets marks content that, in OCR's words, "will not be tested".

**The seven content sections of 6993, with examples of statements from the specification (codes are OCR's; the third column is our note on what is new beyond GCSE Higher)**

| Section and code | Examples of statements | New beyond GCSE Higher |
|---|---|---|
| Algebra (AL) | AL4 linear factors of a polynomial; AL5 completing the square; AL11 recurrence relationships in modelling | Polynomial factor theorem, algebraic fractions and inequalities at greater depth |
| Enumeration (EN) | EN1 binomial expansion; EN4 permutations; EN5 combinations | nPr and nCr notation, the binomial expansion |
| Coordinate Geometry (CG) | CG3 equation of a circle; CG5 tangents and normals; CG8 objective functions | Circles with any centre, linear programming |
| Pythagoras and Trigonometry (PT) | PT2 sine and cosine rules; PT4 the identity sin² + cos² = 1 | Trigonometric identities and equations |
| Calculus (CA) | CA5 stationary points; CA6 their nature; CA11 definite integrals; CA13 area between two curves | Differentiation and integration, entirely new |
| Numerical Methods (NM) | NM2 iteration; NM7 trapezium rule; NM8 over or under estimate | Estimating areas and roots, judging the error |
| Exponentials and Logarithms (EL) | EL2 log as the inverse of a power; EL3 laws of logarithms; EL6 solving ax = b | Logarithms, entirely new |

Calculus and logarithms are the two sections with nothing at all behind them in GCSE, and they take the biggest share of the teaching time in our plan. The others extend GCSE Higher topics: a GCSE student meets the equation of a circle centred on the origin, and 6993 moves the centre; a GCSE student meets tree diagrams, and 6993 adds permutations, combinations and the binomial expansion.

OCR also promises that the paper contains "at least one unstructured problem solving question", one with no parts to lead the student through. Students coming from GCSE often find it the hardest question to start, because GCSE questions are usually broken into steps for them.

## What does "show detailed reasoning" mean in OCR Additional Maths?

It means the calculator can check an answer but cannot replace the method. On those questions a correct answer from a calculator's solve or integrate function does not earn full marks.

The 6993 specification allows any scientific or graphical calculator and adds that "Allowable calculators can be used for any function they can perform." It even expects "an iterative function, such as an ANS key." But some questions carry a printed instruction, "In this question you must show detailed reasoning", and then the rules change. OCR explains that this "is not a restriction on a learner's use of a calculator when tackling the question, e.g. for checking an answer", but it is "a restriction on what will be accepted as evidence of a complete method."

OCR gives two illustrations, which we describe rather than copy. For a logarithm equation, the student must write the step that takes logarithms of both sides, the rearranged form, and an intermediate value; in OCR's words, "Using the solve function on a calculator to skip one of these steps would not result in a complete analytical method." For a definite integral, the student must write the integrated expression and the substitution of the limits; "Just writing down the answer using the definite integral function on a calculator would therefore not be awarded full marks."

**Command words in the 6993 specification and what they require (summarised from OCR's definitions)**

| Command | What it requires |
|---|---|
| Exact | No rounding; keep surds, π or fractions, and rigorous working |
| Show that | The result is given, so every step to it must be shown; a sketch is not enough |
| Determine | Justify any result found, with working where appropriate |
| Give, State, Write down | No working or justification required |
| Hence | Start from the result just found |
| You may use the result | A permitted shortcut, not a required one |
| In this question you must show detailed reasoning | A complete analytical method; the calculator only checks |

The specification has one more calculator rule worth practising: "Correct mathematical notation (rather than 'calculator notation') should be used. Incorrect notation may result in loss of marks." Writing 2.5E-3 instead of 2.5 × 10&minus;3, or "log 80 / log 5" without brackets around a numerator that needs them, are exactly the kinds of slips this rule catches.

## Three 6993-style problems we wrote, with detailed reasoning

Original problems, not OCR questions, from the three sections students meet for the first time. Each is written out the way a "detailed reasoning" question needs.

### Example 1, calculus: stationary points (CA5, CA6)

**Question (ours).** Find the stationary points of y = x³ &minus; 6x² + 9x + 1 and determine their nature.

**Differentiate.** dy/dx = 3x² &minus; 12x + 9 = 3(x² &minus; 4x + 3) = 3(x &minus; 1)(x &minus; 3).

**Solve dy/dx = 0.** x = 1 or x = 3. Then y = 1 &minus; 6 + 9 + 1 = 5 at x = 1, and y = 27 &minus; 54 + 27 + 1 = 1 at x = 3.

**Nature.** d²y/dx² = 6x &minus; 12, which is &minus;6 at x = 1 (a maximum) and 6 at x = 3 (a minimum). So (1, 5) is a maximum and (3, 1) is a minimum.

### Example 2, logarithms with detailed reasoning (EL6)

**Question (ours).** In this question you must show detailed reasoning. Solve 52x &minus; 1 = 80, giving x to 3 significant figures.

**Take logarithms.** log(52x &minus; 1) = log 80, so (2x &minus; 1) log 5 = log 80.

**Rearrange and evaluate.** 2x &minus; 1 = log 80 ÷ log 5 = 2.7227..., so 2x = 3.7227... and x = 1.8613...

**Answer.** x = 1.86 to 3 significant figures. Every line above is needed; a solve function giving 1.86 directly would not show the method.

### Example 3, the binomial expansion (EN1)

**Question (ours).** Find the coefficient of x² in the expansion of (2 + x)5.

**Method.** The general term is 5Cr × 25 &minus; r × xr. For x², take r = 2.

**Working.** 5C2 = 10 and 23 = 8, so the coefficient is 10 × 8 = 80.

**Check.** The coefficients of the whole expansion are 32, 80, 80, 40, 10, 1, and they add to 243, which is 35: the value of (2 + x)5 when x = 1.

### Why these three

Calculus and logarithms have no GCSE foundation, so they are where an FSMQ student needs the most direct teaching. The binomial expansion links the new counting ideas in EN to algebra.

In each example the written method is the answer. That habit carries straight into A level, where "detailed reasoning" questions appear too.

**Practice set (ours, answers at the end).** (1) Differentiate 4x³ &minus; 5x + 2. (2) Evaluate the integral of 3x² + 1 from x = 0 to x = 2, showing the substitution. (3) How many ways can a committee of 3 be chosen from 8 people? (4) Without a calculator, find log2 32. (5) Use the trapezium rule with two strips of width 1 to estimate the area under y = x² from x = 0 to x = 2, and say whether it is an overestimate or an underestimate. **Answers:** (1) 12x² &minus; 5; (2) [x³ + x] from 0 to 2 = (8 + 2) &minus; 0 = 10; (3) 56; (4) 5; (5) ½ × 1 × (0 + 2 × 1 + 4) = 3, an overestimate, because the curve bends upwards between the points (the exact area is 8/3).

## How should a Year 11 student prepare for 6993 alongside GCSE?

Our outline for a student starting in autumn 2026 and sitting the paper on 18 June 2027, with one class a week. OCR's 30 guided learning hours fit easily inside it.

1. **Is it for you? A quick check first.** OCR aims 6993 at students taking GCSE Higher. Before starting, a student should be secure on GCSE Higher algebra: expanding, factorising quadratics, completing the square, solving simultaneous equations.
2. **Autumn 2026: algebra and coordinate geometry.** AL and CG first, because every other section uses them. Polynomials and the factor theorem, circles with any centre, tangents and normals.
3. **December 2026 to February 2027: calculus.** Differentiation, stationary points and their nature, then integration and areas. This is the largest new block.
4. **February to March 2027: logarithms and enumeration.** The laws of logarithms, solving ax = b, permutations, combinations and the binomial expansion.
5. **March 2027: trigonometry and numerical methods.** Identities, the trapezium rule and iteration, including when a method may fail.
6. **April to early May 2027: past papers and GCSE.** One 6993 paper every fortnight, timed to two hours, alternating with GCSE practice before the first GCSE maths paper on 14 May.
7. **15 to 18 June 2027: the final four days.** After the last GCSE maths paper on 14 June, two full 6993 papers and a review of command words, especially "detailed reasoning".

The plan is ours and does not promise a grade. Some schools run 6993 over two years rather than one, or enter students in Year 12; the order of topics still works, spread more thinly. For students heading to A level, the FSMQ topics are the opening chapters of A level pure maths, so nothing learned here is wasted. ## What families ask about OCR Additional Maths

### What is OCR FSMQ Additional Maths?

It is a Level 3 Free Standing Mathematics Qualification from Cambridge OCR, code 6993, aimed at students taking GCSE Maths at Higher tier. It introduces A level topics such as calculus and logarithms in a single two-hour exam.

### When is the OCR Additional Maths exam in 2027?

OCR's final June 2027 timetable lists 6993 Additional Mathematics on the afternoon of Friday 18 June 2027, lasting 2 hours. There is one series a year, in May/June.

### Is OCR Additional Maths a GCSE?

No. It is a Level 3 qualification, the same level as AS and A level, graded A to E rather than 9 to 1. Many students sit it in Year 11 alongside GCSE Maths.

### How many UCAS points is FSMQ Additional Maths worth?

OCR's specification says it attracts up to 10 UCAS points in the new tariff. Whether a particular university or course counts those points is for that university to decide.

### Can you use a calculator in OCR Additional Maths?

Yes. A scientific or graphical calculator is allowed and can be used for any function it can perform. On questions marked "show detailed reasoning", the written method must be complete; a calculator answer alone will not earn full marks.

### What topics are in OCR Additional Maths?

Seven sections: Algebra, Enumeration, Coordinate Geometry, Pythagoras and Trigonometry, Calculus, Numerical Methods, and Exponentials and Logarithms. Calculus and logarithms are entirely new beyond GCSE.

### Is there a formula sheet in OCR Additional Maths?

Yes. OCR gives formulae on page 2 of the integrated answer booklet, and the specification lists them in its section on formulae and identities.

### Can my child resit OCR Additional Maths?

Yes. OCR says learners can retake the qualification as many times as they wish, but there is only one series each year, in May/June.

### When are classes held?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Times are given in UK time.

### Are you connected with OCR?
