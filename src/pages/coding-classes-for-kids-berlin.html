Berlin schools

What do Berlin schools teach about computing, and when?

Less than many parents expect, and later. The facts below come from the Kultusministerkonferenz, the Gesellschaft für Informatik and the Berlin Senate.

Six years of Grundschule

In most of Germany, Grundschule ends after grade 4. Berlin and Brandenburg are the exception: the KMK describes a primary stage that runs from the first to the fourth grade, "in Berlin und Brandenburg bis zur sechsten Jahrgangsstufe", up to grade 6. A Berlin child is therefore in Grundschule from about 6 to about 12, the whole age range this page is about.

That long primary stage is a strength for reading and maths, but it is also six years in which no Berlin rule requires a single computing lesson.

No Informatik for everyone

The Gesellschaft für Informatik counts the Länder where every pupil must take Informatik. In its 2026/27 Informatik-Monitor, Berlin is named first among six Länder "ohne einen für alle verbindlichen Informatikunterricht", without Informatik that is compulsory for all, and it adds that no binding plans to introduce the subject are known in Berlin.

What Berlin does have is the Informationstechnischer Grundkurs, ITG, taught for one lesson a week in grade 7 or 8. After that, Informatik is a Wahlpflicht subject, an elective, usually in grades 9 and 10.

So the honest picture for a Berlin parent is this: if your child is curious about how games and apps are made, the school timetable will not answer that question until grade 7 at the earliest, and then for one lesson a week. Children who start at 7 or 8 at home arrive at the ITG course already able to break a problem into steps, which is exactly the skill that course is meant to begin.

Taught in English

Live classes in English, at your child's own pace

Every class is live, every class is in English, and the mentor adapts to the child, not the other way round.

English only, said plainly

Our mentors teach in English. We do not offer German-language lessons, and we would rather tell you now than after a trial. For a child at a bilingual or international school in Berlin this is simply the language of the classroom; for a child at a German school it is a second subject practised for free.

Pace and accent matched

Mentors speak slowly and clearly, repeat instructions in simpler words, and get used to how each child speaks. A seven year old who answers in short English sentences, or switches to a German word in the middle, is understood and gently given the English one.

Code is the same in any language

Scratch blocks and Python keywords do not change with the language of the lesson. A loop is a loop. Most of what a child learns is shown on the screen, built together and tried, so the English needed is smaller than parents expect.

The mentors are based in India and teach live over video, which is why the times further down suit Berlin afternoons. Your child sees the same mentor every week, so after a few classes that person knows whether your child likes to be challenged, needs a moment before answering, or learns most easily by changing someone else's program first.

A real Berlin project

The Oberbaumbrücke bike counter: the quietest day that was not

Berlin counts its bicycles. The Senate department for mobility publishes every hourly count from its permanent counters, free to reuse. Our older children use one bridge to learn the most useful habit in programming: check what is missing before you believe a record.

The data

The workbook is called Radzähldaten in Berlin and holds one sheet per year from 2012 to 2025. For 2025 it has 8,760 rows, one for every hour, and a column for each counter. We use the two counters on the Oberbaumbrücke between Friedrichshain and Kreuzberg: east-bound and west-bound, counting since 1 June 2015.

Its legend says something every child should read first: a 0 can mean no data or implausible data, and an empty cell means no data at all. In other words, the data warns you about itself.

The question

Which day of 2025 had the fewest bikes crossing the bridge? A ten year old guesses Christmas, or a day of snow. The program gives a different answer, and finding out why is the real lesson.

We give the child a small file, oberbaum_2025.csv, with three columns: the hour, the east count and the west count. The first program adds up each day and skips any hour where a count is missing, keeping a note of those hours.

Thursday 6 November 2025 with 267 bikes looks like the quietest day of the year. But the program also says that 19 hours of that day are missing. Looking at the file shows what happened: the east-bound counter recorded nothing from 05:00 on 6 November to 04:00 the next morning, 24 hours in a row, while the west-bound counter kept counting normally. The 267 bikes are only the five night hours before the gap. Nobody stayed home; a sensor stopped.

The third missing hour is stranger, and children love it. On 30 March 2025, at 02:00, both counters are empty. That was the night summer time began: German clocks jumped from 02:00 straight to 03:00, so that hour never existed. A missing value is not always a fault. Sometimes it is the truth.

  1. Ages 6 to 8, no screen. Make a tally chart of cars passing your window for ten minutes. Then cover two minutes with a hand. Is the covered part "no cars"?
  2. Ages 8 to 10, Scratch. Store the daily totals in a list and find the smallest. Then add one fake day with a 0 and watch it win.
  3. Ages 10 to 12, Python. Run Version 1, read the missing hours, then write Version 2 yourself before looking at ours.
  4. Going further. At 08:00 on a weekday the bridge carries more than five times as many bikes as at 08:00 on a Saturday, but at 03:00 the weekend wins by almost four times. Make a chart of every hour and explain both differences.

A free first contest

Can my child in Berlin enter the Informatik-Biber?

Yes, through the school, and this year it is only days away. The Informatik-Biber is a short online puzzle contest that needs no coding at all.

What it is

The organisers, Bundesweite Informatikwettbewerbe, call the Biber Germany's largest school informatics competition: a free online quiz of logic puzzles for grades 3 to 13, with no prior knowledge needed. More than 580,000 pupils took part in 2025, and 2026 is its 20th year.

It is short: primary pupils solve nine tasks in 30 minutes, grades 5 and 6 get twelve tasks in 35 minutes, and from grade 7 it is fifteen tasks in 40 minutes. Children can take part alone or in pairs.

When it runs

The Biberwochen 2026 run from 9 to 20 November 2026. Schools register their pupils and run it in a lesson, so ask your child's teacher whether the school takes part. A practice version, the Schnupper-Biber, has been open since 14 September 2026.

Results are published in December 2026, and certificates and prizes are sent in January 2027.

The Biber puzzles are about ordering, hidden rules, shortest paths and codes, the same thinking a coding class builds. We do not use or copy the real tasks; in class we write our own puzzles in the same spirit, and children who enjoy them usually enjoy the Biber. If a Berlin school does not take part, the practice puzzles are still a good free afternoon.

Coding classes for kids in Berlin: common questions

Are the classes taught in German?

No. Every class is live and taught in English only. Mentors speak slowly and clearly and adapt to your child's pace and accent, so children who are still learning English can follow. Scratch and Python look the same in any language.

What age can my child start coding?

From 6. Our Coding for Kids course takes children from 6 to 12 in groups by age and starts with Scratch blocks, so no typing or reading of long texts is needed at first. From about 9, children can move to typed Python.

Do Berlin schools teach coding?

Not as a compulsory subject in Grundschule. The Gesellschaft für Informatik names Berlin among six Länder without Informatik that is compulsory for all pupils. Berlin pupils take a one-hour ITG course in grade 7 or 8, and Informatik is an elective, usually in grades 9 and 10.

Is my child too young if they are only in grade 1 or 2?

Usually not. Six and seven year olds start with stories and simple games in Scratch, where blocks snap together and nothing has to be typed. The mentor keeps lessons short and playful, and you will see after the free first class whether your child is ready.

When are classes in Berlin time?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Most Berlin children take classes between about 15:00 and 18:00, or on weekend mornings.

What is the Informatik-Biber and can my child take part?

A free online puzzle contest for grades 3 to 13 that needs no prior knowledge. In 2026 it runs from 9 to 20 November and schools register their pupils, so ask your child's teacher. A practice version, the Schnupper-Biber, has been open since 14 September 2026.

Are the teachers in Berlin?

No. Our mentors are based in India and teach live online. That is why classes fit Berlin afternoons: 16:00 in Berlin is 20:30 in India in winter. Your child sees the same mentor every week.

How big are the groups?

Group batches have fifteen to twenty children of a similar age. If you would like your child to have a mentor to themselves, the one-to-one plan gives two private classes a week at times you choose.

What does it cost, and in which currency?

Fees are monthly and shown on this page in euro at the European Central Bank reference rate. The payment is taken in US dollars and converted by your bank, so the amount on your statement can differ slightly. The first class is free and needs no card.

What will my child actually build?

Younger children build games and animated stories in Scratch. Children from about 9 write Python programs, draw with code and, around 10 to 12, work with real data such as the Oberbaumbrücke bike counter on this page.
