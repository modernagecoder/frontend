The top end

What a selective teen game design program asks for in 2027

To see what serious looks like, we read the 2027 page of Carnegie Mellon’s National High School Game Academy (NHSGA) from start to finish. It is one residential program among many, not a ranking, and the details below are only what Carnegie Mellon publishes.

National High School Game Academy, 2027 cycle, from Carnegie Mellon Pre-College’s own page (read 17 October 2026)

The workload, in their numbers

The first two weeks are classes in 2D and 3D art, programming in Unity, project management, interactive storytelling, game design, animation, sound and public speaking. The page says "A typical day includes five hours of classes and three hours of homework."

It also says "We average approximately 20 total homework assignments over the first two weeks." Our arithmetic: two school weeks are ten weekdays, so that is about two assignments a day, every day, on top of class.

How the teams work

From week three, students build in teams of five or six: programmers, visual artists, generalists and sound designers. A one-week Lightning Round comes first, then a second team pitches three ideas and builds one original game from scratch.

One line surprises many applicants: "We do not assign that role in the NHSGA. Design is a collaborative effort." Nobody is hired as the game designer; design comes out of the whole team.

Source: Carnegie Mellon Pre-College, National High School Game Academy, read 17 October 2026. Dates are for the 2027 cycle; later cycles publish their own. We are not connected with Carnegie Mellon University, and we make no prediction about any admission decision.

Applying

The work sample rules, and what they teach every teen

Selective game programs judge what an applicant has already made. The NHSGA rules are unusually specific, and they are a good checklist even for a teen who never applies there.

Work samples for the 2027 NHSGA application, from Carnegie Mellon’s page
RuleWhat it says
No portfolio links"Do not submit a link to an online portfolio." Upload documents instead, ideally one PDF.
How many"No more than 10 documents or work examples should be submitted."
CodeWritten code is allowed if the applicant explains what skill each sample is meant to show.
Software track"If applying for a software position, please submit a video of the game or project being played or used live."

Prior experienceHelpful but not necessary; the page looks for a creative mindset shown through the samples.

Why the video matters so much

A reviewer can watch thirty seconds of play faster than they can install a build. A short capture that shows the core loop, a win and a loss tells them more than a page of description.

That is why every plan on this page ends with a recorded play-through, whichever engine the teen uses.

Saying who did what

Most teen games are built with friends. A sample that says "I wrote the enemy movement and the save system; my partner drew the art" is honest and much easier to judge.

Keep a short build diary from the first day of camp. Writing the role statement in March is then a matter of copying, not remembering.

Which engine

Godot, Unity or Roblox Studio: what each one allows at which age

Engine choice is partly about taste and partly about rules. Account and licence terms set real age lines, and the hardware at home decides the rest.

Reading the age lines carefully

The Unity line above is the eligibility rule for the free Student plan for secondary school students. Check Unity’s current terms with your teen before creating any account, and keep a parent involved for anyone under 18.

Godot has no account at all, which is one reason we like it for a summer: download, open, build.

Ratings for the game itself

US families often judge teen games by the ESRB labels. The ESRB guide says Teen content "is generally suitable for ages 13 and up" and Everyone 10+ content "is generally suitable for ages 10 and up".

Our suggestion is to keep a student game within what Everyone 10+ describes, so the project can be shown at school, to grandparents and on an application without a second thought.

Sources: Godot licence, Godot FAQ, Unity Student plan, Roblox Studio setup, ESRB ratings guide, all read 17 October 2026. We are not connected with any of these companies or organisations.

Real code

A Godot movement script, and the bug every camp should teach

This is the player movement from Godot’s own first-game tutorial, set out as one script. It runs as written on an Area2D node once four input actions are mapped in the project settings.

Why delta matters

Speed is in pixels per second, but the screen updates in frames. Multiplying by delta converts one into the other. At an assumed 60 frames a second, delta is about 0.0167, so the player moves about 6.7 pixels each frame (400 / 60).

On a slower laptop running 30 frames a second, delta doubles and the step doubles too, so the player still covers 400 pixels in a second. Without delta, a game runs at different speeds on different computers.

What the student learns

Vectors, frame time and clamping are the same ideas behind physics in any engine. A teen who can explain Script 2’s bug out loud understands something that later appears in Unity, in Roblox and in a high school physics class.

We set this bug deliberately in week two of our plan. Students find it by feel first, because diagonal movement looks wrong, and then prove it with the square root of 2.

Six weeks

Our six-week summer plan for one finished game

This is our plan for a teen learning from home with live classes. It assumes about six hours a week: two live lessons and the build time between them. It is deliberately smaller than a residential program, because a finished small game beats an unfinished large one.

Six weeks to a finished game (our plan)
WeekGoalFinished when
1Engine set up and a moving playerThe player moves, stays on screen and the teen can explain delta
2The core loopOne thing to collect or avoid, a score, and the diagonal bug found and fixed
3Win and loseA start screen, a game-over screen and a restart button
4PlaytestFour people outside the family played it; their three biggest complaints written down
5Fix and polishThe three complaints fixed; sound and one visual pass
6Ship and recordAn exported build, a 60 to 90 second play video, and a one-page build diary

Ages 13 and 14

Roblox Studio or browser JavaScript. One level, one mechanic, done properly.

Ages 15 and 16

Godot with GDScript. A short arcade game with a title screen and saved high score.

Ages 16 to 18

Unity or Godot, a level designed on paper first, and a gameplay video fit for an application.

Our time budget, for parents who want the number: six hours a week for six weeks is 36 hours. A residential day at the program above is five hours of class and three of homework, so 36 hours is roughly four and a half of those days. That is why our plan aims at one small game rather than two team games.

The portfolio

A portfolio checklist for a teen game designer

Whether the next step is a selective camp, a school showcase or a college application, the same few items do the work. This checklist is ours, built from the work-sample rules above.

  1. One finished game. Playable from start to end, with a way to win and a way to lose.
  2. A short play video. Sixty to ninety seconds of real play, with no music laid over the game’s own sound.
  3. A role statement. One sentence per project saying exactly what the teen made.
  4. One explained code sample. A function the teen wrote, with three lines on why it is built that way.
  5. A design page. The first sketch of the level next to a screenshot of the final one.
  6. A playtest note. What testers struggled with and what changed because of it.
  7. Everything in one PDF. Matching the no-links rule, with the video uploaded where the application allows it.

What not to include

Tutorial projects copied step by step. Reviewers recognise the standard first-game assets at a glance, and they show following, not designing.

Ten half-finished prototypes. The rule says no more than ten examples; three strong ones are better.

Using AI tools honestly

If an AI tool helped with art or code, say so in the role statement, exactly as you would credit a teammate.

Under-18 students should only use tools whose terms allow their age, with a parent aware of the account. In class we use AI mainly to explain an error message, not to write the game.

Questions from families

Game design summer camp for teens: common questions

What should a teen get out of a game design summer camp?

One finished game that someone else can play, a short video of it being played, and the ability to explain their own code. A certificate on its own shows very little.

How old do you have to be for the National High School Game Academy?

Carnegie Mellon's 2027 page says applicants must be 16 by June 26, 2027 and be a sophomore or junior when they apply, with a B average or better. We are not connected with the program.

When are the 2027 NHSGA deadlines?

The 2027 page lists February 1, 2027 for the scholarship application, with decisions on April 1, 2027, and March 1, 2027 for the basic application, decided on a rolling basis.

Which game engine should a teenager learn first?

For a 13 or 14 year old who plays Roblox, Roblox Studio. For a teen who wants clean scripting and full ownership, Godot. For older teens aiming at C# and 3D, Unity. That is our view, not a rule.

Is Godot really free?

Yes. The Godot licence page says Godot Engine is free and open source, released under the permissive MIT license. No account is needed to download it.

Can my teen use Unity for free?

Unity offers a free Student plan. Its page says secondary school students qualify at 16 years and older if enrolled in an accredited educational institution.

Can my teen make games on a Chromebook?

Roblox's own education FAQ says Roblox Studio cannot run on Chromebooks. Browser-based JavaScript games work well on a Chromebook, which is why we teach that route to Chromebook families.

Is an online game design camp as good as a residential one?

They are different. Residential camps give team life on campus; live online classes give a regular teacher, no travel and the chance to keep building after summer. Choose by what your teen needs.

When are your classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

How can Modern Age Coders help over the summer?

A regular teacher takes your teen through our six-week plan in the engine that suits them, reviews each build, runs a playtest week and helps record the final play video.
