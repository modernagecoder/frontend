## From 4% to 6%: two points, fifty percent, one honest report

The example is synthetic. It is not an examination score, patient statistic or claim about any Al Ghubrah institution.

### Keep the counts beside the rates

Practice period A records 20 successes in 500 attempts. Period B records 30 successes in 500 attempts. The rates are 4% and 6%. Because the denominators match, the absolute increase is also visible as ten additional successes.

```
old_rate = 20 / 500  # 0.04
new_rate = 30 / 500  # 0.06

point_change = (new_rate - old_rate) * 100  # 2
relative_change = ((new_rate - old_rate) / old_rate) * 100  # 50
```

The percentage-point change subtracts two rates and expresses the result on the percentage scale: 6% minus 4% equals 2 percentage points. The relative percentage change divides that difference by the old 4% baseline: 2 divided by 4 equals 50%.

### Why both answers need labels

"Up two percent" is ambiguous and should be rejected. A reader cannot tell whether it means a two-percent relative increase or a move of two percentage points. "Up 50%" is mathematically correct as relative change, but it sounds dramatic without the underlying 20-of-500 and 30-of-500 counts.

The learner must publish the baseline, new value, denominators, absolute counts and time window. If the denominators change, those differences matter too. If a sample or definition changes, even correctly calculated changes may not be comparable.

Preferred sentence

The synthetic success rate rose from 4% (20 of 500) to 6% (30 of 500), an increase of 2 percentage points or 50% relative to the earlier rate.

| Expression | Value | Acceptable? | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Absolute count change | 10 more successes | Yes | Preserves the event difference |
| Percentage-point change | 2 points | Yes | Direct difference between 4% and 6% |
| Relative percentage change | 50% | Yes, with baseline | Difference divided by original 4% |
| "Up 2 percent" | Ambiguous | No | Does not identify point or relative change |

A strong extension asks the learner to write a formatter that prints counts and both changes, then fails a test when the earlier rate is zero. Division by zero is not a marketing opportunity. It is a condition the code must handle explicitly.
