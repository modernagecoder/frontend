# Coding bootcamp for teens

The word bootcamp promises speed. For an adult changing careers, that usually means months of full-time study. For a teenager with school, sports and family, a bootcamp has to mean something different: a short, intense block of building with a clear finish line. This page explains what an honest teen bootcamp contains, the free curricula a motivated teen can already use, the age rules on the platforms involved, the Git commands every bootcamp should teach, and the eight-week plan our teachers run live online.

At a glance: Ages: 13 to 18; Length: Eight weeks; Hours: About ten a week; Stack: HTML, CSS, JavaScript, Git; Finish line: A deployed project and a demo; Group: A batch, or one to one; Classes: Weekdays or weekends, flexible; Start: A free first lesson. Rated 4.9 across 547 Google reviews.

## What a coding bootcamp is, and what it should mean for a teenager

A bootcamp is a format, not a qualification. There is no official definition, so it pays to know what you are buying. This is how we describe the difference.

**Adult bootcamp and teen bootcamp compared (our description, not an industry standard)**

| Feature | Typical adult bootcamp | What a teen bootcamp should be |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Usually a career change | Real skills and one finished project |
| Time | Often full time for months | A fixed number of hours around school |
| Starting point | Assumes an adult who can study alone all day | Assumes a teen who needs a teacher and a rhythm |
| Proof at the end | A portfolio for job applications | A deployed project the teen can explain line by line |
| Promises to watch | Job and salary claims | Any promise beyond skills is a warning sign |

### Why the honest version is smaller

A teen has perhaps ten spare hours a week in term time. A program that claims to make them job-ready in eight weeks is selling the adult promise to the wrong audience.

What eight weeks can do is real: take a teen from small scripts to a working, deployed web project, with the habits professionals use every day.

### What to check in any program

Ask what the teen will have on the last day, who reviews their code, and whether version control is taught or skipped. Ask for the age rules of every platform the program uses.

If an adult bootcamp is considering your teen, read its own admissions page for age limits. We could not read several of those pages for this guide, so we make no claims about them.

## Two free, structured curricula a teen can already follow

A motivated teen does not need to pay for a syllabus. Two well-known free curricula publish theirs in the open. Here is what each says about itself.

**MDN Learn web development and The Odin Project, from their own pages (read 17 October 2026)**

| Curriculum | What it says about itself | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| MDN Learn web development | Core modules "provide a structured set of tutorials teaching the essential skills and practices for being a successful front-end developer" | Core modules, then Extension modules that "cover useful additional skills" |
| MDN, stated level | Designed to take you from "beginner" to "comfortable" (not "beginner" to "expert") | A foundation for more advanced reading |
| The Odin Project | "The Odin Project provides a free open source coding curriculum that can be taken entirely online." | Foundations, then Full Stack JavaScript or Full Stack Ruby on Rails |
| The Odin Project, order | "The courses should be taken in the order that they are displayed." | A fixed sequence, with projects placed throughout |

### Reading MDN&rsquo;s own promise

The list above is MDN&rsquo;s core module sequence. We like that MDN aims for "comfortable", not "expert". It is an honest goal, and it matches what an intensive can achieve for a teenager.

Notice where version control sits: last in the list. In our plan it moves to the middle, because a teen who commits from week five never loses a day of work again.

### Why teens still take a live course

Free curricula are excellent and self-paced, and self-paced is exactly where most teens stall. The material is not the hard part; the rhythm, the feedback and the deadline are.

Our bootcamp follows the same ground with a teacher who reviews every commit. Students are free, and encouraged, to use MDN and Odin alongside it.

## The age rules every teen bootcamp runs into

A bootcamp uses accounts on real platforms, and those platforms set their own minimum ages. These are the lines published by the services a web bootcamp is most likely to use.

**Minimum ages on platforms and communities a teen bootcamp uses, from their own pages (read 17 October 2026)**

| Service | What its page says |
|---|---|
| GitHub (Terms of Service) | "A User must be at least 13 years of age" |
| GitHub Education (student benefits) | Applicants must be "at least 13 years old" and enrolled in a program "such as a high school, college, university, or homeschool" |
| Hack Club | Open to all teens aged 13 to 18; it describes itself as "the world&rsquo;s largest nonprofit movement of teenagers making cool projects" |

### What 13 means in practice

A teen of 13 or older can hold a GitHub account under its terms, which is why our bootcamp starts at 13. Younger students learn the same ideas with a teacher&rsquo;s demonstration account instead of their own.

GitHub&rsquo;s student application explicitly includes homeschool enrolment, so homeschooled teens are not left out of the student benefits.

### AI tools and age

Many AI coding assistants set their own minimum ages, and some are for adults only. A bootcamp for under-18s should list the tools it uses and check each one&rsquo;s terms, with a parent aware of every account.

In our classes, the code a teen commits is code they wrote and can explain. That rule matters more than any tool choice.

Sources: [GitHub Terms of Service](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/github-terms/github-terms-of-service), [GitHub Education student application](https://docs.github.com/en/education/about-github-education/github-education-for-students/apply-to-github-education-as-a-student), [Hack Club](https://hackclub.com/). We are not connected with GitHub or Hack Club.

## The Git commands a teen bootcamp should teach, and why

If a bootcamp teaches only one professional habit, it should be version control. The Pro Git book explains the idea in one heading: "Snapshots, Not Differences".

### Snapshots, explained

The Pro Git book says Git "thinks of its data more like a series of snapshots of a miniature filesystem". Each commit is a photograph of the whole project at that moment, not a list of edits.

The same chapter describes three main states for a file: modified, staged and committed. The add command moves a change to staged; commit takes the photograph.

### Why we teach branches early

The switch command with -c creates a branch, a safe copy of the timeline to experiment on. If the dark colour scheme breaks the layout, the main branch is untouched.

Teens who learn this in week five stop being afraid to try things. That confidence is worth more to an intensive than any single framework.

Source: [Pro Git, What is Git?](https://git-scm.com/book/en/v2/Getting-Started-What-is-Git%3F), read 17 October 2026.

## Our eight-week teen bootcamp plan

This is the plan our teachers run live online. It assumes about ten hours a week: three live lessons of 90 minutes (4.5 hours) and about 5.5 hours of building between them.

**Eight weeks to a deployed project (our plan)**

| Week | Focus | Finished when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | HTML structure | A personal page with headings, lists, links and images |
| 2 | CSS and layout | The page looks right on a phone and a laptop |
| 3 | JavaScript basics | A button that changes the page, written without copying |
| 4 | JavaScript with data | A small quiz or tracker that stores its state |
| 5 | Git and GitHub | The project on GitHub with clear commit messages and one merged branch |
| 6 | Capstone build | A one-page plan and the core feature working |
| 7 | Testing and accessibility | Three people outside the class used it; their problems fixed |
| 8 | Deploy and demo | A live link and a five-minute demo explaining the code |

### Ages 13 and 14

The plan as written, at a steady pace, with a small capstone.

### Ages 15 and 16

A larger capstone, and a first look at a framework in week six.

### Ages 17 and 18

A front end plus a simple server, ready to show on an application.

Our arithmetic: ten hours a week for eight weeks is 80 hours. For comparison, a one-semester high school elective that meets 50 minutes a day, five days a week, for 18 weeks (our assumption of a common timetable) is 75 hours. The bootcamp packs a semester&rsquo;s time into two months, which is why it works best in summer or in a lighter school term.

## Four levels on the way through a bootcamp

The levels our teachers use to place a teen on day one.

**Teen bootcamp levels (ours)**

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1. Starter | Has used blocks or followed tutorials, no projects of their own |
| 2. Scripter | Writes short programs, still copies most structure |
| 3. Builder | Builds a small site or app alone, uses Git with help |
| 4. Shipper | Plans, builds, commits, deploys and explains a project |

### For parents

Ask to see the commit history, not only the finished page. It shows whether the work happened steadily.
Level 1 to level 3 in eight weeks is a strong result. Level 4 usually needs a second project.

### For teens

Commit small and often. A message per change is a diary of what you learned.
If you cannot explain a line, it is not yours yet. Rewrite it until you can.

## Coding bootcamp for teens: common questions

### What is a coding bootcamp for teens?

An intensive block of project-based coding, usually a few weeks to a few months, that ends with something built, deployed and explained. For teens it should fit around school and promise skills, not jobs.

### Will a bootcamp get my teen a job?

We make no job or salary promises, and families should be wary of any teen program that does. What a good intensive gives a teen is real skills, a finished project and professional habits such as version control.

### How old does my teen need to be?

Our bootcamp starts at 13. GitHub's Terms of Service say a user must be at least 13 years of age, and the teen will need their own GitHub account from week five.

### Are there free coding curricula for teens?

Yes. MDN Learn web development offers structured core modules aimed at taking learners from beginner to comfortable, and The Odin Project describes itself as a free open source curriculum taken entirely online.

### Can homeschooled teens get GitHub student benefits?

GitHub's student application lists homeschool alongside high school, college and university as eligible programs, for students at least 13 years old. Check the current page when applying.

### How many hours a week does your bootcamp take?

About ten: three live lessons of 90 minutes and roughly five and a half hours of building. Over eight weeks that comes to 80 hours.

### What will my teen have at the end?

A deployed project with a live link, a GitHub history showing steady work, and a five-minute demo in which they explain their own code.

### When are classes held?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

### Is a bootcamp better than weekly classes?

Neither is better in general. A bootcamp suits a free summer and a clear project; weekly classes suit a busy school year. Some teens do a summer intensive and then keep going weekly.

### Can my teen use AI tools during the bootcamp?

Only tools whose terms allow the teen's age, with a parent aware of the account. Our rule is that every committed line must be one the teen can explain.
