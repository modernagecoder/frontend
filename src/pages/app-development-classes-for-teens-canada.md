# App development classes for teens in Canada

Plenty of Canadian teenagers have an app idea; far fewer know that publishing one runs into age rules before it runs into code. Apple and Google both tie developer accounts to adulthood, so a fifteen year old in Surrey or Sudbury can build a complete app but usually needs a parent to put it in a store. This page sets out those rules in each company's own words, shows where app building sits in Canadian school courses, explains the tools we teach, and walks through the real logic of a small app: finding the nearest City of Hamilton spray pad.

## What Apple and Google require before a teen can publish an app

Building an app has no age limit. Publishing one does. These are the rules from each company's own help pages, read on 22 October 2026. Neither page gives a number for Canada; Apple's depends on the age of majority in the developer's province.

**Developer account rules for app stores, from Apple and Google (read 22 October 2026)**

| Store or programme | What the company says | What it means for a Canadian teen |
|---|---|---|
| Apple Developer Program | You need "an Apple Account with two-factor authentication turned on and be the legal age of majority in your region." | A teen under the age of majority cannot enrol alone |
| Apple, for under-age developers | "If you're under the age of majority in your region, your parent/guardian can enroll with their Apple Account and share their account with you." | A parent enrols; the teen builds and submits under that account |
| Google Play Console | "You must be at least 18 years of age to sign up for a Play Console account." | Under 18, a parent or guardian holds the account |
| Google Play, new personal accounts | "Developers with personal accounts created after November 13, 2023, must meet specific testing requirements before they can make their app available on Google Play." | Plan for a testing period before release, and an Android device to verify the account |
| Google Play fee | A one-time registration fee, paid by card | A cost to discuss before the launch phase |
| Swift Student Challenge (Apple) | "13 years of age or older in all other countries and regions", with free registration as an Apple developer | Open to Canadian students from 13, without a paid account |

### How we handle publishing with teens

Most of a teen's app course is spent building and testing on their own phone and in a simulator, which needs no store account at all. Publishing comes at the end, and only if the family wants it. Where a store requires an adult, the parent opens the account, and the teen prepares everything else: the build, the screenshots, the description and the privacy answers.

We never ask a teen to misstate their age to a store, and we explain to families what each account involves before the launch phase begins.

### Why the rules are worth respecting

A published app has users, reviews, data and sometimes payments, and the account holder is responsible for all of them. That is why the stores tie accounts to adulthood. A teen who learns the release process with a parent beside them learns it properly.

For a teen who wants a public showcase without a store, Apple's Swift Student Challenge accepts Canadian entrants from 13, and an app can also be shared as a web build.

## A spray pad finder on Hamilton's open data

The City of Hamilton publishes the locations of its spray pads under a licence that allows commercial use. The heart of a "nearest spray pad" app is one function: how far is each pad from the user? It is also where most first attempts go wrong. The code and every number below are ours, run on the city's file downloaded on 22 October 2026.

The app's job: from the phone's location, list the four nearest spray pads with their distance. The file holds 68 spray pads with NAME, ADDRESS, COMMUNITY and coordinates, X for longitude and Y for latitude. For testing we use a fixed example point of ours in downtown Hamilton, latitude 43.2557 and longitude -79.8711; in the app the point comes from the phone.

**Our app logic · nearest.js** (javascript)
```javascript
// Our spray pad finder logic (Modern Age Coders, written for this page). Runs in Node or inside a React Native app.
const fs = require("fs");

function distanceKm(lat1, lon1, lat2, lon2) {
  const R = 6371;                                   // mean Earth radius in km
  const rad = (d) => (d * Math.PI) / 180;
  const dLat = rad(lat2 - lat1);
  const dLon = rad(lon2 - lon1);
  const h = Math.sin(dLat / 2) ** 2 +
            Math.cos(rad(lat1)) * Math.cos(rad(lat2)) * Math.sin(dLon / 2) ** 2;
  return 2 * R * Math.asin(Math.sqrt(h));           // the haversine formula
}

function loadPads(path) {
  const [header, ...lines] = fs.readFileSync(path, "utf8").replace(/^\uFEFF/, "").trim().split(/\r?\n/);
  const cols = header.split(",");
  return lines.map((line) => {
    const f = line.split(",");                      // safe here: this file has no quoted fields
    return { name: f[cols.indexOf("NAME")], lat: Number(f[cols.indexOf("Y")]), lon: Number(f[cols.indexOf("X")]) };
  });
}

function nearest(pads, lat, lon, n) {
  return pads
    .map((p) => ({ ...p, km: distanceKm(lat, lon, p.lat, p.lon) }))
    .sort((a, b) => a.km - b.km)
    .slice(0, n);
}

const pads = loadPads("hamilton-spray-pads.csv");
const here = { lat: 43.2557, lon: -79.8711 };       // our example point, downtown Hamilton
console.log(pads.length, "spray pads loaded");
for (const p of nearest(pads, here.lat, here.lon, 4)) {
  console.log(p.name.padEnd(14), p.km.toFixed(2), "km");
}
```

**Output on the city's file (Node 22)** (text)
```text
68 spray pads loaded
Durand Park    0.70 km
Beasley Park   0.74 km
McLaren Park   0.80 km
Central Park   0.94 km
```
The same functions drop into a React Native screen unchanged; only loadPads changes, because an app bundles or downloads the data instead of reading a file.

**The shortcut most first attempts take, and what it does to the answer (our runs on the same file)**

| Method | Second nearest from our point | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Haversine distance (our code) | Beasley Park, 0.74 km | Treats the Earth as a sphere, so a degree east and a degree north are measured fairly |
| Latitude and longitude as a flat grid | Central Park, really 0.94 km away | At Hamilton's latitude a degree of longitude is much shorter than a degree of latitude, so east-west gaps are overstated |
| Flat grid, tested across the whole city | Wrong nearest pad at 297 of 1,800 test points | About one location in six would send a family to the wrong pad |

### What a teen learns from one function

That a plausible-looking result can be wrong, and that the only way to know is to test against something trusted. We check the haversine function by hand on two points whose distance we can measure on a map, before trusting it on 68.

That data has a shape: X is longitude and Y is latitude here, and swapping them is the single most common bug in a map app. The code reads columns by name for that reason.

### Turning logic into an app

In class the next steps are a list screen that shows the four results, a button that asks the phone for its location with the user's permission, and a message when permission is refused. Each step is tested on a real phone.

The teen also writes down, in the app's about screen, where the data comes from and under which licence, exactly as the city asks.

## From idea to a first app: seven steps we follow with teens

Our process for a teen's first complete app, whatever the idea. It is deliberately slow at the start and fast at the end.

1. Write the idea in one sentence. "An app that shows the nearest spray pad" is buildable; "an app for summer" is not yet.
2. Draw every screen on paper. Usually three or four. Each arrow between screens becomes a navigation step later.
3. Find the data. Where does the app's information come from, and does its licence allow use in an app? The Hamilton file does; many files online do not.
4. Write the logic first, without screens. Like nearest.js above: plain functions, tested with known answers.
5. Build one screen at a time. Run it on the phone after every change. A screen that only works in the simulator is not finished.
6. Ask someone else to use it. A parent or friend, with no instructions. Note every place they hesitate.
7. Decide about publishing. If the family wants a store release, a parent opens the account where the store requires an adult, and the teen prepares the release.

Step 4 is the one teens most want to skip, and the one that saves the most time. Bugs in logic are much easier to find when no screen is in the way.

## App development for teens in Canada: common questions

### Can a teenager publish an app on the App Store?

Apple requires the legal age of majority in your region to enrol in the Apple Developer Program. It also says that if you are under the age of majority, a parent or guardian can enrol with their Apple Account and share it with you.

### Can a teenager publish on Google Play?

Google says you must be at least 18 years of age to sign up for a Play Console account, so a parent or guardian holds the account. New personal accounts must also meet testing and device verification requirements before release.

### What age can my teen start app development?

Our App Development for Teens course takes absolute beginners aged 13 to 18. For children aged 8 to 12, App Making for Kids uses blocks first.

### Which tools will my teen use?

JavaScript and React Native, with Expo to run the app on a phone quickly and Firebase for data. One codebase builds for both Android and iPhone.

### Does my teen need a Mac to build iPhone apps?

Not to learn. The React Native site says developers can "Get started quickly with Expo Go", which runs the app on the teen's own phone during development. Publishing to Apple's store has its own requirements, which we explain when a family reaches that stage.

### Is app development taught in Canadian high schools?

Not usually as its own course, but parts of it are. Ontario's ICD2O has students build hands-on projects and ICS4C asks them to design graphical user interfaces; British Columbia lists Web Development 10 and Computer Programming 12.

### Are there app competitions for Canadian teens?

Apple's Swift Student Challenge accepts students aged 13 or older in Canada, with free registration as an Apple developer. Technovation runs a global challenge in which teams build a first solution to a real problem.

### Can my teen use AI tools to build an app?

Within each company's rules. OpenAI requires a parent's or guardian's permission for users under 18, and Anthropic does not allow anyone under 18 to use Claude. In our classes, any AI-suggested code must be explained by the teen.

### When are app classes held for Canadian teens?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Times are set in your own province's zone.

### What will my teen have at the end?

A complete app of their own design, tested on real phones, with its logic written and explained by the teen, such as the Hamilton spray pad finder on this page. Publishing is optional and done through a parent where the store requires it.
