Signature project

Can neighbours predict an area's median age?

The council contains 19 statistical areas (SA2s). For each one we took the median age published in QuickStats and an approximate centre point, then asked a three-neighbour model to guess each median age from the three closest other areas.

Random folds

Hold out areas at random and a test area usually still has its next-door neighbours in the training data, so the model looks strong.

Block folds

Hold out a whole stretch of the peninsula, such as Avalon to Terrey Hills, and the model must guess from areas further away. The error rises to 2.72 years.

Still useful

Even with blocks, the neighbour model beats simply guessing the average of the other areas, 2.72 years against 3.93.

The result that did not hold

Cut the peninsula into five blocks instead of four and the gap disappears: 2.19 years both ways. Use one neighbour instead of three and blocks score better than random folds. With only 19 areas, the choice of split moves the score as much as the model does.

The model, the blocks and the error figures are our own work; the median ages are published ABS values printed exactly. The lesson is about how to measure, not a claim about why ages differ along the coast.

The same caution applies far beyond maps. Readings taken minutes apart, photos of the same person, or sales from the same shop all leak information between training and test data if they are split at random.

Coding and AI

If AI tools can train a model in seconds, what is left for a Northern Beaches student to learn?

Judging the result. Training is now the easy part; knowing whether a score means anything is the skill.

Ask an AI assistant for a model and a cross-validation score and it will usually split the data at random, because that is the default. On data with a map, a calendar or repeated people in it, that default can report a model as better than it is, and the code will run without a single warning.

A student who has watched the peninsula error move from 2.23 to 2.72 years just by changing the split knows to ask how the test was built. That question is what employers and universities want from people who work with AI.

The student who questions the split is the one who can be trusted with the model. The longer argument is in why teenagers should still learn to code in 2026.

FAQ

Northern Beaches AI and programming questions

Questions Northern Beaches parents and learners often ask.

How many people live in the Northern Beaches council area?

The 2021 Census QuickStats for the Northern Beaches local government area record 263,554 residents, a median age of 41 and 105,017 private dwellings.

What is cross-validation?

It is a way to test a model by hiding part of the data, training on the rest and checking the guesses, then repeating with a different part hidden. The final score is the average across all the rounds.

Why split the peninsula into blocks?

Because next-door areas are alike. If a test area's neighbours stay in the training data, the model gets help it would not have on genuinely new places. Blocks remove that help.

What did the Northern Beaches project find?

With four folds and three neighbours, random splits gave an average error of 2.23 years and north-to-south blocks gave 2.72. With five blocks the difference vanished, which is itself the lesson: 19 areas are too few for one score to settle anything.

Is AI suitable for primary school children?

Yes, through games about patterns and prediction in Scratch. Python machine learning usually starts around 12 to 13.
