Homeschool coding and math in British Columbia

In British Columbia, home education rests on a few short sections of the School Act and one section of the School Regulation. They set a date, 30 September each year, and a distinction that matters for every family: a child registered for home education is not the same as a child enrolled in a school program, including an online learning school. This page quotes the law on both, explains where coding and math fit, then sets out one week of study and a trail project we teach. Our coding and math classes sit inside the family's own program, and we do not grant provincial credits. Nothing on this page is legal advice: read the Act and Regulation yourself and ask the school you register with.

The law itself

What BC's School Act and School Regulation say about home education

The ministry's homeschooling page on gov.bc.ca could not be read on 22 October 2026 (the connection was refused), so we went to the law itself on bclaws.gov.bc.ca, the King's Printer site. Both the Act and the Regulation were shown as current to October 13, 2026.

Home education in the BC School Act and School Regulation, quoted from bclaws.gov.bc.ca (read 22 October 2026)
WhereWhat the law says
School Act, Division 4The division is headed "Home Education".
School Act s.12A parent of a child of school age "(a) may educate the child at home or elsewhere in accordance with this Division, and (b) must provide that child with an educational program."
School Act s.13(1)The parent "must register the child on or before September 30 in each year with (a) a school of the parent's choice that is operating in British Columbia", or with a francophone school where eligible, or "(c) an independent school operating in British Columbia."
School Act s.13(3)"A school or francophone school that registers a child under this section must provide the child with access to educational services in accordance with the regulations."
School Regulation s.3(1)The registering school "must offer (a) evaluation and assessment services sufficient to enable the parents of the child to determine the educational progress achieved by the child in relation to students of similar age and ability, and (b) the loan of educational resource materials that are authorized and recommended by the minister".
School Regulation s.3(2)"With the permission of a board", a registered child "may audit educational programs offered by the board", on terms the board sets.

What this means for a coding and math plan

The Act asks the parent to provide an educational program, and the Regulation gives the family a school whose evaluation services can show progress "in relation to students of similar age and ability". That is a useful outside check for math in particular.

The Act does not list subjects for home education. Families who want a structure can use the BC curriculum, which includes coding through Applied Design, Skills, and Technologies and through Computer Science 11 and 12 under Mathematics.

This summarises two pieces of BC legislation for families; it is not legal advice. The ministry's own guidance, which we could not reach, may add detail; please read it, and ask the school you register with how it applies the Regulation.

Modern Age Coders is not connected with the BC Ministry of Education and Child Care, any school district or any independent school. We teach classes; we do not register, assess or report on home education.

Registered or enrolled

Home education or online learning school: the line BC's law draws

BC families searching for "BC homeschool programs" often find online learning schools, which are a different thing in law. The Act defines online learning as "a method of instruction that relies primarily on communication between students and teachers by means of the internet", and an online learning school as "a school or francophone school that offers instruction to its students through online learning only".

Where we sit: our classes are private lessons a family chooses as part of its own program. They are not a BC school, they are not an online learning school under the Act, and taking them does not enrol a child anywhere. A family that registers under section 13 and adds our coding and math classes is still a home-education family.

A sample week

A weekly coding and math plan for a registered BC teen

Our own plan for a home-educated teen in about Grade 10, built from two live classes with us and three short solo sessions. It assumes the family also uses the registering school's evaluation services once or twice a term.

  1. Live session 1, math (60 minutes): one Foundations of Mathematics and Pre-calculus 10 idea, such as slope and rate of change, taught from examples and checked with a second method.
  2. Solo session A (30 minutes, next day): a practice set on that idea, marked by the teen against worked answers, with corrections written beside each error.
  3. Live session 2, coding (60 minutes): a Python program that uses the same idea, such as the Trail Profile below, written by the teen with the teacher reviewing each line.
  4. Solo session B (30 minutes): extend the program: a new input, a second statistic, or a test case that should break it.
  5. Solo session C (20 minutes, end of week): a short log entry: what was learned, one thing still unclear, and the file names of the week's work.
  6. Once or twice a term: ask the registering school about its evaluation and assessment services, which the Regulation says must be offered, to see progress against students of a similar age.

A project from our classes

Project: Trail Profile, climbing and slope in Python

Our teachers wrote this project for teens learning at home in Grades 10 to 12. It takes elevation readings along a hiking trail and works out the total climb, the net change and the steepest stretch. The math is slope as rate of change; the code uses the constructs BC's Computer Programming 11 names: "input/output, logic, decision structure, and loops".

The data. Ten elevation readings taken every 200 m along a made-up trail, from 120 m to 520 m above sea level. One short stretch goes downhill.

  1. Spot the difference. Total climb is 410 m but the net change is 400 m. The 10 m dip between 1200 m and 1400 m has to be climbed again, so climb and net change are different quantities.
  2. Check the steepest stretch by hand. From 1000 m to 1200 m the trail rises from 340 m to 420 m: a rise of 80 over a run of 200, which is 40 percent.
  3. Graph it. Plot distance against elevation on paper. The steepest stretch is the steepest line segment, and the average grade, 22.2 percent, is the slope of the line joining the first and last points.
  4. Change the data. Add a reading so the trail ends lower than it starts, and predict what each line of output will say before running it.
  5. Go further. Grade 11 and 12 students can fit a line of best fit, or compute the angle of the steepest stretch with an inverse tangent.

Why this project fits BC

Computer Programming 11 describes computational thinking as "decomposition, patterns and generalizations, abstraction, and algorithmic thinking". The project decomposes a trail into stretches, generalises one calculation to all of them, and keeps only the numbers that matter.

It also suits a province where many families walk steep trails; any real trail with elevation markers can supply new data.

The idea underneath

Total climb and net change sound the same and are not, just as registered and enrolled sound similar and are not. Knowing which definition you are using is the lesson family behind this BC page.

Students who can say which quantity they computed, and why, have understood rate of change rather than memorised a formula.

The online learning route, in the Regulation's words

School Regulation s.3(3): "A child in grade 10, 11 or 12 registered in a school, francophone school or independent school under section 13 of the Act may enroll in all or part of an educational program that is (a) offered by a board or an independent school, and (b) delivered through online learning."

Section 3(4) adds that enrolling in board programs not delivered through online learning means the child "ceases to be registered under section 13 (1) (a) of the Act and is enrolled as a student with the board." Ask the school before signing up for any course.

Pacific time, all year

Class times on BC's permanent clock

BC stopped changing its clocks in 2026, which simplifies a home-education timetable.

The BC government announced permanent, year-round daylight saving time after clocks moved forward on Sunday, March 8, 2026, which puts most of the province at UTC-7 all year. East Kootenay and Golden keep UTC-6, the same as Alberta. Eastern Canada still changes, so a class at 11:00 a.m. in Vancouver is 2:00 p.m. in Toronto during winter 2026 to 2027 and 3:00 p.m. there once Eastern clocks spring forward on 14 March 2027 (our own conversion, for families who share classes with relatives in the east). For home-educated students, weekday mornings in BC are open, so class times rarely clash with anything.

Questions from BC families

Homeschool coding and math in BC: common questions

How do I register for home education in BC?

The School Act says a parent must register the child on or before September 30 each year with a BC school of the parent's choice, a francophone school where eligible, or a BC independent school. Ask that school about its process.

Do I have to follow the BC curriculum when homeschooling?

The School Act asks the parent to provide an educational program but does not list subjects for home education. Many families use parts of the BC curriculum anyway. The ministry's guidance, which we could not reach today, may say more, so check it.

What does the school I register with have to provide?

The School Regulation says it must offer evaluation and assessment services so parents can see progress compared with students of similar age and ability, and the loan of educational resource materials authorised and recommended by the minister.

Is an online learning school the same as homeschooling in BC?

No. The School Act defines online learning as instruction that relies primarily on internet communication between students and teachers. A student in an online learning school is enrolled, while a home-educated child is registered under section 13.

Can a home-educated BC teen take courses for credit?

The Regulation says a registered child in Grade 10, 11 or 12 may enroll in all or part of a program delivered through online learning by a board or independent school. Enrolling in a non-online board program ends the home-education registration. We do not grant provincial credits.

Do your classes count as enrolment in a school?

No. Our classes are private lessons that a family adds to its own program. They are not a BC school or an online learning school, and they do not enrol a child anywhere.

Can home-educated students in BC write math contests?

CEMC contests run through schools, since only educators can open a CEMC school account. The Canadian Mathematical Society offers an Independent Students route for the COMC, with in-person Open Writing Centres hosted by some university partners.

How much coding and math is enough each week?

There is no official figure. Our sample plan for a Grade 10 teen uses two live hours and about eighty minutes of solo work each week.

When are classes for BC families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Most of BC is on UTC-7 all year, so times stay fixed.

Is this page legal advice?

No. It quotes the School Act and School Regulation so families can find the text quickly. Read the official versions and ask the school you register with before deciding anything.
