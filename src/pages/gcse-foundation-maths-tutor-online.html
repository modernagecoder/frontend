GCSE Foundation maths tutor online

A Foundation tier student does not need the hardest question on the paper to pass GCSE Maths. Grade 4 is earned on the common ground: percentages, ratio, equations, area, probability, done accurately and written down. AQA's examiners said as much about the June 2025 Foundation papers. Most students had time to finish, and many marks were lost not to difficult maths but to habits: working out by hand on a calculator paper, leaving out steps the question asked for, multiplying by 100 instead of 1000. This page explains what grades 4 and 5 mean, sets out where Foundation students lost marks in 2025, and gives the plan we tutor to, with four worked examples of our own.

In short

GCSE Maths Foundation tier is the entry level of the GCSE Maths exam in England, awarding grades 1 to 5, and it is sat by students in Years 10 and 11 and by resit students on AQA, Pearson Edexcel or OCR. A Foundation maths tutor helps a student secure grade 4 and push for grade 5 by fixing the common-ground topics and exam habits that carry most marks. Grade 4 and 5

What is the highest grade on GCSE Foundation maths, and what does grade 4 mean?

The highest grade on Foundation tier is 5. Grade 4 is the line most students, schools and colleges watch, and Ofqual set it with a particular meaning.

Every board awards Foundation tier grades from 1 to 5; Pearson's specification says simply "Foundation tier: grades 1 to 5." When Ofqual set up the 9 to 1 scale for the first awards in 2017, it fixed two anchor points that matter for Foundation students. "Broadly the same proportion of students will achieve a grade 4 and above as currently achieve a grade C and above." And "Grade 5 will be positioned in the top third of the marks for a current Grade C and bottom third of the marks for a current Grade B." Ofqual also cautioned that "it is not right to say simply that a new grade 4 will equal a current grade C": the match is at the bottom of the grade.

What Foundation papers are made of (Pearson Edexcel 1MA1 specification, Foundation tier)
Part of the paperShare of Foundation marksWhat it asks for
AO1: use and apply standard techniques50 per centRecall, notation, routine methods and multi-step tasks
AO2: reason, interpret and communicate25 per centDeductions, chains of reasoning, explanations
AO3: solve problems25 per centTurning a situation into maths and interpreting the answer
Number22 to 28 per centThe biggest topic areas on Foundation, with ratio
Ratio, proportion and rates of change22 to 28 per centPercentages, value for money, scales, speed
Algebra17 to 23 per centExpressions, equations, sequences, graphs

Half of the Foundation marks go to standard techniques, and number and ratio together take nearly half of the content. That is the arithmetic of a grade 4. A student who is fluent and accurate on routine number, ratio and equations has most of the marks they need before the problem-solving questions begin. Ofqual's 2014 statement also confirmed that "grades 4 and 5 available through both tiers", so a Foundation student is not sitting a lesser exam for those grades.

Why grade 4 carries weight after Year 11

Pearson's specification states that "Students are expected to continue with their study of GCSE Mathematics after the age of 16 if they have not achieved the qualification at Key Stage 4."

In practice, a student who leaves Year 11 below grade 4 usually keeps studying maths in sixth form or college. Our resit page sets out the post-16 rule in full.

Is grade 5 realistic on Foundation?

Yes, for a student who is secure at grade 4 by the spring of Year 11. Grade 5 sits higher up the same paper, so the route is accuracy on the later, longer questions rather than new Higher-only content.

If a student is scoring well into grade 5 on full Foundation papers, the tier question is worth reopening with the school; our Foundation vs Higher page explains how.

Sources: Ofqual, Setting standards for new GCSEs in 2017 (12 September 2014), and the Pearson Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Mathematics 1MA1 specification, Issue 2. Read on 31 October 2026.

Where marks go

Where did Foundation students lose marks in the June 2025 GCSE Maths papers?

AQA publishes a report on every paper. Its June 2025 Foundation calculator reports (8300/2F and 8300/3F) are unusually blunt, and most of what they describe is fixable in weeks.

From AQA's June 2025 Foundation reports (8300/2F and 8300/3F), summarised
What happenedWhat AQA wroteType of loss
Calculator left unused"some marks were lost due to arithmetical errors being made" when students worked by hand on a calculator paperHabit
Working not shown"Students did not always show working when instructed to do so"Habit
Angles in similar shapes"Only 25% of the cohort appreciated that the angle remained constant"Knowledge
Showing expressions are equivalent"Less than one third were able to successfully show the two expressions were equivalent"Algebra
Unit conversions"A common error here was to use 100 instead of 1000."Recall
Probability treesSome "thought they needed to add the probabilities" instead of multiplyingKnowledge
Forming an equation from a perimeterDescribed as "answered very poorly"Algebra

Read the right-hand column. Of the seven losses, two are pure habit and one is a single fact (there are 1000 metres in a kilometre, 1000 grams in a kilogram). None of those three needs new mathematics. They need practice in the way the paper is sat. The 2F report also recorded that "the vast majority had sufficient time to complete the paper", so speed is rarely the problem. Accuracy and layout are.

Higher-crossover questions

The 3F report noted that "Some of the questions that were common with the Higher tier proved challenging for students on this tier." These are the grade 4 and 5 questions, and they reward secure algebra.

Good news on routine work

On a unitary method question, AQA recorded "just over 80% completing the question successfully". Foundation students can do this mathematics; the gaps are narrow and named.

Answer what was asked

On a relative frequency question, many students "calculated 132 correctly but then were unsure how to answer". A correct number is not the answer if the question wants a fraction or decimal.

Source: AQA, GCSE Mathematics 8300/2F and 8300/3F, Reports on the examination, June 2025, from the AQA 8300 assessment resources. The findings come from AQA papers, but the topics are common to every board's Foundation tier.

The plan

How do you get a grade 4 in GCSE Foundation maths?

Bank the routine marks, build the common-ground topics, and only then stretch to the last questions. The split and the steps are our own; the evidence for them is above.

Our "bank, build, stretch" split for a Foundation student
StageWhat it coversHow we practise it
BankHabit and recall marks: calculator use on Papers 2 and 3, units, working on every line, answering in the form askedA five-point checklist on every question for six weeks, until it is automatic
BuildThe common ground: percentages, ratio, equations, area and perimeter, angles, probabilityShort topic sets, then the same topic inside a mixed paper
StretchThe longer questions near the end, including those shared with HigherOne crossover question per lesson, worked slowly and then retried a week later
  1. Find the starting grade. One full past paper on the student's board, timed, marked against the scheme. That is the honest baseline.
  2. Sort every lost mark. Habit, recall, or understanding. Habit and recall losses are fixed first, because they are fastest.
  3. Put a calculator in the hand. On Papers 2 and 3, every calculation that can go into a calculator does. AQA's 2025 reports show this alone saves marks.
  4. Learn the short recall list. Metric conversions, angle facts, area formulae, fraction, decimal and percentage equivalents. Five minutes a day.
  5. Build the common ground. One topic a week from number, ratio and algebra, tested in a mixed paper the week after.
  6. Add one stretch question a lesson. Crossover questions are where grade 4 becomes grade 5.
  7. Full papers from February of Year 11. Timed, marked, logged, so the summer papers feel ordinary.

Group or 1-on-1 for Foundation?

A group of 15 to 20 Foundation students suits a learner who is close to grade 4 and needs steady practice and the push of a class.

1-on-1 suits a student well below grade 4, a student who freezes in class, or a resit student with a few months and a very specific list of gaps.

What a tutor cannot do

Promise a grade. We tell families what the papers show, month by month, and what would move the result.

Replace the practice between lessons. Twenty minutes a day on the recall list does more than an extra hour once a week.

Worked examples

Four Foundation questions we wrote, worked step by step

Our own questions on the topics AQA's June 2025 Foundation reports flagged. They are not taken from any board's papers.

1. Similar shapes: the angle does not change

Question (ours). Triangle Q is an enlargement of triangle P with scale factor 3. In P, one angle is 40° and the side opposite it is 5 cm. Find the matching angle and side in Q.

Side. Lengths scale: 5 × 3 = 15 cm.

Angle. Angles do not scale. An enlargement keeps the shape, so the angle is still 40°.

Check. If angles tripled, a triangle's angles would add to 540°, which is impossible. That one-line check is worth teaching.

2. Forming an equation from a perimeter

Question (ours). An equilateral triangle has sides 3x + 2, 5x − 6 and another side. Find x and the perimeter.

Use the fact. Equilateral means all sides are equal, so 3x + 2 = 5x − 6.

Solve. 2 + 6 = 5x − 3x, so 8 = 2x and x = 4. Each side is 3 × 4 + 2 = 14 cm.

Perimeter. 3 × 14 = 42 cm. The equation comes from the shape's property, not from adding the expressions together.

3. A probability tree: multiply along, add between

Question (ours). The probability that a bus is late is 0.2, and independently that a train is late is 0.3. Find the probability that both are late, and that exactly one is late.

Both late. Along one branch, multiply: 0.2 × 0.3 = 0.06.

Exactly one. Bus late and train on time: 0.2 × 0.7 = 0.14. Bus on time and train late: 0.8 × 0.3 = 0.24. Two separate outcomes, so add: 0.14 + 0.24 = 0.38.

Rule. Multiply along a path; add different paths. That sentence fixes the error AQA described.

4. A best buy, compared fairly

Question (ours). Cereal A is 750 g for 3.15 (in pounds). Cereal B is 500 g for 2.20. Which is better value?

Same quantity. Price per 100 g. A: 3.15 ÷ 7.5 = 0.42. B: 2.20 ÷ 5 = 0.44.

Answer with a reason. A is better value, because 100 g of A costs 42p and 100 g of B costs 44p.

Habit. The calculator does the division, and the answer is written as a sentence with both figures in it.

Practice set (ours, answers below). (1) Convert 3.4 km to metres. (2) Increase 60 by 35 per cent. (3) Share 72 in the ratio 5 : 3. (4) Solve 4x − 7 = 2x + 9. (5) A spinner lands on red with probability 0.35; it is spun 200 times; how many reds would you expect? (6) A square has the same perimeter as a rectangle measuring 9 cm by 5 cm; find the side of the square. Answers: (1) 3400 m; (2) 81; (3) 45 and 27; (4) x = 8; (5) 70; (6) the perimeter is 28 cm, so the side is 7 cm.

Exam habits

The five-point checklist we use on every Foundation question

Our own checklist, written from the losses AQA described. Students tick it on practice papers until they stop needing it.

  1. Is a calculator allowed? If so, use it for every calculation, including the easy-looking ones.
  2. Have I written the working? One step per line, especially where the question says "show".
  3. Are the units right? Metres or kilometres, grams or kilograms, pence or pounds, and 1000 not 100.
  4. Is the answer in the form asked for? A fraction, a decimal, a percentage, a sentence, a ratio in its simplest form.
  5. Does it make sense? A probability between 0 and 1, angles that add up properly, a price that is not absurd.

Formulae help too. Ofqual requires a formulae sheet in the 2025, 2026 and 2027 GCSE Maths exams, on every board and in the November series. It lists formulae students would otherwise have to memorise, so Foundation students should practise with a copy of their own board's sheet beside them, and know where each formula is on it before the exam.

Foundation questions

What families ask about Foundation maths tutoring

What is the highest grade on GCSE Foundation maths?

Grade 5. Foundation tier awards grades 1 to 5 on every board, and Ofqual set the scale so that grades 4 and 5 are available on both tiers.

Is a grade 4 the same as a C?

Not exactly. Ofqual set grade 4 so that broadly the same proportion of students get a 4 or above as used to get a C or above, but it warned against saying a 4 simply equals a C.

How many marks do you need for a grade 4 on Foundation?

It changes every year and by board, because grade boundaries are set after each series is marked. The safe approach is to aim for a comfortable margin above recent boundaries on full past papers.

What topics come up most on Foundation papers?

Number and ratio, proportion and rates of change are the biggest areas: Pearson gives each 22 to 28 per cent of Foundation marks. Algebra takes 17 to 23 per cent, and geometry and statistics the rest.

Why do Foundation students lose easy marks?

AQA's June 2025 reports name habits: working by hand on calculator papers, not showing working when asked, unit slips such as using 100 instead of 1000, and answering in the wrong form.

Can my child get a grade 5 on Foundation?

Yes. Grade 5 is the top of the Foundation scale, reached by accuracy on the later questions, including ones shared with Higher. A student securely in grade 5 might discuss the tier with their school.

Is there a formula sheet for Foundation maths?

Yes. Ofqual requires a formulae sheet in GCSE Maths in 2025, 2026 and 2027, at both tiers and in the November series. Practise with your own board's sheet.

Do you tutor AQA, Edexcel and OCR Foundation?

Yes. The Foundation content is the same on every board, and we practise with the past papers of the board your school uses.

When are lessons?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. All times are UK time.

Can you guarantee a grade 4?

No, and nobody honestly can. We show families what each full paper says, month by month, and we are not connected with any exam board.
