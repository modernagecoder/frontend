### The teacher picks the language

The ministry's January 2018 guide says the choice of language for both courses is left to individual teachers. Its suggestions for Computer Science 20 are Python, Java, PHP, JavaScript and Visual Basic; for Computer Science 30 it lists Python, Processing, Java, PHP and JavaScript.

### Blocks only to begin

Blockly and Scratch are suggested as a starting environment, but the guide says a visual tool should not be the core language of Computer Science 20, and that students should start coding as soon as possible. It even suggests a Karel the Robot style language as the bridge.

### Four ways of thinking

The guide names four dimensions of computational thinking: decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction and algorithm design. They are the habits we teach from the first class, long before a student meets either course.

Computer Science 20 has five goals, from core principles and programming fundamentals to computing and society, career exploration and a student-directed project. The last one is the most demanding: outcome CS20-SD1 asks students to develop a coding project or research a topic of their own choice. A student who arrives in Computer Science 20 already comfortable with functions and lists can spend that project on something ambitious, rather than on learning syntax in the last weeks of the semester.

Our classes run alongside Computer Science 20, Computer Science 30 and the math courses, and we do not grant Saskatchewan credits. Credits are earned at school. We are not connected with the Saskatchewan Ministry of Education or any school division.

## Pre-Calculus 30 and Calculus 30, and how they meet programming

The same curriculum site lists the senior math courses: Foundations of Mathematics, Pre-Calculus, Workplace and Apprenticeship Mathematics, and Calculus 30. Two of them matter most to students heading for computer science or engineering.

**Selected Pre-Calculus 30 and Calculus 30 outcomes (Saskatchewan Ministry of Education, curriculum.gov.sk.ca; groupings ours)**

| Course | Outcomes | What the outcomes cover |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-Calculus 30 | P30.1 to P30.5 | Angles in degrees and radians, the unit circle and six trigonometric ratios, trigonometric graphs, equations and identities |
| Pre-Calculus 30 | P30.6 to P30.11 | Operations on and compositions of functions, transformations, inverses, logarithms, polynomials up to degree 5, radical and rational functions |
| Pre-Calculus 30 | P30.12 and P30.13 | Permutations with the fundamental counting principle, combinations and the binomial theorem |
| Calculus 30 | C30.3 to C30.6 | Limits and continuity, differentiation as a rate of change, curve sketching, optimization and related rates |

Counting is where Pre-Calculus 30 and Computer Science 30 overlap most. P30.12 asks students to demonstrate understanding of permutations, including the fundamental counting principle, and a short recursive function that lists every arrangement of four letters makes the formula visible: 24 arrangements, printed one by one. We use exactly that exercise in our own classes, written by us, to connect the two courses.

### Logarithms in code

P30.9 covers evaluating logarithms, laws of logarithms and solving equations. A binary search that halves a sorted list each step finishes in about log base 2 of n steps, which turns an abstract outcome into a number a student can measure.

### Calculus 30

Calculus 30 adds limits, derivatives and their applications. Students who write a few lines of Python to estimate a slope from shrinking intervals see what a limit is doing before they are asked to prove anything about it.

## How big is a typical Saskatchewan park? Mean and median on the province's own file

The Government of Saskatchewan publishes its parks boundaries on GeoHub. Our students download the 172 records, convert the areas to square kilometres and find that the average tells a misleading story. All the figures here are ours.

**Our calculation on Saskatchewan Parks Boundaries, queried 22 October 2026: area per park type in square kilometres (area as computed by the province's map service; our conversion and rounding)**

| PARKTYPE in the file | Rows | Mean area | Median area |
|---|---|---|---|
| RS, recreation site | 104 | 3.22 | 0.58 |
| PA, protected area | 24 | 2.37 | 0.49 |
| PP-N, natural environment provincial park | 12 | 583.02 | 235.42 |
| PP-R, recreation provincial park | 11 | 24.27 | 5.73 |
| PP-H, historic provincial park | 9 | 0.40 | 0.13 |
| HS, historic site | 8 | 0.02 | 0.01 |
| PP-W, wilderness provincial park | 4 | 1,207.50 | 1,151.70 |
| All 172 rows | 172 | 72.61 | 0.61 |

This short script produces the table from the downloaded file, using only Python 3 and its built-in statistics module:

```
import json
import statistics

with open("parks.json", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    parks = [feature["attributes"] for feature in json.load(f)["features"]]

by_type = {}
for park in parks:
    km2 = park["Shape__Area"] / 1_000_000
    by_type.setdefault(park["PARKTYPE"], []).append(km2)

for kind, areas in sorted(by_type.items(), key=lambda p: len(p[1]), reverse=True):
    print(kind, len(areas),
          "mean", round(statistics.mean(areas), 2),
          "median", round(statistics.median(areas), 2))

every = [a for areas in by_type.values() for a in areas]
print("all", len(every), "mean", round(statistics.mean(every), 2),
      "median", round(statistics.median(every), 2))
```

### 1. Convert the units

The Shape__Area field is in square metres, because the service stores the boundaries in a metric map projection. Dividing by 1,000,000 gives square kilometres. Writing the divisor as 1_000_000 lets Python read it while a person can still count the zeros.

### 2. Mean against median

Across all 172 rows the mean is 72.61 km2 but the median is 0.61 km2. A few huge northern parks pull the mean up: Lac La Ronge Provincial Park alone is about 3,191.9 km2, while 99 of the 172 rows are under 1 km2. The median is the honest answer to how big a typical park is.

### 3. Check the labels

The dataset description lists protected areas as PR, but the file itself uses PA for those 24 rows. A careful student trusts the data over the description, then writes the mismatch down so the next reader is not caught out.

### What the numbers leave out

Areas come from the service's map projection, so treat the second decimal as approximate. The description says the boundaries were last updated on 22 September 2021, so a park created since then may be missing. And a park's area says nothing about how many people visit it or what it offers.

For a stronger student the next step is a chart: a histogram of park areas on a normal scale is one tall bar and a long empty tail, while the same data on a logarithmic scale spreads out neatly, which ties straight back to the logarithms of Pre-Calculus 30. ## Saskatchewan's one clock, and the Time Act, 2026 bill

Saskatchewan does not change its clocks, and the time zone database has it at UTC-6 through the year. Neighbouring provinces changed in 2026, which matters for anyone scheduling across the Prairies.

**Prairie and India clocks from 1 November 2026 (offsets from IANA tzdb 2026e and provincial time laws; the example times and conversions are ours)**

| Place | Offset all year | A 4 pm class in Saskatchewan is, there | Same moment in India |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saskatchewan | UTC-6 | 4:00 pm | 3:30 am the next day |
| Alberta | UTC-6 | 4:00 pm | 3:30 am the next day |
| Manitoba | UTC-5 | 5:00 pm | 3:30 am the next day |

### The bill as printed

Bill No. 58, The Time Act, 2026, defines central standard time as UTC-6 hours and says Saskatchewan includes the City of Lloydminster. Its last section says it comes into force by order of the Lieutenant Governor in Council, and the assent line on the copy we read is blank, so we treat it as a bill, not yet as law in force.

### A shared Prairie clock

With Alberta at UTC-6 all year and Manitoba at UTC-5 all year from November 2026, Regina and Calgary now always read the same time and Winnipeg is always an hour ahead. Saskatoon families can schedule with Alberta relatives without a seasonal adjustment.

### Phone numbers

Saskatchewan numbers with the 306, 474 and 639 area codes work in our booking form. Enter the ten digits; the +1 is supplied.

For our teachers in India, a Saskatchewan class is the simplest in Canada to plan: India is eleven and a half hours ahead every week of the year. A Saturday class at 9 am in Regina is 8:30 pm the same day in India, all year, which is why weekend mornings are a comfortable fit. We still write every confirmation in Saskatchewan time.

## A mean is easy to compute and easy to misread. So is AI output

The parks project shows the habit we care about most: checking whether a correct-looking number answers the question asked.

Ask an AI tool for the average size of a Saskatchewan park and it may compute 72.61 km2 correctly and still mislead, because the typical park is under a square kilometre. Spotting that takes a student who knows what a mean and a median do, and who looks at the data before trusting a summary of it.

Our students write their own analysis first, then compare it with what a tool produces, and explain any difference. Teens use AI tools only within each provider's age rules, with parental permission where a provider requires it; younger children learn how AI predicts things by building small programs, not by using adult tools.

Computer Science 20 lists privacy and the impact of technology among its outcomes; judging AI output is the newest part of that same job. The longer argument is in [is coding still worth learning in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
