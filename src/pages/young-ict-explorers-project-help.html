Young ICT Explorers project help

Young ICT Explorers invites Australian students to solve a real problem or express an idea with technology, then explain it to judges. The ideas are rarely the hard part; students tend to have more than they can build. What separates a strong entry from a half-finished one is usually planning: choosing what the project must do, building that well, and leaving time for the report and the review. This page explains the competition as its organisers publish it, with every rule and date checked at source, then shows a simple prioritising method any team can use. We are independent of Young ICT Explorers, and the project always stays the students' own work.

In short

Young ICT Explorers is a non-profit national digital technology competition for Australian students in Years 3 to 12, founded by SAP. Students enter individually or in teams of up to four, in year categories from 3-4 to 11-12, submit a written report of no more than 5 pages, and present to judges. In 2026, documentation was due 31 July, and national judging runs 9 to 21 November.

How it works

Entry, categories and the 2026 calendar

The organisers describe a YICTE project as a student-created digital solution that responds to a real-world need. Here is how entry works, as published for 2026.

Young ICT Explorers 2026 key dates, as published by the organisers
When (2026)What happens

Students can enter alone or in teams of up to four, in the category matching their school year: 3-4, 5-6, 7-8, 9-10 or 11-12. Every entry includes a written report of no more than five pages, and each participating student needs a project talent release form completed by a parent or guardian.

Project files go into a ZIP that must not exceed 20 MB. Larger videos can be shared as private links in the report, as the FAQ explains.

Reviews are the judging rounds. Most teams present live over Zoom in a 10-minute session: 5 minutes to pitch, present or demonstrate, then 5 minutes of questions and feedback. Teams that cannot attend may submit a pre-recorded video of up to 8 minutes instead.

Every team receives written feedback and a certificate of achievement or participation. The organisers note that all dates are subject to change, so their key dates page is the place to confirm the following year's calendar.

Sources: YICTE 2026 key dates; YICTE rules; YICTE FAQ. Read 7 October 2026.

Judging

Four criteria, and what they mean in practice

Every entry is first judged in a Core Award category for its year level. Optional awards, such as those for cybersecurity and privacy, sustainability and the use of AI, are only open to projects already entered in a core category.

Core judging criteria, as listed by the organisers, with what judges ask
CriterionQuestions the organisers listWhat it means for a team
Creativity and InnovationDoes the project show original thinking, and what makes it different from similar products?Know the existing tools and say clearly what yours does that they do not
Level of DifficultyHow challenging was it for the students' year level, and how were problems solved?Keep a record of problems met and how each was overcome
Quality and CompletenessHow well does it meet its objectives, and is it a working product or a prototype?A small thing that works beats a big thing that half works
Documentation and PresentationIs it explained clearly in the review, and is the report well structured?Plan the report and the 5-minute pitch, do not leave them to the last night

The organisers welcome both fully developed solutions and prototypes. That matters for planning: a team does not need a polished product, but it does need a working core it can demonstrate and explain.

The suggested report structure covers the introduction and how the idea was chosen, the design and features with labelled images, research, problems encountered, a user manual and a conclusion on what the team learned and would change.

Project types the organisers list include apps and web tools, games and interactive media, robotics and hardware, AI and data projects, and sustainability and community solutions. Anything students imagine, design and build with digital technologies is eligible.

Projects must be suitable for all ages. On copyright questions, such as using music in a video, the FAQ points students to the Copyright Act 1968 and the Australian Copyright Council rather than giving legal advice.

Sources: YICTE Core Awards; What is a YICTE project. Read 7 October 2026.

Worked lesson

MoSCoW: choosing what the project must do

MoSCoW sorts every feature into Must, Should, Could and Won't (this time). It is a simple way to make sure the working core gets built first. The project below is invented for this lesson.

The idea

Three Year 8 students want a lost property finder for their school: staff log found items with a photo, and students search for what they lost.

The time

From mid June to the 31 July deadline is about six weeks. Three students working two hours a week each gives 36 hours in total.

The rule

Plan the Must features to use no more than about 60 percent of the time, so problems, testing and the report have room.

The team's feature list with estimated hours and MoSCoW labels (invented example)
FeatureEstimate (hours)LabelRunning total of Must and Should
Add a found item with photo and description6Must6
Search by item type and colour5Must11
Written report, no more than 5 pages6Must17
Test plan and test log3Must20
Mark an item as claimed3Should23
Sort results by date found2Should25
Simple staff login5Should30
Email notice when a matching item is logged4Couldnot counted
Photo recognition to suggest the item type8Couldnot counted
A separate phone app12Won't (this time)not counted
Sharing items between schools10Won't (this time)not counted

The Must features add up to 20 hours, about 56 percent of the 36 available, which leaves room for surprises. Adding the Should features brings the total to 30 hours. Of the two Could features, only the 4-hour email notice fits, taking the plan to 34 hours with 2 hours to spare; the photo recognition feature, at 8 hours, would push the team past its time. The two Won't items are not failures. Writing them down protects the team from being tempted halfway through, and they make an honest answer to the report question "What would you do differently next time?"

Using it with a real team

Teams estimate badly at first, usually far too low. The fix is to re-estimate each week: when a Must takes longer than planned, something lower down moves to Won't, rather than the deadline being missed.

Students should make the labels together and agree them, because the arguments about what is truly a Must are where the real design thinking happens.

Why judges notice

A team that can explain why it left features out shows exactly the judgement the Level of Difficulty and Quality and Completeness criteria reward.

MoSCoW is used by professional software teams for the same reason. Learning it on a school project builds a habit that lasts well beyond one competition.

Our role

Teaching the skills, never building the entry

Young ICT Explorers celebrates what students imagined, designed and built themselves. Our lessons build their skill so they can do exactly that.

Lessons cover the programming a project needs (app logic, data storage, simple web pages, Python for hardware and data), plus testing, writing clear documentation and practising a 5-minute pitch with questions afterwards.

For primary teams, we often start with block-based app making, which lets a Years 3-4 or 5-6 team produce a working app without fighting syntax.

We do not write a team's code, design its project, write its report or present for it. Judges ask how the project was made, and a team that did the work can answer.

We do not promise an award or a place in the national finals. We teach skills, planning and presentation, which are what the criteria reward.

Questions about Young ICT Explorers

What students, parents and teachers ask

What is Young ICT Explorers?

A non-profit national digital technology competition for Australian students in Years 3 to 12, founded by SAP, in which students create and explain a digital solution to a real need or a creative idea.

Who can enter, and in what categories?

Students in Years 3 to 12, individually or in teams of up to four, in categories by school year: 3-4, 5-6, 7-8, 9-10 and 11-12.

How long can the report be?

The rules say reports must not exceed 5 pages in total, and the project and report files must fit in a ZIP of no more than 20 MB.

What happens at the review?

Most teams present live on Zoom in a 10-minute session: 5 minutes to present or demonstrate and 5 minutes of questions. Teams that cannot attend can submit an 8-minute pre-recorded video.

What is MoSCoW prioritisation?

A planning method that labels each feature Must, Should, Could or Won't (this time), so the essential part of a project is built first and the rest is added only if time allows.

When are the 2026 national finals?

National judging runs from 9 to 21 November 2026, followed by the National Awards Ceremony on 26 November 2026, according to the organisers.

Does the project have to be finished?

No. The organisers say both fully developed solutions and prototypes are welcome. What matters is a working core that the team can demonstrate and explain.

Will you build our project?

No. We teach programming, testing, documentation and presenting. The project, its code and its report stay the students' own work.

Can a primary student enter alone?

Yes. The FAQ confirms individuals can enter, and a classmate may present on behalf of a sole entrant who cannot attend judging.

When should we start for next year?

The organisers say submissions can be made from the February launch. Starting early in the school year leaves time to build, test and write the report well before the deadline.
