Adam today A small town with a very large footnote Adam is cited constantly in Omani history and described in a mixture of firm dates and careful hedges. Both kinds of number end up in the same column. The birthplace of a ruling house Ahmad bin Said, who founded the Al Bu Said line, was born here. A town whose main historical record is one person is a town whose data is a handful of rows, and small tables are where imprecision does the most damage. A quarter still standing Harat al Busaid is described as one of the oldest lanes in Adam, with around 120 old houses and 6 defensive towers. One of those numbers was counted and one was estimated, and the sentence does not say which is which any more clearly than that. Dates that are firm and dates that are not A birth year of 1694 sits alongside a dynasty beginning about 1749. Every history is written like this. The trouble starts only when a spreadsheet treats both as integers, because integers have no way to remember that one of them was hedged. Arithmetic has no place to put the word about Subtraction takes two numbers and returns one. It has nowhere to record that one input was approximate, so it does not. The result is as precise-looking as the most precise input, which is exactly backwards: it should be as vague as the vaguest.

Subtract, because the question is obvious How old was he when the dynasty began? 1749 minus 1694 is 55. The answer is correct arithmetic on the numbers as entered, and it is printed with no hedge on it at all, because the hedge was never in the cell to begin with. 3. Put the hedge back and look again If about 1749 means within two years, the answer is 53 to 57. Within five years, 50 to 60. Within ten, 45 to 65. The honest answer is a window between four and twenty years wide, and 55 is only the middle of it. Why is losing the word about such a serious problem? Because the loss is invisible and permanent. Once 55 is written into a cell, nothing downstream can recover the fact that one of its inputs was hedged. Every later calculation inherits a confidence the underlying evidence never had, and each step makes the claim harder to walk back.

Does adding an exact number to an estimate improve it? No, and this is the part people find genuinely surprising.
