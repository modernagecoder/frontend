## Eight planets, two numbers each

The Jet Propulsion Laboratory's table of approximate planetary positions gives each orbit's average distance from the Sun in astronomical units (au), and how many degrees the planet travels in a century. Dividing 36,000 by that rate gives its year.

**From JPL's table: distance, year, and the Kepler ratio**

| Planet | Distance, au | Year, Earth years | Year squared divided by distance cubed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mercury | 0.387 | 0.241 | 1.0000 |
| Venus | 0.723 | 0.615 | 1.0000 |
| Earth and Moon | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.0000 |
| Mars | 1.524 | 1.881 | 1.0000 |
| Jupiter | 5.203 | 11.863 | 0.9991 |
| Saturn | 9.537 | 29.448 | 0.9998 |
| Uranus | 19.189 | 84.018 | 0.9990 |
| Neptune | 30.070 | 164.790 | 0.9988 |

The last column is the whole of Kepler's third law. For every planet, the year squared divided by the distance cubed comes out as 1 to within about a tenth of one per cent, when years and au are the units. The small shortfall for the giant planets is a question learners are invited to chase: the numbers are averages over two and a half centuries, and the big planets tug on one another.

## A straight line, a prediction, and a planet on the scales

Learners discover the law from data, test it on a planet they held back, and then use it as a measuring instrument.

### 1. Logs make it straight

Plot the logarithm of each year against the logarithm of each distance and the eight points fall on a line. Its slope, 1.4999 in our fit, is the power in the law: year grows as distance to the power three halves.

### 2. Predict what you hid

Fit only Mercury to Mars, then predict Neptune, thirty times further out. The four inner planets give 164.89 years; JPL's figure gives 164.79.

### 3. Weigh Jupiter

The same law holds for moons round a planet, with the constant set by the planet's mass. Distance cubed over period squared, in au and years, gives Jupiter's mass as a fraction of the Sun's.

**Jupiter weighed four times, from JPL satellite elements, run 25 September 2026**

| Moon | Distance from Jupiter, km | Orbit, days | Jupiter's mass as a fraction of the Sun's |
|---|---|---|---|
| Io | 421,800 | 1.762732 | 1 / 1,039 |
| Europa | 671,100 | 3.525463 | 1 / 1,032 |
| Ganymede | 1,070,400 | 7.155588 | 1 / 1,048 |
| Callisto | 1,882,700 | 16.690440 | 1 / 1,048 |

Four moons, four nearly identical answers: Jupiter carries roughly one thousandth of the Sun's mass. The two outer moons agree with each other almost exactly; the two inner ones give answers about one per cent different. Learners are asked to explain that rather than hide it, and the notes on JPL's table about how its average elements are defined are the place to start. The same calculation with our own Moon, 384,400 km away on a 27.322-day orbit, puts the Earth and Moon together at about one 330,000th of the Sun.

### Why this matters more when AI can recite the answer

Any chatbot will tell you Jupiter's mass. What it cannot give a learner is the experience of getting that number themselves from four moons and a law found in a table, and of noticing where the answers disagree. A person who has done that knows the difference between a fact and a measurement, and can judge an AI's confident numbers because they know how such numbers are made.

## What Kepler's law could not tell Kew's astronomers

The law gives every distance as a multiple of Earth's distance from the Sun. It says nothing about how many miles that is. A transit of Venus is one way to find out.

### Ratios, not miles

From Kepler alone, Venus orbits at 0.723 of Earth's distance, but the size of the whole plan is unknown. It is a map with no scale bar.

### Two views of a transit

Observers far apart on Earth see Venus cross the Sun along slightly different tracks. The gap depends on how far away Venus is, and that is the missing scale.

### Today it is fixed

The astronomical unit is now defined as exactly 149,597,870,700 metres, the value JPL lists from a 2012 resolution of the International Astronomical Union.

With that scale, Venus at a transit is about 0.277 au from Earth in a simple circular-orbit picture, around 41.4 million kilometres. Sixth formers can go on to model the two tracks and see how tiny the angles are that eighteenth-century observers had to measure. Pupils working towards GCSE physics or maths can follow the ratios, the log plot and the Jupiter calculation. The planetary figures are JPL's; every fit, prediction and mass on this page is our own calculation, run on 25 September 2026. ## AI knows Kepler's law already. Why should a Richmond teenager rediscover it?

Because knowing a law and being able to use it as a tool are different skills.

An AI can state Kepler's third law in a sentence and quote Jupiter's mass to several figures. A learner who has plotted JPL's numbers on log scales, seen the slope come out at one and a half, predicted Neptune from four inner planets and then weighed Jupiter from its moons has something the AI's sentence cannot give: a working method for pulling a law out of data and turning it into a measuring instrument. That method is what physicists, engineers and data scientists do every day.

Learners also practise a scientist's honesty: noticing that Io and Europa disagree slightly with the outer moons, and asking why instead of rounding it away. AI will keep supplying facts; people who know where facts come from will keep being the ones who find new ones.

Kew Observatory was built because people wanted to measure the heavens. A Richmond learner who can do some of that measuring with code is continuing a very local project. The longer argument is in [why teenagers should still learn to code in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).

### What is Kepler's third law?

That the square of a planet's orbital period is proportional to the cube of its average distance from the Sun. Using years and astronomical units, the ratio is 1, and JPL's figures give 1 to within about a tenth of a per cent for all eight planets.

### How can moons weigh a planet?

The same law applies to moons round a planet, but the constant depends on the planet's mass. Comparing the moons' distance cubed over period squared with the planets' gives the planet's mass relative to the Sun: about one thousandth for Jupiter.

### Where does the data come from?

From NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory Solar System Dynamics pages: the table of approximate planetary positions, and the mean elements of planetary satellites. Both are free to read.
