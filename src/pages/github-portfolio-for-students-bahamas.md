# Ninety seconds. Four steps. None of them involves running your code.

> Every student on the twelve-month track finishes with a public GitHub repository and something running at an address a stranger can open. This page explains what a reader actually does with that link, in four steps and about ninety seconds, and what a repository therefore has to contain to survive the encounter: a README a human would read, an error figure in a real unit, an explicit statement of what the project cannot do, and a commit history spread across the year rather than a single upload. Firm privacy rules apply to every student under eighteen.

## Why a link outperforms a certificate

An admissions reader or an interviewer with a stack of applications can do one of two things with a claim about coding ability. They can take it on trust, or they can click something.

A certificate offers the first. A repository offers the second, and the second is worth incomparably more, because it converts an assertion into evidence a reader can assess in ninety seconds.

That is the entire argument for treating publication as part of the teaching rather than something a student does afterwards, and it is why the fifth rung runs alongside the others from about the middle of the second quarter.

## What makes a student repository persuasive

| Element | The weak version | The version that works |
|---|---|---|
| History | One commit, on the final day, called final | Small commits spread across the weeks it took |
| README | The default file, untouched | What it does, how to run it, what the data was |
| Result | Ninety-four per cent accurate | Wrong by about four degrees, against a stated baseline |
| Limits | Not mentioned | One honest paragraph on where it stops working |
| Access | A screen recording | An address a stranger can open |

The first row is the one students most often get wrong and the one technical readers weight most heavily. A history of small dated commits cannot be manufactured at the end, which is precisely why it is trusted: it is the one signal in a student portfolio that is expensive to fake.

## The paragraph students resist hardest

Writing down what the project cannot do feels to a sixteen year old like undermining their own work. It is the opposite. A stated limitation is the clearest available signal that the author understands their own system, and its absence is the clearest signal that they do not.

We make it a required section, every project, every quarter. Students find it uncomfortable for roughly a term and then find it useful permanently, including in contexts that have nothing to do with software.

## Three things that sink an otherwise good repository

**Personal data.** Anything about identifiable people who did not agree to it. This is a rule about people rather than engineering, and it is absolute: no names, no contact details, no scraped profiles, nothing about classmates.

**Credentials committed by accident.** An API key in a config file, pushed publicly, is the single most common serious mistake in student repositories. We teach the environment-variable habit before there is anything worth protecting, because the habit has to predate the need.

**A repository nobody can run.** Instructions that assume the author's laptop. If a stranger cannot get it running from the README alone, the project is a private artefact with a public address.

## When to start

From about twelve, and from about the middle of the second quarter of the year, once there is something real to publish. Earlier than that there is nothing to put in it, and an empty account with a good name is not a portfolio.

What matters is that the first commit is early, because the asset being built is the history, and a history is the one thing that cannot be started later.
