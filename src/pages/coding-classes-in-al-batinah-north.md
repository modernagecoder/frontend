## The Al Batinah North Simpson's-paradox project

The learner receives two synthetic tables of task completion. Each row has wilayat, period, completed tasks and assigned tasks. The dashboard shows a surprising result: every wilayat improves, yet the combined governorate rate falls.

| Teaching group | Earlier completed | Earlier assigned | Earlier rate | Later completed | Later assigned | Later rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Saham | 90 | 100 | 90.0% | 95 | 100 | 95.0% |
| Liwa | 40 | 50 | 80.0% | 85 | 100 | 85.0% |
| Shinas | 14 | 20 | 70.0% | 150 | 200 | 75.0% |
| Al Khaburah | 6 | 10 | 60.0% | 65 | 100 | 65.0% |
| Combined | 150 | 180 | 83.3% | 395 | 500 | 79.0% |

Inside each subgroup, the rate rises by five percentage points. Saham moves from 90 to 95 percent, Liwa from 80 to 85, Shinas from 70 to 75, and Al Khaburah from 60 to 65.

Across all observed records, the rate falls from 83.3 to 79.0 percent. This happens because the later period contains a much larger share of assignments in groups whose rates are lower even after improvement.

This is Simpson's paradox. An aggregate rate is a weighted average. When the subgroup weights change, the combined direction can reverse the direction within every group.

The subgroup and aggregate statements can both be mathematically correct. They answer different questions. A wilayat improvement question belongs at subgroup level. A question about all observed assignments belongs at aggregate level and includes the changed composition.

The learner does not average the four percentages equally. Earlier denominators are 100, 50, 20 and 10. Later denominators are 100, 100, 200 and 100. The combined rate comes from total completed divided by total assigned.

| Audit field | Question answered | Failure prevented |
|---|---|---|
| Entity level | Wilayat or governorate? | Different questions are merged |
| Metric version | What counts as completed? | Definition changes look like progress |
| Numerator | How many tasks met the rule? | Percentage cannot be reconciled |
| Denominator | How many tasks were eligible? | Unlike populations are compared |
| Period | Are windows aligned? | Timing drives the result |
| Subgroup rate | What happened inside each group? | Reversal remains hidden |
| Composition | How did subgroup shares change? | Mix is mistaken for performance |
| Decision question | Which level should guide action? | Convenient trend is selected |

A fixed-mix or standardised comparison may isolate changes in subgroup rates from changes in composition. It answers a hypothetical question using documented weights. The adjusted result must be labelled and displayed separately from the observed total.

The interface shows numerators and denominators beside every rate. A stacked count chart reveals the composition shift. Automated tests confirm that subgroup counts sum exactly to the combined row.

If eligibility, task difficulty or the completion definition changes within a wilayat, the apparent subgroup improvement may also be invalid. Simpson's paradox is not permission to stop the audit after finding one explanation.

The final caption states both findings: every synthetic subgroup improved, while the combined observed rate fell because later records were concentrated more heavily in lower-rate groups.

Every task and count in the example is invented. It does not describe learners, schools, companies, hospitals, ports or public services in Al Batinah North.
