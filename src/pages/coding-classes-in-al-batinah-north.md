## Four improving wilayats and one falling total

Synthetic completion records demonstrate Simpson's paradox without claiming local performance.

### 1. Calculate each subgroup

The learner divides completed synthetic tasks by assigned tasks within Saham, Liwa, Shinas and Al Khaburah for two periods.

### 2. Read the surprising aggregate

Every wilayat improves by five percentage points, but the combined rate moves from 83.3 percent to 79.0 percent.

### 3. Inspect the changing weights

The later period contains many more assignments in the lower-rate groups. The aggregate answers a composition-weighted question, not the within-wilayat change question.

### Both directions can be mathematically correct

Within every named subgroup, the rate increases. Across the observed records, the combined rate decreases because the mix changes. A useful explanation states which question matters for the decision instead of selecting the more convenient direction.

## Eight checks before a trend arrow appears

Every rate retains its level, population, numerator, denominator and period.

| Check | Question | Failure if skipped | Safer action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Entity level | Wilayat or governorate? | Different questions are merged | Label level beside value |
| Numerator | What events count as success? | Definitions drift | Version the metric |
| Denominator | Who or what was eligible? | Rates use unlike populations | Publish counts |
| Period | Are the windows comparable? | Timing drives the direction | Align dates |
| Subgroup rate | What happened inside each group? | Aggregate hides reversal | Show disaggregation |
| Composition | How did subgroup weights change? | Mix is mistaken for performance | Display shares |
| Standardisation | Is a fixed-mix comparison needed? | Adjusted result looks observed | Label method and weights |
| Decision question | Which level should guide action? | Convenient trend is selected | State purpose before conclusion |

### Do not average percentages equally

Subgroup rates have different denominators. The observed total comes from combined counts, not the mean of four displayed percentages.

### Show the mix directly

A stacked bar or count table reveals that later assignments are concentrated differently. The reversal stops looking mysterious.

### Keep like groups comparable

If definitions or eligibility change within a wilayat, the apparent within-group improvement may also be invalid.

### Standardise for a defined question

A fixed set of weights can isolate rate changes from composition, but the adjusted result must remain separate from the observed aggregate.

### Explain both views

The report can say every subgroup improved while the observed combined rate fell because the record mix shifted.

### Test reconciliation

Automated checks confirm that subgroup numerators and denominators sum exactly to the displayed total.

### What is the Al Batinah North Simpson's-paradox project?

The learner audits two synthetic reporting periods. Completion improves inside Saham, Liwa, Shinas and Al Khaburah, yet the combined governorate rate falls because the later period contains a much larger share of records from lower-rate groups. The dashboard exposes subgroup numerators, denominators and composition before explaining the aggregate reversal.

### How can every wilayat improve while the total rate falls?

An aggregate rate is a weighted average of subgroup rates. If the mix of observations shifts toward groups with lower rates, the combined result can fall even when each group improves. The total and subgroup statements answer different questions, so the report needs both plus the changing weights.

### How does the learner prevent a misleading aggregate?

The learner keeps wilayat identifiers, displays every numerator and denominator, compares like groups across like periods, tests the composition shift, and states whether the decision concerns each wilayat or the region as a whole. Standardisation may answer a fixed-mix question, but it must be labelled and cannot replace the observed result.
