FAQ

Everything Canadian parents and learners ask in their first call.

Do classes run on my Canadian time zone?

Yes. Classes are set on your own clock: BC and Yukon stay on UTC-7 all year, Alberta, Saskatchewan and the Northwest Territories on UTC-6, Manitoba on UTC-5 from 1 November 2026, Ontario and Quebec on Eastern time, then Atlantic and Newfoundland time. Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

Do you follow my province’s curriculum?

We set each plan beside the course your child takes at school: coding in Ontario’s Grade 1 to 8 math curriculum, BC’s ADST and Computer Programming 11 and 12, Alberta’s CTS computing science courses, Quebec’s QEP, Manitoba’s Computer Science 20S to 40S, Saskatchewan’s Computer Science 20 and 30, and the courses in the other provinces and territories. We teach alongside the student’s school course and do not grant Ontario credits or credits in any other province. Tell us the course on the trial call.

Can you help with Waterloo, U of T, UBC, McGill or another Canadian CS programme?

We teach the programming and problem solving that first-year computer science assumes, prepare students for the CEMC’s Canadian Computing Competition, and explain each university’s published prerequisites in our guides to Waterloo, U of T and UBC. Admission decisions belong to the universities, so we make no admission promises.

Are classes taught in French?

Classes are taught in English, the language of most programming documentation. If your child learns math or science in French at school, tell us on the trial call so the teacher can connect the English terms to the ones used in class.

How are fees charged for Canadian families?

There is one monthly fee for eight live classes, two a week, in a group batch or 1-on-1. The pricing section shows the fee in your own currency. There is no registration fee, and you can cancel before the next billing cycle.

Are classes paused over Canadian school holidays?

You decide. You can stop for the December break, March Break or the summer by not renewing that month, with one message and no fee. Some families use a break for a focused project sprint instead; see our March Break and summer pages.

Will my child learn real AI?

Yes. Students build small AI apps from the ground up: prompting, structured output, retrieval, tool-calling agents and evaluation. The tools are chosen by age, so under-18s never use services that require users to be 18.

How is AI safety handled for younger children?

Under-13s never operate language-model tools on the open internet without a mentor present, and we choose tools by their own age rules: Claude is for users 18 and over, and ChatGPT needs a parent’s permission under 18. Parents see which tools are introduced each month.

Can siblings share a slot?

Siblings can join the same group batch when their levels are close; otherwise each does better in a class of their own. Ask on the trial call and we will suggest what fits.

How do we pay?

Payment is online by card when you enrol, billed monthly, with no joining fee. The pricing section shows the fee in your own currency.

Do you teach students from French-immersion and independent schools?

Yes. Tell us the course your child takes at school and the teacher plans around it, whatever kind of school it is, using the words your child already hears in class.

What's the cancellation policy?

The first trial is always free. After that, you can cancel before the next monthly billing cycle and you will not be charged again, with no fee and no contract. Refunds follow our refund policy.

Inside your first month

Week by week: what actually happens for a Canadian family.

A typical first month for a Grade 7 Canadian student starting from zero. Pacing adapts up or down for younger or older learners across all 13 provinces and territories.

Week 01

Trial & first contact

A free trial lesson of 30 to 45 minutes on your own clock. The teacher meets your child, gets a feel for any Scratch or school coding so far, and the course they take at school. Calendar invites follow for the next two classes.

Output: a personalised termly plan
Week 02

Foundations & computational thinking

First two real sessions. Sequencing, decomposition, debugging, the joy of "the bug is mine to fix." Older students start in Python in VS Code; under-9s start in Scratch. Always real, no drag-and-drop blocks past age 9.

Output: a tiny program, your child's own
Week 03

Python on a real laptop

VS Code installed, Python 3 running, terminal no longer scary. First original program, not from a tutorial, with input/output, variables, loops. Pushed to your child's first personal GitHub repository.

Output: first GitHub commit
Week 04

The first deploy

End-of-month checkpoint: a small project chosen by the student, an NHL stats lookup, a Toronto-weather widget, a Whistler powder-day predictor, a CEGEP study quiz, shipped to GitHub Pages or Vercel. You receive a written progress note.

Output: a live URL you can share

Inside a typical class

A live one-hour lesson, minute by minute.

An example of ours: one 1-on-1 class for a Grade 9 Canadian student in their third month. Group classes follow the same shape.

00 to 03 min

Hi + warm-up

Three minutes of "how was the week", the human bit. Mentor scrolls back the chat history, cameras come on. Settles the student.

03 to 10 min

Recap and tiny quiz

A short recall test of last lesson. Not for marks, for the brain. The mentor watches for the lightbulb moments and the cobwebs.

10 to 22 min

New concept + live coding

About twelve minutes of new material. Live-shared VS Code. Mentor types two lines, student types the next two. Wrong moves welcome. Errors read aloud.

22 to 48 min

Project work · the heart of the class

The student leads. The mentor coaches. This is where the actual learning happens, feature shipped, bug fixed, function refactored. The mentor types nothing; only asks.

48 to 56 min

Code review & reflection

Several minutes to look back. What's good? What could be cleaner? The student names one thing they're proud of. This bit matters more than it sounds.

56 to 60 min

Next steps + goodbye

Mentor writes a 4-line note to the parent: what was covered, how the student felt, optional homework, plan for next class. Cameras off. Done.

Further questions Canadian parents and learners ask before booking.

"Will the mentor coach Canadian Computing Competition (CCC) and the Canadian Computing Olympiad (CCO)?"

We prepare students for the CEMC’s CCC Junior and Senior with original practice problems of ours, and keep going with students who want to aim higher. CEMC lists the 2027 CCC for Wednesday 17 February 2027; students enter through a school, and CEMC asks home schools to contact it for help. See our CCC Junior and CCC Senior guides.

"Can the mentor help with the Waterloo Euclid contest or the AIF supplementary form?"

Euclid is a CEMC math contest for Grade 12, and our Euclid guide and Waterloo math contests course prepare it with original problems of ours. The AIF (Admission Information Form) is written by the student; our AIF guide explains what Waterloo asks, and a mentor can help a student describe their own projects clearly.

"My child is in French immersion. Will that work?"

Yes. Classes are in English, and code and its documentation are mostly in English anyway. Tell us the French terms your child uses at school and the teacher will connect the two.

"Can the mentor help with university essays beyond CS programmes?"

Mentors help with the technical sections of CS / engineering / data science applications. For non-technical essay coaching we usually point families at specialist services, we'd rather do one thing well than spread thin.

"Do you build on Hour of Code and other coding initiatives?"

Yes. Free coding initiatives are good first steps for Canadian kids. We pick up where they leave off: from "I tried Hour of Code at school" to "I have a real Python program on GitHub that I built with my mentor over six months."

"What about during March Break and the Christmas / Holiday period?"

You decide. Stopping for the December break or March Break just means not renewing that month, with one message and no fee. Some students use March Break for a focused project sprint instead.

"Can the mentor write a Common-App-style reference letter for US universities?"

For a student who has worked with a mentor for a while, the mentor can write a short factual note describing the projects built and the skills seen in class. Check what each programme accepts before you ask.

Common Canadian parent worries

Six honest answers to the questions Canadian parents type into Google at midnight.

The quiet ones. We'd rather just answer them in advance.

"My child already has too much screen time. Won't this make it worse?"

It’s a fair worry. Classes are one hour, twice a week, and most of that time the child is making something, not watching. There is a real difference between creating and scrolling, and the class is built around the first.

"Will my child learn fake 'kid Python' and not real programming?"

No. We start in Scratch for the youngest learners (Grade 1 to 3) and only as a bridge. Within the first months students write real, runnable Python that imports real libraries, handles real errors and pushes to a real GitHub repo. We deliberately avoid "Python for kids" tools that hide the real language behind a friendly mask.

"What if my child loses interest after a month? Am I locked in?"

No lock-in. You can cancel any month before the next billing cycle, no fee, no awkward conversation, no exit interview. If your child loses interest, we'd rather hear about it early and either pause for a few weeks or pivot the project track to something more exciting (game design, music programming, AI art) than have you grit your teeth through a year you paid for.

"Is AI safe for my 9-year-old? I've read worrying things."

Under-13s never use language-model tools on the open internet without a mentor present, and we follow each tool’s own age rules. The AI projects your child builds are educational and reviewable by you, prompt by prompt.

"My child is shy, won't a 1:1 video lesson be too much?"

Shy children often do well here. The first lesson is built around the code, not the camera: your child looks at VS Code, not at a stranger’s face. Mentors give long pauses, no pressure, and let the screen do the talking.

"Is this another bootcamp pretending to be a school?"

No. Bootcamps compress 12 weeks of intense, transactional study aiming at a job. We're the opposite: a slow, year-round mentorship that respects how Canadian school years actually work. We don't promise jobs after 12 weeks. We promise a real codebase, a real GitHub history, a real mentor relationship and a real shift in your child's relationship with maths, logic and computing.

"How does this compare to a coding bootcamp?"

An intensive bootcamp is a short, full-time course for adults. We are the opposite shape: steady, year-round live classes for all ages, including kids. Some adults use classes like ours before a bootcamp to get ready, or after one to keep going. See coding classes for adults in Canada.

"Can the mentor work with my Canadian high school's Specialist High Skills Major (SHSM)?"

Yes, as outside help. SHSM requirements are set by the school and the ministry, so check with your SHSM coordinator what counts. Our part is the technical depth behind the projects.

"Do you accept students from Canadian online and homeschool families?"

Yes. Live online classes fit homeschool rhythms well, with a group batch or 1-on-1 at a pace you set. See our homeschool guides for Ontario, BC and Alberta.

"Will the mentor handle technical setup on a Chromebook / school-issued laptop?"

Yes. Many Canadian schools issue Chromebooks. We have a Chromebook-friendly setup path (VS Code in the browser via GitHub Codespaces, or a Linux subsystem if the device allows). The mentor walks your child through the setup live in the first class.

"Can the mentor help me get ready for a university hackathon?"

Yes. A mentor can help a university student get ready for a hackathon: a team plan, a project small enough to finish, and the last steps of shipping it.

"Can you help with scholarship applications?"

We do not run or award scholarships. Where a scholarship asks for a portfolio or evidence of initiative, a mentor can help a student present their own projects clearly; the application is the student’s.

"Does the trial happen in our local time zone?"

Yes, always. The trial is booked on your own clock, wherever you are in Canada, and the Canada time zones guide sets out the 2026 rules.

"How quickly do you respond between lessons?"

Mentors aim to reply within one Canadian working day to written questions between classes, faster if there's a contest deadline or test approaching. For urgent setup problems before a class, WhatsApp tends to be the quickest channel.

"Will the mentor coach IB Computer Science alongside Canadian provincial curricula?"

Yes. We teach students who follow the IB Diploma in Canada. The mentor sets the weekly plan beside IB Computer Science, Higher Level included, and guides the Internal Assessment, which the student writes themselves.

"Will I get a transcript or report I can use with my child's school?"

Ask for a written progress summary: what was covered, what was built and what comes next. You can share it with your child’s teacher, SHSM coordinator or guidance counsellor.

"How do you compare with local Canadian coding schools?"

Local schools that teach in person suit many children well. We teach live online, on your clock, with real Python and a modern AI track. Our guide to choosing an online coding class in Canada lists the questions to ask any provider, us included.
