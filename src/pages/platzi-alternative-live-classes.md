### What a library does well

Range: if you want to sample design on Monday and SQL on Friday, a library lets you. Price per topic is usually low, and you can study at 2 a.m. on a phone in a taxi.

Revision: a recorded explanation can be replayed as often as you need, which suits people who like to go slowly through a hard idea alone.

### What a live class does well

Momentum: the class happens whether or not you feel like it, and a mentor expects you there. For many adults that single fact is the difference between finishing and not.

Correction: a mentor sees the mistake you did not know you made, asks why you wrote that line, and makes you explain it. A video cannot ask you a question back.

Many learners combine the two: a live course for the backbone of a skill, and a library for side topics. That is a perfectly sensible plan, and nothing about our classes stops you using any platform alongside them.

## Why do people forget what they watched? A spaced-repetition lesson in Python

One reason recorded courses feel productive and then fade is that watching is not recalling. A simple answer from learning science is the Leitner box system: test yourself, and space the reviews out as an idea sticks. This lesson builds it in Python.

### How the boxes work

Every idea starts in box 1 and comes back the next day. Recall it without help and it moves to box 2, which comes back after 3 days, then box 3, after 7. Forget it and it drops straight back to box 1.

The program tracks three ideas from a Python lesson (slicing, dict.get and f-strings) over 14 days, using an invented record of right and wrong answers. Each idea stores its box and the day it is next due.

### What the output shows

On day 4, dict.get is forgotten and falls from box 2 to box 1, so it returns on day 5. That is the system doing its job: the weak idea gets attention, the strong one (slicing) is left alone until day 11.

Across 14 days the three ideas need 12 reviews. Drilling all three every day would mean 42. Spacing is not about working less; it is about spending effort where memory is weakest.

**Leitner boxes for three Python ideas** (Python)
```python
# Leitner boxes: box 1 comes back after 1 day, box 2 after 3, box 3 after 7.
GAP = {1: 1, 2: 3, 3: 7}

# Our invented record: True means recalled without help.
answers = {
    'slicing':   [True, True, True],
    'dict.get':  [True, False, True, True, True],
    'f-strings': [False, False, True, True, True],
}
box = {k: 1 for k in answers}
due = {k: 1 for k in answers}
reviews = 0

for day in range(1, 15):
    today = [k for k in answers if due[k] == day]
    for k in today:
        ok = answers[k].pop(0)
        reviews += 1
        box[k] = min(box[k] + 1, 3) if ok else 1
        due[k] = day + GAP[box[k]]
    if today:
        print('day', day, today, '->', box)

print('reviews:', reviews, '| daily drilling:', 14 * 3)
```

**What it prints** (Output)
```output
day 1 ['slicing', 'dict.get', 'f-strings'] -> {'slicing': 2, 'dict.get': 2, 'f-strings': 1}
day 2 ['f-strings'] -> {'slicing': 2, 'dict.get': 2, 'f-strings': 1}
day 3 ['f-strings'] -> {'slicing': 2, 'dict.get': 2, 'f-strings': 2}
day 4 ['slicing', 'dict.get'] -> {'slicing': 3, 'dict.get': 1, 'f-strings': 2}
day 5 ['dict.get'] -> {'slicing': 3, 'dict.get': 2, 'f-strings': 2}
day 6 ['f-strings'] -> {'slicing': 3, 'dict.get': 2, 'f-strings': 3}
day 8 ['dict.get'] -> {'slicing': 3, 'dict.get': 3, 'f-strings': 3}
day 11 ['slicing'] -> {'slicing': 3, 'dict.get': 3, 'f-strings': 3}
day 13 ['f-strings'] -> {'slicing': 3, 'dict.get': 3, 'f-strings': 3}
reviews: 12 | daily drilling: 42
```
Exact output from running the program with Python 3.14. The right and wrong answers are invented for the lesson.

In our live classes, the mentor plays the part of the boxes: the first minutes of each lesson go back to whatever the learner struggled with last time, and ideas that are solid are left alone. With a library you can do the same yourself, and this little program is a fair place to start.

1. **Predict.** If slicing had been forgotten on day 4, on which day would it come back?
2. **Change a rule.** Make box 3 return after 14 days and count the reviews again.
3. **Find the crash.** Shorten one list of answers and explain, in English, why Python raises IndexError.
4. **Use it.** Replace the three ideas with three things you are learning this month.

## How do you choose an online coding course in Peru?

The same eight checks work for a platform, a bootcamp, a university extension course or us.

1. **Name the goal.** A job, a project, an exam or curiosity. Each points to a different course.
2. **Check the format in writing.** Recorded, live, or a mix, and how much of each.
3. **Ask who reads your code.** A person, a test suite, peers, or nobody.
4. **Check the language.** Which language every lesson and every piece of feedback is in.
5. **Try before paying.** Platzi opens the first three classes of any course; we offer a free live class. Use them.
6. **Look at a finished project.** Ask what a learner at the end can build, and see one.
7. **Read the stopping rules.** Pauses, cancellation and renewal, before you pay.
8. **Decide after a month.** Can you now do something alone that you could not before?
