Plenty of children in Germany play games every day; far fewer have made one. Making a game turns a child from player into designer: they decide the rules, test them, and find out why their first version is too easy or impossible. Younger children build small games in blocks; older ones type real scripts. In every case the child designs the rules first, which is where most of the learning happens.

### Design before code

Every project starts on paper: what is the goal, what stops the player, how do you win or lose. Children who skip this step build games that are either impossible or over in ten seconds, and they learn that quickly.

Then the game is tested, by the child, by the group and sometimes by a younger sibling, and changed. That loop of testing and fixing is the real skill.

### Making, not copying

We do not hand children finished game templates to recolour, and they do not paste code from tutorials or AI tools. Each mechanic is built and understood.

The result is slower at first and much faster later, because a child who knows why their jump works can build the next one alone.

## How do German age ratings for games work?

In Germany, the age on a game box comes from the Jugendschutzgesetz, and for games the testing is organised by the USK. Here is what the labels mean, in the words of the law and the USK.

**The five German age labels for games, under paragraph 14 of the Jugendschutzgesetz**

| Label in the law | In English | What the USK says about such games |
|---|---|---|
| Freigegeben ohne Altersbeschränkung | No age restriction | Gives no information on whether a game is suitable for pre-school children or educationally valuable |
| Freigegeben ab sechs Jahren | From age 6 | Mostly family games that may be more exciting and competitive, such as fast racing, building and strategy games |
| Freigegeben ab zwölf Jahren | From age 12 | Often clearly more combat-focused, framed by historical, comic, futuristic or fairy-tale stories |
| Freigegeben ab sechzehn Jahren | From age 16 | Games aimed at older teenagers and adults |
| Keine Jugendfreigabe | No youth release | For adults only |

Sources: Jugendschutzgesetz, paragraph 14 (2) and (2a), gesetze-im-internet.de; USK, "Welche Alterskennzeichen gibt es?" and "Wer ist und was macht die USK?", usk.de; read on 3 November 2026. The English summaries are ours.

### Who the USK is

The USK, Unterhaltungssoftware Selbstkontrolle, describes itself as "eine freiwillige Einrichtung der Games-Branche", a voluntary body of the games industry, responsible for age testing of digital games in Germany. For online games and apps it also issues labels within the international IARC system.

Its own statistics for 2025 report labels for 1,675 titles in the classic procedure and around 1.3 million games online.

### What changed recently

Under paragraph 14 (2a), labels also carry symbols that give the main reasons for the age rating. The USK's 2023 summary headline mentions higher age labels for in-game purchases and loot boxes.

For parents the useful point is that the label is about content and risks, not about whether a game is good for learning. The USK says so plainly about its lowest label.

A game your child makes in class has no USK label, because nobody sells it. That puts the responsibility on us. In our courses, game themes stay child-friendly: collecting, racing, puzzles, mazes, building and friendly rivals, never violence. When a child asks to remake a game they play that carries a higher label, the mentor helps them build its mechanics, such as a timer, a level or a shop, inside a theme suitable for their age.

## How long does it take to roll a six and leave home?

In Mensch ärgere Dich nicht, the classic German board game, your pieces wait at home until you roll a six. Many families play that you get three tries while all your pieces are still at home. How much do those three tries really help? Our ten to twelve year olds answer with a program, and then check it with maths.

### The rule

Each turn you roll one dice. With all your pieces at home you may roll up to three times; the moment a six appears, a piece comes out and your wait is over. The question is how many turns that takes on average, with one try a turn and with three.

Children guess first. The usual answers are "six turns with one try, two with three", which is nearly right, and the program shows by how much.

### The method

A function plays the waiting part of the game once, rolling random numbers from 1 to 6 until a six comes up, and returns the number of turns. Then the program plays it 10,000 times and takes the average.

We fix the random seed so the class gets the same numbers every run. Without it, every child's answer is slightly different, which is itself a lesson.

**Version 1: play the waiting game 10,000 times** (Python)
```python
import random

random.seed(2026)

def turns_to_leave_home(tries_per_turn):
    turns = 0
    while True:
        turns += 1
        for _ in range(tries_per_turn):
            if random.randint(1, 6) == 6:
                return turns

games = 10000
for tries, label in [(1, "One try a turn:"), (3, "Three tries a turn:")]:
    total = 0
    for _ in range(games):
        total += turns_to_leave_home(tries)
    print(label, round(total / games, 2), "turns on average")
```
It prints: One try a turn: 6.12 turns on average and Three tries a turn: 2.36 turns on average.

Now the maths. On one roll the chance of missing a six is five in six. Missing three times in a row has a chance of five sixths, times five sixths, times five sixths, about 0.5787, so the chance of getting out in a turn with three tries is about 0.4213. When something happens with chance p each turn, it takes on average 1 divided by p turns. Version 2 does that arithmetic.

**Version 2: the exact answer** (Python)
```python
miss = 5 / 6
for tries in [1, 3]:
    six_this_turn = 1 - miss ** tries
    print(tries, "tries:", round(six_this_turn, 4), "chance per turn,", round(1 / six_this_turn, 2), "turns expected")
```
It prints: 1 tries: 0.1667 chance per turn, 6.0 turns expected and 3 tries: 0.4213 chance per turn, 2.37 turns expected.

**Simulation against exact maths (our calculations)**

| Rule | 10,000 simulated games | Exact expected value |
|---|---|---|
| One try a turn | 6.12 turns | 6.00 turns |
| Three tries a turn | 2.36 turns | 2.37 turns |

All figures are ours, from Python 3.14 with random seed 2026 for the simulation. The board-game rule with three tries is a common family rule, used here as the example.

The simulation lands within a whisker of the maths, which tells the children two things: their program is probably right, and 10,000 games is enough to trust an average. Then Version 3 asks a question the average hides. Averages are calm; single games are not.

**Version 3: the unluckiest player** (Python)
```python
random.seed(7)
waits = [turns_to_leave_home(3) for _ in range(games)]
print("Out in the first turn:", waits.count(1))
print("Longest wait:", max(waits), "turns")
```
After Version 1, it prints: Out in the first turn: 4285 and Longest wait: 18 turns.

In 10,000 games with three tries, 4,285 players got out in their very first turn, close to the 4,213 the maths predicts. And one unlucky player waited 18 turns. Every family has met that player. Now the children know that nothing was wrong with the dice: across thousands of games, very long waits are simply bound to happen to someone.

1. **Ages 7 to 8, a real dice.** Roll until you get a six, twenty times, and tally how many rolls each one took. What was your longest wait?
2. **Ages 8 to 10, Scratch.** Make a dice sprite that picks a random number from 1 to 6 and counts the rolls until a six. Run it ten times and write down the answers.
3. **Ages 10 to 12, Python.** Run Version 1, then write Version 2 before reading ours. Do the two agree?
4. **Going further.** Invent a fairer rule, for example "a five or a six gets you out". Change one line and predict the new average before running it.

## Does making games help with Informatik at school?

Yes, more than parents often expect. A game is a program with loops, conditions, variables and events, which are the topics school Informatik starts with.

School Informatik arrives at different ages across Germany. The Informatik-Monitor 2026/27 shows one weekly Informatik lesson in grades 5 to 10 at a Bavarian Mittelschule, Informatik und Medienbildung in grades 5 to 10 at every school type in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, and two hours a week in grades 7 and 8 at a Saarland Gemeinschaftsschule. A child who has built a few games arrives with the ideas already in their hands. Our [Informatik Companion](/courses/informatik-companion-course-klasse-5-10) course follows the school subject itself, in English, for children who want help alongside school.

Games also teach the habit that school tasks rarely leave room for: testing your own work and changing it. The dice project above has no single right answer to copy, only a program that either matches the maths or does not. That is the same thinking the free Informatik-Biber rewards, a puzzle contest for grades 3 to 13 that needs no prior knowledge.
