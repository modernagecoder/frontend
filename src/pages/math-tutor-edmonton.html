There is. Modern Age Coders teaches math to Edmonton students over live video, covering everything between Grade 1 and Math 30-1 or 30-2; a teacher works with one student, or with fifteen to twenty students placed at the same level. This page explains those steps from Alberta’s own documents, then teaches a modelling lesson on the City’s own tree inventory, works a problem from it, and covers contests and prices.

Every public school in Edmonton follows the provincial programs of study. The facts below come from Alberta Education and Childcare’s 2026 to 2027 bulletins and schedules; each row links to our detailed page.

Two details change how an Edmonton family should prepare. The Grade 6 bulletin says the use of a calculator is not permitted in Part A or Part B, so fluency with fractions, decimals and ratio is not optional. And for 2026 to 2027 only, the Grade 9 bulletin says classes in schools piloting the new Mathematics 9 curriculum will not be required to write the Grade 9 test; other classes will. Ask the school which applies. Our Grade 6 PAT page and Grade 9 PAT page have practice sets.

No coaching to the test

Alberta’s Grade 9 parent guide says Alberta Education and Childcare discourages test rehearsal and coaching. We agree: our lessons teach the mathematics the test draws on, and the test result follows from that.

A curriculum in transition

Alberta implemented new K to 6 math in 2022/2023 and plans mandatory new 7 to 9 math from September 2027. A child in Grade 5 now will meet the new Grade 7 to 9 course. Our guide to the new curriculum covers it.

Then -1 or -2

The Math 30-2 bulletin describes it as preparing students for post-secondary programs that do not require the study of calculus. Math 30-1 serves those that do. Our Math 30-1 or 30-2 page sets them side by side.

In Grade 12 both Math 30-1 and Math 30-2 end in a diploma exam that Alberta now weights at 30 percent of the final mark, with 70 percent from course work. For the senior years see our Math 30-1 tutor page. Lower down, the habit that matters most is the one both achievement tests reward: working without a calculator until the numbers make sense.

Alongside school, never instead

Our lessons follow the course an Edmonton student takes at school. We do not grant Alberta credits, and achievement test results, course marks and diploma results all come through the school and the province. We are not connected with Alberta Education and Childcare, the City of Edmonton or any Edmonton school board.

Edmonton data lesson

How fast does an Edmonton bur oak grow? A line, a curve and a warning

The City of Edmonton keeps an inventory of the boulevard and open space trees it owns, with a planting date and a trunk diameter for each. Students turn one species into a growth model, fit a straight line and an exponential curve, and discover that the curve that fits best is the one that fails first. The averages, fits and projections below are ours.

The dataset is called Trees, published by the City of Edmonton, which describes it as a map of boulevard and open space trees owned and maintained by the City, adding that not all City-owned trees are captured on this map. When we queried it on 23 October 2026 it held 482,603 trees. Rather than download every record, we asked the City’s data service for the average diameter at breast height of bur oaks, grouped by planting year.

Half the inventory shares one planting date

Before any modelling, a check: 258,504 trees, or 53.6 percent of the file, carry the planting date 1 June 1990, against 15,767 for the next most common year, 2014. Among bur oaks, 1,992 trees share that date. A single day cannot account for half a city’s trees, so we treat the date as a stand-in for “unknown” and leave 1990 out. The file does not say what the date means; that reading is ours.

We kept planting years 2004 to 2025 with at least 50 oaks each: 22 points covering 15,848 trees. A least-squares line gives d = 0.5325t + 3.337, about half a centimetre of trunk a year, with r = 0.950 and R squared 0.903. Fitting a straight line to the logarithm of the diameter gives the exponential model d = 4.697(1.0567)t, growth of about 5.67 percent a year, and its R squared is higher, 0.959. Both models come from this short program:import json, math, statistics with open("by_year_OakBur.json", encoding="utf-8") as f: years = [(int(r["yr"]), float(r["d"]), int(r["n"])) for r in json.load(f)] keep = [(2026 - y, d) for y, d, n in years if 2004 <= y <= 2025 and n >= 50] age = [a for a, _ in keep] size = [d for _, d in keep] line = statistics.linear_regression(age, size) curve = statistics.linear_regression(age, [math.log(d) for d in size]) print("linear: d =", round(line.slope, 4), "t +", round(line.intercept, 3)) print("exponential: d =", round(math.exp(curve.intercept), 3), "x", round(math.exp(curve.slope), 4), "^ t")

Inside the data

From 1 to 22 years the two models stay within about a centimetre of each other, and the curve follows the young trees slightly better, which is why its R squared is higher. On the evidence of the fit alone, a student would choose the exponential.

Outside the data

At 100 years the exponential predicts a trunk more than eleven metres across. A model that grows by the same percentage every year cannot describe a living tree for long, however well it fits the first two decades. The linear model’s 56.6 cm is at least believable.

What age really means

We took age as 2026 minus the planting year, but the file does not say when each diameter was measured. If a tree was last measured years ago, its true age at measurement is smaller. Saying so is part of the answer, not an excuse.

A worked problem, ours

Worked problem: when does an oak reach 30 centimetres?

Our own problem for Math 20-1 and Math 30-1 students. It asks the same question of both oak models and shows how a logarithm settles the exponential one.

Question. Using the linear model d = 0.5325t + 3.337 and the exponential model d = 4.697(1.0567)t, with d in centimetres and t in years: (a) Check both against the age-12 average of 8.4 cm. (b) When does each predict a 30 cm trunk? (c) What is the exponential model’s doubling time, and why should that worry us?

Step 1, check one point. At t = 12 the line gives 0.5325 times 12 plus 3.337, about 9.73 cm, and the curve gives 4.697 times 1.0567 to the power 12, about 9.1 cm. The data says 8.4 cm. Both are a little high here, and the curve is closer, which matches its larger R squared.

Step 2, solve the line. 30 = 0.5325t + 3.337 gives t = (30 − 3.337) / 0.5325, about 50.1 years. That is a Grade 9 skill: undo the addition, then the multiplication.

Step 3, solve the curve with logarithms. 30 = 4.697(1.0567)t means 1.0567t = 30 / 4.697. Taking logarithms of both sides, t = log(30 / 4.697) / log 1.0567, about 33.6 years. This is the Math 30-1 move: a logarithm brings the unknown down from the exponent.

Step 4, find the doubling time. Solving 1.0567t = 2 gives t = log 2 / log 1.0567, about 12.6 years. A model that doubles a trunk every 12.6 years forever is what produced the 1,167 cm prediction at 100 years. The doubling time is the quickest way to see that an exponential model has a limited life.

Try it yourself. (1) Find the age at which the two models give the same diameter, by graphing both. (2) Rewrite the exponential model as d = 4.697ekt and find k. (3) In two sentences, say which model you would give a city forester planning 40 years ahead, and why. We go through your answers in the free lesson.

Math and AI

Should an Edmonton student let an AI build the model?

Only after they could build it themselves. Otherwise they cannot tell a good model from a bad one.

Ask an AI tool for a model of oak growth and it may pick the exponential because its R squared is higher, then predict an eleven-metre trunk without blinking. It is also unlikely to notice that half the inventory shares one planting date. A student who has built both models knows the question needs judgment, which no amount of computing power supplies.

Our teachers have Edmonton students predict, calculate and then defend a result in words. AI tools come later, and only for students of an age our policy allows, as a way of checking work already done by hand. Work submitted for marks at school stays the student’s alone.

The Grade 6 and Grade 9 tests both ban calculators for a reason: number sense is what tells you when an answer, from a person or a machine, is wrong. The longer argument is in why understanding still beats copying when AI is everywhere.
