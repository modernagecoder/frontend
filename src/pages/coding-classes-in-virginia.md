# Coding Classes in Virginia

> Virginia was the first state in the country to require computer science at every K-12 level. From Kindergarten to Grade 8 it is delivered inside other subjects, with no lesson of its own and no test to confirm it happened. Modern Age Coders teaches it as an actual subject, live, for ages 6 to 67, in batches of five to eight or privately, at USD 100 and USD 150 a month for eight classes. The opening class is free.

## What Virginia requires, and how

In **November 2017** Virginia became the first state to require computer science instruction at all K-12 levels, adopting the **Computer Science Standards of Learning**, taught from the 2019 to 2020 academic year.

| Band | How computer science is delivered | What checks it happened |
|---|---|---|
| Kindergarten to Grade 8 | Addressed within existing content areas, including language arts and history | Nothing specific |
| Middle school | **Stand-alone electives** | Course completion, if chosen |
| High school | **Stand-alone electives** | Course completion, and an exam where one exists |

Compare the other states we teach. New Jersey obliges every high school to *offer* a course. Illinois legislates an entitlement to be offered one. Texas lets computer science *substitute* for a graduation requirement, which forces schools to staff it. Each creates a thing that visibly exists or visibly does not.

Integration creates neither. No period on a timetable, no teacher whose job it is, no end-of-course test whose absence would be noticed. A fifth-grade teacher already carrying reading, writing, mathematics, science and history is asked to deliver computer science standards inside those subjects, usually without having studied the material.

**We are not saying it never works.** Where a school has a genuinely interested teacher, integration produces some of the best elementary computer science anywhere, because the ideas arrive attached to real content. The problem is variance, not ceiling.

**How to check your own school.** Ask something specific, not general. Not whether the school teaches computer science, which always gets a yes, but which lesson in the last month contained it, what the child produced, and who planned it. A school doing it well answers in one breath.

## Standard credits, verified credits, and the 5 C's

A standard credit is earned by passing the course. A **verified credit** needs the course *and* a pass in the associated end-of-course SOL test, or another Board-approved assessment.

| Diploma | Standard credits | Verified credits |
|---|---|---|
| Standard Diploma | At least 22 | 5 |
| **Advanced Studies Diploma** | At least 26 | 5 |

The verified credits bite hardest, because they cannot be recovered by effort in class alone. Mathematics is where they are most often lost, which is the unglamorous reason our mathematics track runs beside the coding rather than as a separate upsell.

Above the credit counting sits the **Profile of a Virginia Graduate**, requiring students to demonstrate foundational skills in Virginia's 5 C's: **critical thinking, creative thinking, collaboration, communication, and citizenship**. Every one is easier to demonstrate with something built than something revised.

## The Virginia student data project

Virginia's student project is built on a mistake that has justified more useless spending, across more fields, than any other in statistics. It runs on the commonwealth's own published school data.

The Virginia Department of Education publishes SOL pass rates for every school, division and subject, with three years available together through the **School Quality Profiles**. You need a before and an after, and there it is.

The question sounds like real policy work: suppose a support programme were targeted at the lowest-performing schools. How would you tell whether it worked?

Almost everybody answers the same way: compare those schools before and after. So the student does exactly that, on real data, with **no intervention at all**. They take the bottom tenth in year one and look at the same schools in year two.

They go up.

**The trap.** Any single year's score is partly durable quality and partly luck: which children were in that cohort, who was ill, how a small school swings on a handful of pupils. Select the lowest scores and you have selected, among other things, the worst luck. Luck does not repeat, so the group rises next year unaided. Select the top tenth and they fall. This is **regression to the mean**, and it means a programme aimed at the worst performers will show improvement even if the programme is an empty room.

The follow-up is the valuable part: given the naive comparison is worthless, what would settle it? Students arrive at the answer the field arrived at. You need a comparison group of similar schools that did not get the programme, and ideally assignment should not have been made on the basis of the very score you are about to measure. That is where a fourteen year old meets the reason controlled trials exist, as the way out of a problem they personally just fell into.

It transfers to medicine, sports form, a company's worst sales region, and every headline about a school turnaround.

**Said explicitly to students:** this is about statistics, not about judging schools or the people in them. The whole point is that the movement is not telling you about quality, which is exactly why treating it as if it were is so damaging.

Students pull the current published files themselves.
