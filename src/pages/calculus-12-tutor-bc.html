The BC curriculum

What Calculus 12 covers, in the curriculum’s words

The BC curriculum page for Calculus 12 lists its content in seven groups. Read top to bottom, it is a complete first course in single-variable calculus: functions, limits, derivatives, their uses, integrals, their uses, and a first look at differential equations.

The four big ideas, in one paragraph each

The first big idea says the concept of a limit “is foundational to calculus.” Everything else in the course is defined through it. The second says “Differential calculus develops the concept of” instantaneous rate of change, built up from average rate of change. The third says “Integral calculus develops the concept of determining a product involving a” continuously changing quantity over an interval.

That third idea has a lovely elaboration: area as “height x width” when the height keeps changing, volume as “area x length” when the cross-section keeps changing, and work as “force x distance” when the force keeps changing. Then: “Finding these products requires finding an infinite sum.” The fourth idea says “Derivatives and integrals are” inversely related, which is the fundamental theorem.

The inquiry questions are good revision questions

The page offers sample questions for teachers. Several make excellent oral revision for students: “How can we use historical examples (e.g., Achilles and the tortoise) to describe a limit?”, “How can a rate of change be instantaneous?” and “What is the value of using rectangles to approximate the area under a curve?”

Two more go straight to the heart of the integration unit: “Why is the fundamental theorem of calculus so fundamental?” and “What is the difference between an antiderivative and an integral?” A student who can answer the last one aloud, in two sentences, understands the unit. We ask it in nearly every integration lesson.

Estimate before you calculate

The estimation competency is the one most students skip and most teachers reward. Before finding the area under a curve, sketch it and guess. Before computing a limit, try x values close to the target in a table. A guess that is far from the computed answer is the earliest warning that something went wrong.

Both worked problems below begin with an estimate. In the first, a sign change tells us where the root must be before any calculus happens. In the second, rectangles give an approximate area that the exact answer then confirms.

Slips and misunderstandings are different problems

The curriculum’s note that mistakes range from calculation errors to misconceptions is practical advice. A dropped negative sign in the chain rule is a slip: the student knows the rule and needs a checking habit. Writing the derivative of sin(3x) as cos(3x) is a misconception: the student does not yet see the inner function and needs the idea rebuilt.

We keep a two-column error log with each student: slips on one side, misunderstandings on the other. Slips shrink with routine. Misunderstandings need a lesson, and the log shows exactly which lesson.

Where students struggle

Where Calculus 12 students usually get stuck

From our teaching, the same handful of places trip up most students, and very few of them are about calculus itself. Most are algebra and function skills from earlier grades showing their gaps under pressure. This table is ours.

The algebra underneath

A Calculus 12 student who struggles is rarely struggling with the idea of a derivative. They are struggling to factor a cubic, rewrite a root as a power, or simplify a fraction with fractions inside it, all while also learning something new. The calculus is the visible part; the algebra is the weight underneath.

So our first class with a new student includes ten minutes of pre-calculus checks: exponent laws, factoring, logarithms, the unit circle. Whatever breaks becomes the first thing we fix, because it will otherwise break again in every unit.

Taking it alongside Pre-calculus 12

If your teen’s timetable places Calculus 12 in the same year as Pre-calculus 12, a decision each school makes for itself, expect the two to meet early. The curriculum lists Pre-calculus 12 parent functions in its content, so the two courses overlap in their opening weeks.

If your teen is doing both at once, the order of units matters. We look at both teachers’ outlines together and teach the function topic one week before the calculus topic that needs it, whenever the timetable allows.

Worked problem 1, ours

A root found twice: the intermediate value theorem, then Newton’s method

The curriculum lists both the intermediate value theorem and Newton’s method. This problem, ours, uses them as a pair: the first proves a root exists and traps it, the second finds it to ten decimal places in three steps.

The problem. Show that the equation x3 + 2x − 7 = 0 has a solution between 1 and 2. Narrow it to an interval of width 0.1, then use Newton’s method from x0 = 1.5 to find it to ten decimal places.

  1. Check the theorem’s condition. f(x) = x3 + 2x − 7 is a polynomial, so it is continuous everywhere. The intermediate value theorem needs exactly that: a continuous function on a closed interval.
  2. Find a sign change. f(1) = 1 + 2 − 7 = −4 and f(2) = 8 + 4 − 7 = 5. One is negative and one is positive, so by the theorem f takes the value 0 somewhere between 1 and 2.
  3. Narrow it. f(1.5) = 3.375 + 3 − 7 = −0.625 and f(1.6) = 4.096 + 3.2 − 7 = 0.296. The sign changes again, so the root lies between 1.5 and 1.6. That is our estimate, before any derivative.
  4. Set up Newton’s method. f′(x) = 3x2 + 2. Each step follows the tangent line down to the x-axis: xnew = x − f(x) / f′(x).
  5. Do the first step by hand. At x = 1.5, f = −0.625 and f′ = 3(2.25) + 2 = 8.75. So x1 = 1.5 + 0.625 / 8.75 = 1.5 + 0.0714... = 1.5714..., which is 11/7, inside the interval as expected.
  6. Let the program do the rest, and check it. Each step roughly doubles the number of correct digits. The code below prints four steps.

What the steps show

Look at f(x) in the output: about 0.02, then 0.00003, then 0.00000000004. The error does not just shrink, it collapses, because near a simple root each tangent line is an almost perfect copy of the curve. Students who watch this happen once remember why the method is worth learning.

Steps 3 and 4 print the same ten digits. The method has converged as far as those ten places can show, and the last f(x) of zero is the computer reaching the limit of its own precision.

When Newton’s method goes wrong

The formula divides by f′(x). Start where the tangent is nearly flat and the next guess flies far away; start exactly where f′(x) = 0 and the method cannot take a step at all. Here f′(x) = 3x2 + 2 is never smaller than 2, so this function is well behaved, which is why we chose it.

This is why the intermediate value theorem comes first in a careful solution. A trapped root and a sensible starting value inside the trap make Newton’s method reliable. Without them, a test question can send a student into a calculation that never settles.

Worked problem 2, ours

An area by rectangles, by trapezoids, and exactly

The curriculum names the “Riemann sum, rectangle approximation method, trapezoidal method” and the fundamental theorem, and lists average value among the applications. This problem of ours uses all of them on one curve, so the approximations can be judged against the exact answer.

The problem. Let f(x) = 4x − x2 on the interval from 0 to 3. (a) Estimate the area under the curve with six strips of width 0.5, using left endpoints, right endpoints, midpoints and trapezoids. (b) Find the exact area. (c) Find the average value of f on the interval and the x value where f equals it.

  1. Sketch and guess. The curve rises from 0 at x = 0 to a peak of 4 at x = 2, then falls to 3 at x = 3. A rectangle 3 wide and about 3 high suggests an area near 9.
  2. Compute the four approximations. With width 0.5 the strips start at 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 and 2.5. The program below adds the six heights for each method and multiplies by the width.
  3. Find the exact area with the fundamental theorem. An antiderivative of 4x − x2 is 2x2 − x3/3. Evaluate from 0 to 3: (18 − 9) − 0 = 9.
  4. Find the average value. Average value is the integral divided by the width: 9 / 3 = 3. Solve 4x − x2 = 3: x2 − 4x + 3 = 0, so (x − 1)(x − 3) = 0 and x = 1 or x = 3. Inside the interval, the function equals its average at x = 1, and again at the right endpoint.
Six strips under 4x − x² from 0 to 3, against the exact area of 9 (ours, from the run above)

Left rectangles8.1250−0.8750The curve mostly rises, so left heights are mostly too low
Right rectangles9.6250+0.6250Right heights are mostly too high while the curve rises
Midpoint rectangles9.0625+0.0625The curve bends down, so a midpoint height slightly overshoots

The pattern worth noticing

The midpoint error is +0.0625 and the trapezoid error is −0.1250: half the size and the opposite sign. That is not a coincidence for a curve like this one, which bends the same way everywhere. A student who spots it can defend choosing the midpoint method with evidence, which is exactly the explain-and-justify competency at work.

The trapezoid estimate is also the average of the left and right estimates, (8.125 + 9.625) / 2. Seeing that in the numbers is a good way to remember why the trapezoidal method works.

Why the exact answer still matters

Six strips got the midpoint method within 0.07, but only the fundamental theorem says the true value is exactly 9. That is the answer to the curriculum’s question about why the theorem is so fundamental: it turns an infinite sum, which we can only approximate, into a subtraction of two antiderivative values.

The average-value step is the third big idea in miniature. A rectangle of height 3 and width 3 has the same area as the curved region, and the curve reaches that height at x = 1.

Practice, ours

Twelve Calculus 12 practice questions

We wrote all twelve of these ourselves; none comes from a BC school, a district or an exam paper. They run through the content groups in order, limits first and differential equations last. Use paper and pencil before checking the answers further down.

  1. Limit algebraically. Find the limit of (x2 − 9) / (x − 3) as x approaches 3.
  2. One-sided limits. For f(x) = |x − 2| / (x − 2), find the left and right limits at x = 2. Does the two-sided limit exist?
  3. End behaviour. Find the limit of (3x2 − x) / (5x2 + 4) as x grows without bound.
  4. Definition of the derivative. Use the limit definition to find the derivative of f(x) = x2 + 3x.
  5. Rules. Differentiate (a) x2 sin(3x) and (b) ln(x) / x.
  6. Implicit differentiation. For the curve x2 + xy + y2 = 7, find dy/dx at the point (1, 2).
  7. Related rates. A spherical snowball melts so that its volume decreases at 10 cm3 per minute. How fast is its radius decreasing when the radius is 5 cm?
  8. Mean value theorem. For f(x) = x3 − 3x on the interval from 0 to 2, check the conditions and find every c that the theorem promises.
  9. Substitution. Evaluate the integral of 2x(x2 + 1)4 from x = 0 to x = 1.
  10. By parts. Find the integral of x ex, then evaluate it from 0 to 1.
  11. Volume of a solid. The region under y = √x from x = 0 to x = 4 is rotated about the x-axis. Find the volume.
  12. Initial value problem. Solve dy/dx = 2y with y(0) = 3. Then describe the slope field: where are the slopes positive, negative and zero?

Answers, ours

Answers, with the reasoning

Every answer was checked with SymPy 1.14, a computer algebra system. The working is what earns marks, so each answer shows the step that matters.

  1. 6. Substituting gives 0 over 0, the signal to simplify. Factor: (x − 3)(x + 3) / (x − 3) = x + 3 for x ≠ 3, so the limit is 3 + 3 = 6.
  2. Left limit −1, right limit 1; no two-sided limit. For x just below 2, the top is the positive version of a negative bottom, so the ratio is −1. Just above 2 it is 1. The one-sided limits disagree.
  3. 3/5. Divide top and bottom by x2: (3 − 1/x) / (5 + 4/x2). As x grows, 1/x and 4/x2 go to 0, leaving 3/5. The leading coefficients decide end behaviour when the degrees match.
  4. 2x + 3. [(x + h)2 + 3(x + h) − x2 − 3x] / h = (2xh + h2 + 3h) / h = 2x + h + 3, which approaches 2x + 3 as h approaches 0.
  5. (a) 2x sin(3x) + 3x2 cos(3x). (b) (1 − ln x) / x2. Part (a) is the product rule, with the chain rule giving the 3 inside the cosine term. Part (b) is the quotient rule: (x · 1/x − ln x · 1) / x2.
  6. −4/5. Differentiate every term: 2x + (y + x dy/dx) + 2y dy/dx = 0. Collect: dy/dx (x + 2y) = −(2x + y), so dy/dx = −(2x + y) / (x + 2y). At (1, 2): −4/5. The xy term needs the product rule, the most common place to lose marks.
  7. About 0.0318 cm per minute, exactly 1/(10π). V = (4/3)πr3, so dV/dt = 4πr2 dr/dt. Only now substitute: −10 = 4π(25) dr/dt, so dr/dt = −1/(10π). The negative sign says the radius is shrinking.
  8. c = 2/√3, about 1.155. A polynomial is continuous and differentiable everywhere, so the conditions hold. The average rate of change is (f(2) − f(0)) / 2 = (2 − 0) / 2 = 1. Solve 3c2 − 3 = 1: c2 = 4/3. Only the positive root lies inside the interval.
  9. 31/5. Let u = x2 + 1, so du = 2x dx, and the limits change from 0 and 1 to 1 and 2. The integral of u4 from 1 to 2 is (32 − 1)/5 = 31/5.
  10. (x − 1)ex + C, and 1 from 0 to 1. Take u = x (it gets simpler) and dv = ex dx. Then the integral is x ex − ∫ ex dx = (x − 1)ex + C. From 0 to 1: 0 − (−1) = 1.
  11. 8π. Each disc has radius √x, so area πx. The volume is π times the integral of x from 0 to 4, which is π · 16/2 = 8π, about 25.1 cubic units.
  12. y = 3e2x. The rate is proportional to y, so the solution is exponential: y = Ce2x, and y(0) = 3 gives C = 3. In the slope field, slopes are positive above the x-axis, negative below it and zero along it, and they steepen the further a point is from the axis.

Question 12 is the kind a calculator cannot do for you: drawing a slope field by hand, row by row, is the fastest way to understand what a differential equation says. For more graph-reading practice before Grade 12, our precalculus tutoring page covers the functions that Calculus 12 builds on.

Study plan, ours

A study plan for Calculus 12 tests and the final

How Calculus 12 is assessed is set by the school and the teacher, so start by finding out. This plan is ours and assumes unit tests plus a final assessment; adjust it once you know the real format.

  1. Get the format in writing. Ask how many unit tests there are, whether there is a final exam, whether calculators are allowed, and how much each piece counts.
  2. Do the pre-calculus check first. Exponent laws, factoring, logarithm rules, the unit circle and inverse trigonometric values. Ten minutes each, and fix whatever breaks before anything else.
  3. Learn each rule with its reason. For the product, quotient and chain rules, keep one example per rule that you can differentiate both ways (by rule and by expanding) to check.
  4. Practise limits three ways. For each limit, try a table of values, a graph and algebra. The curriculum lists all three, and a test may ask for any of them.
  5. Write the conditions every time. Before using the intermediate value theorem or the mean value theorem, write its conditions and check them. Teachers mark this.
  6. For related rates and optimization, draw first. Picture, variables with units, the relationship, differentiate, then substitute. In that order, every time.
  7. Pair each integral with a sketch. A sketch with a guessed area catches sign errors and wrong limits before they cost marks.
  8. Keep the two-column error log. Slips on one side, misunderstandings on the other. Review it before every test.
  9. Do one mixed set each week. Twelve questions from different units, like our set above, because a final mixes topics and a unit test does not.
  10. Sit one timed practice under real conditions a week before the final, then spend the last days on the error log, not on new material.

After Grade 12

Is Calculus 12 required for university?

Families often ask whether Calculus 12 is needed for a science or engineering degree. UBC’s own requirements tool gives a clear answer for British Columbia applicants, and it is not what many people expect.

What that means

In the program list the tool returned for BC applicants, Pre-Calculus 12 is the math requirement for Science and for Engineering, and Calculus 12 appears only once, as recommended for Food and Resource Economics. So Calculus 12 is, on UBC’s lists, preparation rather than a gate.

Preparation still counts. First-year science and engineering students start university calculus in their first term, and those who have met limits, derivatives and integrals once already spend that term deepening ideas instead of meeting them. Requirements change between years and between universities, so check each program’s own page in the year your teen applies.

Who grants the credit

Only a school can award Calculus 12: the student’s own secondary school, a district summer program or an online learning school that teaches the course and marks it. How families use us

Most students join when the course gets ahead of them: limits made sense, then the chain rule did not; related rates arrived and the diagrams stopped helping. We start from the latest test or assignment, find the first idea that broke, and rebuild from there.

For parents

Calculus 12: frequently asked questions

What does Calculus 12 cover in BC?

The BC curriculum lists functions, limits and continuity, the definition of the derivative and the differentiation rules, applications such as concavity, the mean value theorem, Newton's method, related rates and optimization, integration by Riemann sums, the trapezoidal method, substitution and by parts, areas, volumes and average value, and differential equations with slope fields.

Do you grant Calculus 12 credit?

No. Calculus 12 credit comes only from a school that teaches and marks the course; we tutor beside it and do not grant provincial credits. That school might be the student's own secondary school, a district summer program or an online learning school.

Do I need Pre-calculus 12 before Calculus 12?

Usually, yes: the first content group of Calculus 12 starts from the parent functions met in Pre-calculus 12, so the course builds on it. Prerequisites and timetables are set by each school, so ask yours how it schedules the two; if they overlap, we can teach the function topic just before the calculus topic that needs it.

Is Calculus 12 required for UBC Science or Engineering?

On 22 October 2026, UBC's requirements tool for British Columbia applicants listed Pre-Calculus 12 as the math requirement for Science and for Applied Science (Engineering), with no Calculus 12. It listed Calculus 12 as recommended for Food and Resource Economics. Check UBC's pages in the year you apply.

Is Calculus 12 hard?

It is demanding, mostly because it uses every algebra and function skill from earlier grades at once. Students who struggle are usually stuck on factoring, exponents, logarithms or trigonometry rather than on calculus ideas, and those gaps can be fixed. Nobody can honestly promise a mark in advance.

What is the difference between Calculus 12 and AP Calculus?

Calculus 12 is a BC provincial course taught and assessed by the school. AP Calculus is a separate program with its own exam, available only where a school offers it. Both cover limits, derivatives and integrals; the BC topic list is on this page. Whether a student can take one or both depends on the school.

Does Calculus 12 include integration?

Yes. The curriculum lists the definition of an integral, definite and indefinite integrals, Riemann sums, the rectangle and trapezoidal methods, the fundamental theorem of calculus, antiderivatives, substitution and integration by parts, with applications to area, volume of solids and average value of functions.

How should my teen study for the Calculus 12 final?

Confirm the format first, then check pre-calculus skills, practise limits by table, graph and algebra, learn each derivative rule with an example, draw before every related rates or optimization problem, sketch before every integral, keep an error log, and sit one timed mixed practice a week before.

What times are classes for BC families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Since March 2026 the BC clock no longer changes, so the class time you agree in Pacific time does not move in November.

How much does Calculus 12 tutoring cost?

The first class is free. After it, each month is paid on its own, for eight live classes, and you can leave at any month end. The fees section on this page shows the current figure for a batch and for one to one.
