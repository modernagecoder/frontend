Markham data lesson

Measuring 3,424 Markham trail segments with the distance formula

The City of Markham publishes its Trails layer as open data: each piece of trail is a list of coordinates in metres, stored with the City’s own LENGTH value. We computed every length ourselves and compared. The results below are our calculations.

The City describes the layer as trails within Markham used for walking and hiking. Its coordinates are in a projected system, UTM zone 17 north, where x and y are measured in metres east and north. That matters for a student: in a projected grid, the length of a short straight piece really is the square root of (change in x) squared plus (change in y) squared, the same formula taught in Grade 10. On a map in latitude and longitude it would not be.

A segment is not one straight line but a chain of short ones, so its length is the sum of the distance formula over each pair of neighbouring points. The program below does exactly that for every segment, then counts how often our answer agrees with the City’s stored value. It uses only Python’s standard library; math.dist is the distance formula written for you:import json, math def polyline_length(paths): total = 0.0 for path in paths: for a, b in zip(path, path[1:]): total += math.dist(a[:2], b[:2]) return total segments = [] for name in ("trails_page_0.json", "trails_page_2000.json"): segments += json.load(open(name))["features"] gaps = [] for seg in segments: ours = polyline_length(seg["geometry"]["paths"]) gaps.append((abs(ours - seg["attributes"]["LENGTH"]), seg["attributes"]["OBJECTID"])) print("segments:", len(gaps)) print("within 1 cm:", sum(g < 0.01 for g, _ in gaps)) print("within 1 m:", sum(g < 1 for g, _ in gaps)) print("largest gaps:", sorted(gaps, reverse=True)[:2])

Agreement is evidence

Our formula and the City’s field agree to the centimetre on 3,240 segments. That is strong evidence the stored lengths are flat, two-dimensional lengths on the same grid, not lengths along the slope of the ground.

Disagreement is a question

Two segments are off by about three metres. The file does not say why, so we do not guess. A student should report the gap, show both numbers and say which further check would settle it.

Mean against median

The mean segment, 72.4 m, is about twice the median, 36.7 m, because a few long segments stretch the average. Most pieces of a trail network are short.

A worked problem, ours

One short piece of path in Randall Park, measured by hand

Segment OBJECTID 45 is an asphalt path in Randall Park with just three points, which makes it ideal for a Grade 10 analytic geometry question. The coordinates are the City’s; the question and solution are ours.

The three vertices of segment OBJECTID 45 in the City of Markham Trails layer, in metres (UTM zone 17 north), shown to four decimals
Pointx (east)y (north)
A637,875.11724,855,890.0231
B637,869.81144,855,889.1058
C637,865.76454,855,887.3702

Part (a), A to B. The change in x is −5.3058 and the change in y is −0.9173. Squaring gives 28.1515 and 0.8414, which add to 28.9930, so AB = √28.9930 ≈ 5.3845 m.

Part (b), B to C. The changes are −4.0469 and −1.7356. Their squares are 16.3774 and 3.0123, with sum 19.3897, so BC ≈ 4.4034 m.

Part (c), the whole segment. AB + BC ≈ 9.7879 m. The City’s LENGTH field stores 9.78788496 m. The two agree to a tenth of a millimetre, which tells us the field was computed from these very coordinates in the same flat grid.

Part (d), the rounding trap. Round each coordinate to two decimals first and repeat the calculation: the answer becomes about 9.7954 m, seven millimetres too long. Rounding the inputs before subtracting large, nearly equal numbers loses accuracy; round only at the end. This is the same caution MCV4U asks for with vector components.

Try it yourself. Find the midpoint of AC and the straight-line distance from A to C. Explain why AC is shorter than AB + BC, and what that says about how curved this little path is.

Many Markham students already know the multiple-choice Waterloo contests. The Canadian Intermediate and Senior Mathematics Contests ask for more, and they come early in the school year.

The three full-solution questions are where a careful student gains the most: like part (d) above, the marks go to reasoning that is complete and honest about its steps. Our CIMC and CSMC guide covers both papers.

Through a school only

CEMC does not take entries from individual students. A Markham student enters through their school, or or, when their own school sits the contest out, through a nearby school willing to take them in.

Past papers, no account

CEMC posts the last ten years of contests free of charge. Students use them at home; our lessons add original problems written in the same spirit.

The rest of the year

CEMC lists Pascal, Cayley and Fermat for 23 and 24 February 2027, Euclid for 6 April 2027 and the Fryer, Galois and Hypatia papers for 7 April 2027. Our Waterloo contests page has the full calendar.

Not connected with CEMC

CEMC’s rule is that “all students must participate through their school”. We are not connected with CEMC or the University of Waterloo. We never reproduce their problems or solutions; our contest material is original and labelled as ours.

Math and AI

Should a Markham student let AI measure the trails?

It can run the formula. It cannot tell whether the formula was the right one for the coordinates.

An AI tool will sum distances for you in a second. Hand it latitude and longitude and it may still apply the flat formula and return nonsense with confidence. Knowing that this layer is in metres on a projected grid, and that rounding early costs seven millimetres, is mathematical judgement the student has to own.

Our teachers keep that judgement with the student: sketch first, compute one case by hand, then let code do the rest and check it against something independent. School work that is marked stays the student’s own.

Ontario math asks students to reason and communicate, not only compute; those are the parts a tool cannot do for them. The longer argument is in why real understanding outlasts the tools that compute for us.
