## What actually happens in a live online Python lesson?

Most people in Mönchengladbach who ask about online classes have tried a recorded course and stopped. The difference is a person who can see your screen.

### The first ten minutes

The lesson opens on a video call. The mentor asks what happened with last week's exercise, and the learner shares their screen with the code still on it. If something did not run, that is where the lesson starts, because a mistake you made yourself teaches more than one in a slide.

Then the mentor sets the day's problem in plain English: one sentence, one goal, for example "count how many lines in this file mention solar".

### The middle and the end

The learner types; the mentor watches. When a learner writes a loop that never stops, the mentor does not fix it for them. They ask what the loop is waiting for. In a group, the mentor moves between learners and shares the most useful mistake with everyone.

The last minutes are for a small task to finish before next time, written down in the chat so nobody has to remember it.

## How do you go from no Python to real projects?

Four stages, each one ending with something the learner has built and can explain. The times are typical, not promises; some move faster, some take longer, and both are fine.

1. **Stage 1: talking to the computer.** print, variables, input, if and else. The first program asks your name and answers back. By the end, a small quiz that keeps score.
2. **Stage 2: repeating and deciding.** for and while loops, lists, functions. Learners write a times-table tester and a guessing game, then learn to read an error message line by line instead of panicking.
3. **Stage 3: files and real data.** Opening a CSV file, reading it row by row, counting with a dictionary, sorting. This is where the Mönchengladbach solar project below sits.
4. **Stage 4: your own project.** The learner chooses a question, finds or makes the data, writes the program, and explains the answer and its limits to the mentor as if presenting it.

## The solar register: which date answers your question?

Mönchengladbach's open data portal publishes the city's part of the Marktstammdatenregister, the national register of power and gas units, under a licence that allows reuse with attribution. Our Stage 3 learners use it to learn a habit that saves professionals from embarrassing charts.

### The data

The city's copy has 18,864 rows, every one in Mönchengladbach. In our count, 12,277 are solar units (energietrager "Solare Strahlungsenergie"), 6,413 are battery storage units and 14 are wind turbines. The register's own description calls it "das zentrale amtliche Register für Strom- und Gasanlagen in Deutschland" and says operators are legally obliged to register.

Each row has two dates that look alike: inbetriebnahmedatum, the day the unit was switched on, and registrierungsdatum, the day it was entered in the register.

### The question

How fast did solar grow in Mönchengladbach? A learner who picks the first date column they see might get one answer; a learner who picks the other gets a very different one.

The learner's file, mg_energy_units.csv, is the city's export as downloaded on 3 November 2026. The first program counts solar units per year by both dates side by side.

**Version 1: count by both dates** (Python)
```python
import csv

started, registered = {}, {}
with open("mg_energy_units.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f, delimiter=";"):
        if row["energietrager"] != "Solare Strahlungsenergie":
            continue
        start = row["inbetriebnahmedatum_der_einheit"][:4]
        signed_up = row["registrierungsdatum_der_einheit"][:4]
        started[start] = started.get(start, 0) + 1
        registered[signed_up] = registered.get(signed_up, 0) + 1

for year in ("2010", "2018", "2019", "2023", "2025"):
    print(year, " switched on:", started.get(year, 0), "  registered:", registered.get(year, 0))
```
It prints: 2010 switched on: 207 registered: 0, 2018 switched on: 87 registered: 0, 2019 switched on: 138 registered: 377, 2023 switched on: 2345 registered: 2237 and 2025 switched on: 2366 registered: 2402.

By registration date, Mönchengladbach had no solar at all in 2010 or 2018. That is obviously false: 207 units were switched on in 2010 alone. The register simply did not take entries before 2019, so every older unit was entered later, in a rush. In 2020, 302 solar units were switched on, but 1,506 were registered. A chart by registration date would show a spurious solar boom in 2020; a chart by switch-on date shows the real growth.

**Version 2: check the story before you chart it** (Python)
```python
older = sum(n for year, n in started.items() if year != "" and year < "2019")
print("Solar units switched on before 2019:", older)
print("Units with no switch-on date yet:", started.get("", 0))
print("First registration year in the file:", min(registered))
running = 0
for year in sorted(y for y in started if y != ""):
    running += started[year]
    if year in ("2018", "2022", "2025"):
        print("Switched on by the end of", year, ":", running)
```
It prints: Solar units switched on before 2019: 1634, Units with no switch-on date yet: 156, First registration year in the file: 2019, Switched on by the end of 2018 : 1634, Switched on by the end of 2022 : 3242 and Switched on by the end of 2025 : 10420.

**Mönchengladbach solar units by year: switched on versus registered (our count)**

| Year | Switched on | Registered |
|---|---|---|
| 2010 | 207 | 0 |
| 2019 | 138 | 377 |
| 2020 | 302 | 1,506 |
| 2022 | 775 | 756 |
| 2023 | 2,345 | 2,237 |
| 2025 | 2,366 | 2,402 |

Data: Stadt Mönchengladbach open data portal, dataset Marktstammdatenregister (publisher Bundesnetzagentur, Marktstammdatenregister), licence Datenlizenz Deutschland Namensnennung 2.0, downloaded 3 November 2026. Counts are ours, solar units only, all operating states included. The 156 units with no switch-on date are listed as In Planung.

The answer the data supports is this: 1,634 solar units were running in Mönchengladbach by the end of 2018, 3,242 by the end of 2022 and 10,420 by the end of 2025, so more than two thirds of today's units were switched on in the last three full years. Since 2023 the two columns move together, because new units are registered when they are installed. The lesson is not about solar. It is that a column name is a promise you have to check: before you count, ask which event the date records.

1. **Warm up.** Run Version 1 and write one sentence about 2010 that is true and one that is false.
2. **Your turn.** Change the program to count battery storage units ("Speicher") instead. In which year does the first one appear by each date?
3. **Stretch.** Find units whose registration year is more than five years after their switch-on year. How many are there?
4. **Explain it.** Write three sentences for a newspaper reader on why the 2020 registration figure is not a boom.
