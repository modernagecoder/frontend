Maths for home-educated children in the UK

Home-educating parents in every UK nation must give their child a suitable, efficient full-time education, but none of the four requires them to follow a national curriculum. Maths is usually the subject where families want most help, and it is also the subject where progress is easiest to show. Modern Age Coders teaches maths live online to home-educated children from age 5 to GCSE, in a group or one to one, starting with our elementary maths course. This page sets out what each nation's guidance says, the milestones worth borrowing from the curriculum, how to keep a record that shows progress, and how GCSE Maths works for a private candidate.

Do home-educated children have to follow the national curriculum for maths?

No. In England the Department for Education's guidance for parents lists no legal requirement to teach the National Curriculum, give formal lessons, mark work or "match school-based, age-specific standards". Wales says home-educated children need not follow the Curriculum for Wales, Scotland's 1980 Act asks for "efficient education" suitable to age, ability and aptitude, and Northern Ireland's guidance says a child "is not obliged to follow the curriculum or take tests". What every nation does require is a suitable education, and England's guidance notes councils "may use minimum expectations for literacy and numeracy in assessing suitability". So maths matters, on the family's own terms. DfE figures for autumn 2025 put home education at 1.5 per cent of children of comparable ages in England. Modern Age Coders teaches home-educated children maths live online, from first number to GCSE, alongside whatever curriculum the family uses.

The law, nation by nation

What does the law ask of home-educating parents in each UK nation?

The wording differs, but the core is the same: an education suitable to the child, delivered efficiently, without a compulsory curriculum. Maths is not named in any of the four laws.

Home education and maths in the four UK nations, from each government's own guidance
NationThe dutyCurriculumWorth knowing for maths
EnglandEfficient full-time education suitable to age, ability and aptitude (Education Act 1996, section 7)Not requiredCouncils "may use minimum expectations for literacy and numeracy in assessing suitability"
WalesThe same section 7 duty, applied in WalesNot required to "follow the Curriculum for Wales or any other specified curriculum"Not required to "meet criteria for the number of learning hours"; every council has agreed an exam centre for home-educated candidates
ScotlandEfficient education suitable to age, ability and aptitude (Education (Scotland) Act 1980, section 30)No set curriculumCouncil consent is needed to withdraw a child already attending a public school (section 35)
Northern IrelandFull-time education suitable to age, ability and aptitude, normally from age four to 16"not obliged to follow the curriculum or take tests"The Education Authority may visit annually; "there are no funds directly available for parents"

What England's guidance says you need not do

Paragraph 2.11 of the Department for Education's guidance for parents lists things with no legal requirement, including to "give formal lessons", "mark work done by the child", "formally assess progress, or set development objectives" and "match school-based, age-specific standards".

It also says there is no legal definition of full-time. School pupils "normally have about five hours tuition a day for 190 days a year, spread over about 38 weeks", but home education "does not have to mirror this", and parents need not "have a timetable".

And what makes life easier

The same guidance adds, in paragraph 2.12: "it is likely to be much easier for you to show that the education provided is suitable if attention has been paid to the breadth of the curriculum and its content, and the concepts of progress and assessment in relation to your child's ability."

It even anticipates a maths-minded child: suitability "should take account of any specific aptitudes (for example if a child is very good at mathematics, it might focus more on that than some other subjects)."

All read 31 October 2026. We give no legal advice; your council or education authority is the place to ask.

Milestones worth borrowing

What maths should a home-educated child know at each age?

Nothing is compulsory, but the national curriculum for England gives three clear checkpoints in primary maths, and they are a useful yardstick even for families who follow no curriculum at all.

Three checkpoints from the national curriculum in England, with what we watch for
CheckpointThe curriculum's wordsWhat it looks like at home (ours)
End of Year 2, about age 7"pupils should know the number bonds to 20 and be precise in using and understanding place value"Says 13 and 7 make 20 without counting; knows the 4 in 47 is forty
End of Year 4, about age 9"pupils should have memorised their multiplication tables up to and including the 12 multiplication table"Answers 8 times 7 or 54 divided by 9 in a breath, in any order
End of Year 6, about age 11"pupils should be fluent in written methods for all 4 operations, including long multiplication and division, and in working with fractions, decimals and percentages"Does 47 times 36 and 936 divided by 24 on paper correctly, and knows three eighths is 0.375

The curriculum frames all of this around three aims: that pupils become fluent, "reason mathematically by following a line of enquiry, conjecturing relationships and generalisations", and solve problems, "including breaking down problems into a series of simpler steps and persevering in seeking solutions". Home education can serve the second and third aims unusually well, because there is time to follow a question wherever it goes. The first aim, fluency, is the one most often missed, because it needs short, frequent, slightly dull practice that a project-led day can quietly skip.

Ages 5 to 7 (our example)

There are 7 cups and 3 more plates than cups. How many plates? Ten. The habit to build is saying the sum out loud, 7 and 3 more, before counting anything.

Ages 8 to 11 (our example)

A bus leaves at 8:05 and every 12 minutes after. How many leave before 9:00? 8:05, 8:17, 8:29, 8:41, 8:53: five. Listing beats guessing, and the list is the working.

Ages 11 to 14 (our example)

Share 84 sweets in the ratio 3 to 4. There are 7 parts, so one part is 12, giving 36 and 48. Check that 36 plus 48 is 84 before moving on.

At ages 14 to 16 the target is usually GCSE. Our example at that level: solve 3(x minus 2) equals 2x plus 5. Expand to 3x minus 6, so x minus 6 equals 5 and x is 11. Check by substitution: 3 times 9 is 27, and 22 plus 5 is 27. That last line, the check, is worth teaching as a ritual from the first algebra lesson.

Source: National curriculum in England: mathematics programmes of study. The examples and the "at home" column are ours.

Showing progress

How can home educators show maths progress to the council?

There is no required format. A light record kept through the year makes any informal enquiry straightforward, and it helps the child too.

  1. Write down, in a few lines, what maths your child is working on this term and why: your own aims, not a school's.
  2. Keep dated samples: one piece of written working a week is plenty, including the mistakes.
  3. Note the checkpoints met, such as number bonds to 20 or the 12 times table, with the date.
  4. Record the resources and lessons used, whether books, online lessons, games or projects.
  5. Once a term, write a short paragraph on what improved and what is next.

An example entry (ours)

Week of 12 October. Fractions of amounts: found three quarters of 56 and two fifths of 35 using unit fractions first. Mistake: found one quarter but forgot to multiply by three on two questions; corrected after we drew a bar. Next: fractions of measures, such as three quarters of a kilogram.

That is four sentences and it shows content, method, a real error, a correction and a plan, which is exactly the "progress and assessment" England's guidance mentions.

What councils and authorities may ask

In England councils may make an informal enquiry. In Northern Ireland, nidirect says a family can respond by writing a report, providing samples of work, or meeting an Education Authority officer at home or elsewhere, with or without the child.

A maths record does double duty here, because numeracy is the area England's guidance names alongside literacy as one where minimum expectations may be used.

When a family uses our lessons, each child's teacher sends a short note after each block on what was covered and what is secure, which slots straight into a record like the one above. We do not replace the parent's own judgement, and we do not report to councils; the record is yours.

GCSE as a private candidate

How does a home-educated child sit GCSE Maths?

As a private candidate, through an exam centre that agrees to enter them, at the family's cost. Plan the centre first, then the board and tier.

AQA GCSE Mathematics 8300, from AQA's specification
FeatureWhat AQA says
TiersFoundation, grades 1 to 5; Higher, grades 4 to 9
Papers"Students must take three question papers at the same tier." All in the same series
Paper 1Written exam, 1 hour 30 minutes, 80 marks, non-calculator
Papers 2 and 3Calculator papers of the same length and marks
WeightingEach paper is one third of the GCSE
Private candidatesMay be "self-taught, home-schooled or have private tuition, either with a tutor or through a distance learning organisation. You must be based in the UK."

Why so many families face this

DfE's autumn 2025 figures show home education rising with age, "from 3% expected to be in year group 1 to 19% expected to be in year group 11", and "Over a third of EHE children were expected to be in either year groups 10 or 11." GCSE is therefore a live question for a large share of home-educating families.

England's guidance asks parents to think early about "whether you expect your child to sit public examinations such as GCSEs or not".

Wales has gone further

The Welsh Government says "All local authorities have agreed to accept independent candidates from home-educated families at an identified examination centre." Families in Wales should ask their council which centre that is before choosing a board.

Elsewhere, finding a centre is the family's task, and the centre often decides which board and tier are possible.

A week of maths

How much maths should a home-educated child do each week?

No law sets a number. Our suggestion is short, frequent fluency work plus one longer session that goes deep, and the curriculum's own wording on fluency points the same way.

A sample week for a 9-year-old, our suggestion and nothing more
DayWhatHow long
MondayTimes tables and number bonds, quick-fire10 minutes
TuesdayLive lesson: one new idea, such as fractions of amountsAbout an hour
WednesdayPractice from the lesson, then a puzzle20 minutes
ThursdayMaths in life: a recipe, a timetable, a shopping list20 minutes
FridayQuick-fire again, then write the week's record entry together15 minutes

Why short and often

Fluency, the first national curriculum aim, comes from "varied and frequent practice". Ten minutes daily beats an hour once a week for recall, and it leaves the long session free for understanding.

Home education is good at the long session. It is the ten minutes that slips.

Where a teacher helps most

With the new idea, especially past age 11, when ratio, negative numbers and algebra arrive and many parents last met them decades ago.

With a second adult's view. A teacher who sees the same child every week notices a stuck method early, and a group of peers working on the same idea answers a common worry about isolation.

Home education maths questions

What home-educating parents ask about maths

Do I have to follow the national curriculum for maths?

No, in any UK nation. England's guidance lists no legal requirement to teach the National Curriculum, Wales says children need not follow the Curriculum for Wales, and Northern Ireland says a child is not obliged to follow the curriculum. The education must still be suitable.

How many hours of maths a week do home-educated children need?

No law sets a figure. England's guidance says there is no legal definition of full-time and parents need not keep a timetable, and Wales says there are no criteria for learning hours. We suggest short daily fluency work plus one longer session.

What maths should my child know by age 11?

There is no legal standard for home education, but the national curriculum in England expects fluent written methods for all four operations, including long multiplication and division, and confidence with fractions, decimals and percentages by the end of Year 6. It is a useful yardstick.

Will the council check my child's maths?

In England a council may make an informal enquiry and may use minimum expectations for literacy and numeracy when judging suitability. A dated record with samples of work is the simplest way to answer.

Can a home-educated child sit GCSE Maths?

Yes, as a private candidate through an exam centre that agrees to enter them. AQA's specification says a private candidate may be home-schooled and must be based in the UK, and the family pays the entry costs.

How is GCSE Maths structured?

For AQA 8300, three papers of 1 hour 30 minutes and 80 marks each, all at the same tier, with Paper 1 non-calculator. Foundation covers grades 1 to 5 and Higher grades 4 to 9.

Is there any funding for home education maths in Northern Ireland?

nidirect says there are no funds directly available for parents who decide to educate their children at home. In England and Wales any help with exam costs is at the council's discretion.

Can my child have lessons during the school day?

Yes, as 1-on-1 lessons booked at a time that suits you. Group batches mostly run after school, because they include children who attend school; all times are in UK time.

When are lessons?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Every time is given in UK time.

Do you report to the council?

No. We send you short written notes on what was covered and what is secure, for your own records. Decisions and contact with the council stay with you.
