Maths competitions and enrichment for able children in the UK

A child who finds school maths easy has more on offer in the UK than most parents realise: national challenges sat in a school hall, olympiads marked by hand, team events, free computing contests, Saturday masterclasses and residential summer schools. Nobody hands a family the whole list, so here it is for 2026 to 2027, each entry read at the organiser's own website on 1 November 2026, with a guide to each part and a page for each competition. One thread runs through all of it: almost every door opens from inside a school.

In short

The main maths competitions for able children in the UK are run by the UK Mathematics Trust (UKMT): the Junior, Intermediate and Senior Mathematical Challenges, their Kangaroo and olympiad follow-on rounds, the British Mathematical Olympiad and two team challenges. Alongside them sit the Primary Mathematics Challenge for ages 9 to 11, the Scottish Mathematical Challenge, and in computing the free UK Bebras Challenge, the TechFirst Girls Competition (formerly CyberFirst Girls) and the British Informatics Olympiad. Enrichment comes through Ri Masterclasses, UKMT summer schools and free university maths clubs. Nearly all are entered through a school. Modern Age Coders is not connected with any organiser; we teach the problem-solving behind them live online, in a group batch of 15 to 20 or one-to-one, through our Olympiad and Competition Maths course, and the first class is free.

The thread

Who actually enters a child for these?

Almost always the school, and that changes what a parent should do first.

UKMT is plain about it: every pupil is entered through a registered UKMT centre, and "Your institution must be in the UK, a Crown Dependency or be a British International School." The Primary Mathematics Challenge goes through schools too, teachers register UK Bebras and TechFirst Girls entries, and BIO round one is sat at school and marked by a teacher before moderation.

Enrichment is narrower still. Ri Masterclasses work by nomination: "Masterclasses are free to attend, school students are nominated by their teacher." Summer school invitations go through schools, one pupil per school.

So the most useful conversation is with the head of maths or the computing teacher, not a tutor. Ask which challenges the school enters, whether it puts a team forward, and whether it nominates for masterclasses.

Families outside school have a route too, though it is a favour rather than a right. UKMT says a homeschooled child can approach a local school that is a registered centre and ask to be hosted, "however they are not obliged to do so".

Enrichment and able pupils

What enrichment is there for able maths pupils in the UK?

Enrichment means maths beyond the syllabus with no exam at the end. The UK has a mix of free university outreach, nominated programmes and things a family can simply join, and the pages below take each in turn.

The mathematics national curriculum in England does not ask schools to race able pupils ahead. It says pupils who grasp concepts rapidly "should be challenged through being offered rich and sophisticated problems before any acceleration through new content". Our page on gifted and talented maths and coding in the UK starts from that sentence and asks what stretch in maths and in programming looks like when a child is not simply moved up a year.

A maths circle is the gentlest way in: a small group meeting regularly over one rich problem. The Oxford Online Maths Club, a weekly recorded livestream, needs "no registration". Online maths circles in the UK lists the free university options and who each suits.

Masterclasses are more formal. The Royal Institution ran its first secondary series in 1981, primary in 1998, and "secondary school Computer Science Masterclasses launched in 2014". The maths masterclasses for able students page covers nomination and what to do without one.

Summer schools are the most selective. UKMT's residential week invites pupils aged 14 to 16 "selected at random from the top 1.5% of results from the Intermediate Mathematical Challenge". The online maths summer school page explains the invitations and gives a summer plan for everyone else.

For children learning at home, maths for home-educated children deals with law, planning and exams. For teenagers who want coding to count, coding for the Duke of Edinburgh Skills section maps it onto Bronze, Silver and Gold; the DofE asks for "an average of 1 hour a week" and a finish "before your 25th birthday".

Quoted from the National curriculum in England: mathematics programmes of study, UKMT Summer Schools, Ri Masterclasses, the Oxford Online Maths Club and DofE timescales, read raw between 31 October and 1 November 2026.

Maths competitions

Which maths competitions can UK pupils enter, and how do the rounds connect?

UKMT runs three stages: a multiple-choice first round in school, a Kangaroo follow-on for many high scorers, and a written olympiad for fewer. Around them sit a primary challenge, team events and Scotland's own competition.

UKMT says "over 700,000 young people take part" in its challenges, Kangaroos and olympiads each year, and the first-round papers carry the same instruction to every candidate: "Do not expect to finish the whole paper in the time allowed." On those papers "calculators and measuring instruments are forbidden". And "All UKMT Competitions are now paper-format only", so practise on paper.

For a parent seeing the whole ladder for the first time, maths olympiad training in the UK is the overview of every written round and how training changes as a pupil climbs.

The youngest competitors start with the Primary Maths Challenge, a 25-question paper for ages 9 to 11 whose last five questions offer no options at all; that page is about pacing. The multiple-choice follow-ons, Junior, Grey, Pink and Senior, are covered together on UKMT Kangaroo rounds practice, including what the wrong options are there for.

UKMT Team Maths Challenge practice covers the junior and sixth-form team events, where "a maximum of two students from the older year group" may sit in a team of four.

The Junior Mathematical Olympiad page is about a twelve-year-old's first written proof. The Cayley, Hamilton and Maclaurin page sorts out which year sits which paper.

At the top, the British Mathematical Olympiad page covers BMO1 and BMO2, which UKMT calls "the UK's hardest national mathematics competition". The Mathematical Olympiad for Girls page explains its mix of answer-only and written problems.

The Scottish Mathematical Challenge has run since 1976, from P7 to S6, and marks the explanation rather than the bare answer.

Computing programmes and competitions

What computing competitions and programmes are there, and what happened to CyberFirst?

Fewer contests than maths, most of them free, and one big change in 2026: CyberFirst is now part of TechFirst.

GOV.UK describes TechFirst as "the government's flagship tech skills programme", and its guidance pages are published under the Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport. The National Cyber Security Centre, which ran CyberFirst from 2016, says the programme "has now transitioned over to TechFirst under the leadership of DCMS", with the NCSC staying on as the cyber pathway lead.

The old bursary is now an undergraduate scholarship. CyberFirst courses and bursary in 2026 sorts out which free courses still run and what the scholarship asks for: at least 120 UCAS tariff points and a degree "in any subject to start in September 2027".

The girls' competition is now the TechFirst Girls Competition, "a free, UK wide competition for girls aged 12 to 13, delivered by IBM", building on "the CyberFirst Girls Competition, which ran from 2016 to 2025". Teachers registered teams from 5 October to 10 November 2026 and the competition runs from 11 to 20 November 2026. The CyberFirst Girls Competition page covers the rename and the logic the puzzles lean on.

UK Bebras Challenge practice is for anyone aged 6 to 19: 45 minutes of interactive tasks from 9 to 20 November 2026, run by the Raspberry Pi Foundation, where "every participant receives a certificate". At the other end, the British Informatics Olympiad is a three-hour programming exam for under-19s "resident in mainland Britain", with a final in Cambridge and a team of four sent to the International Olympiad in Informatics.

For the very youngest, KS1 coding lessons online explains what the computing curriculum expects in Years 1 and 2, the prediction and debugging a six-year-old Bebras entrant meets. The UK competitions calendar lays out every coding, maths and AI contest month by month, cipher and AI contests included.

How to choose

How do you choose the right competition or programme for your child?

Start from the school year and the school, not the competition. Seven steps.

  1. Write down your child's school year in the organiser's terms. UKMT sets eligibility by school year, and the same pupil is Year 8 in England or Wales, S2 in Scotland and Year 9 in Northern Ireland.
  2. Ask the school whether it is a registered UKMT centre and whether it enters UK Bebras. Those answers decide most of what follows.
  3. Find the first-round paper that matches the year: the Primary Mathematics Challenge or Primary Kangaroo at the top of primary school, the Junior Challenge up to Year 8, the Intermediate up to Year 11, the Senior up to Year 13. A Year 7 or 8 pupil is eligible for the Intermediate as well as the Junior.
  4. Add one computing contest. UK Bebras suits almost everyone from age 6; the TechFirst Girls Competition suits girls in Year 8; the British Informatics Olympiad suits a sixth former who already programs.
  5. Ask about the nominated programmes early: masterclass nominations come from teachers, summer school invitations reach schools in March, and team places are limited to one team per school for the Team Maths Challenge.
  6. Practise on the organisers' own free past papers, on paper, and for written rounds practise full explanations.
  7. Then open the page for that round, above or in the full list at the foot of this page.

If a child is ahead of their year

Enter the paper for the actual school year first. UKMT sets eligibility as "and below", so a younger pupil may sit an older paper, but a strong score on their own paper can open the follow-on round meant for their year.

If a child is not entered at school

Ask politely, and ask early in the autumn term, before papers are ordered.

Questions parents ask

Maths competitions and enrichment: common questions

What maths competitions are there for able children in the UK?

The largest are UKMT's Junior, Intermediate and Senior Mathematical Challenges with their Kangaroo and olympiad follow-on rounds, the British Mathematical Olympiad and two team challenges. There is also the Primary Mathematics Challenge for ages 9 to 11 and, in Scotland, the Scottish Mathematical Challenge.

How does my child enter a UKMT challenge?

Through a school that is a registered UKMT centre. The school buys the papers, runs the challenge and receives the results. Follow-on rounds are by invitation from a qualifying score or by a discretionary entry bought by the school.

Can a home-educated child enter UKMT competitions?

Yes, if a registered school agrees to host them. UKMT suggests approaching local schools and asking, but says schools are not obliged to agree, and it will not say which schools are registered.

What age is the Primary Maths Challenge for?

The organiser says it is aimed at pupils aged 9 to 11 who are in primary school. The 2026 paper runs from 9 to 20 November 2026, with 25 questions in 45 minutes and an invite-only Bonus Round in February 2027.

What is the hardest UK maths competition?

UKMT calls British Mathematical Olympiad Round 2 the UK's hardest national mathematics competition. It is four problems in three and a half hours on 20 January 2027, and UKMT invites around 100 pupils from BMO1.

Are there free computing competitions for children?

Yes. UK Bebras is free and open to ages 6 to 19, the TechFirst Girls Competition is free for girls aged 12 to 13, and the British Informatics Olympiad is free to enter for eligible pupils under 19 in mainland Britain.

What happened to CyberFirst?

It is now part of TechFirst. The NCSC says CyberFirst has transitioned to TechFirst under the leadership of DCMS, the girls' competition is now the TechFirst Girls Competition, and the former bursary has become a TechFirst undergraduate scholarship.

How do children get into Ri Masterclasses or the UKMT summer school?

Both depend on the school. Ri Masterclasses are free and pupils are nominated by a teacher. UKMT summer school invitations go, through schools, to pupils picked at random from the top 1.5% of Intermediate Challenge results, aged 14 to 16.

When are your lessons, and do you enter pupils?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. We do not enter anyone for any competition and have no link with any organiser; the school does that. Lessons are live online, the first is free, and we never promise a score or a qualification.
