What you learn

What does an app development course actually teach?

Much more than screens. A phone app is a small program that must remember things, talk to a server, survive being closed at any moment and pass a store review. Our courses teach each of those as a separate skill.

The language first

Dart, Kotlin or Swift, learned as a language before any framework: variables, functions, classes, lists and errors. Learners who skip this step end up copying widget code they cannot change.

Screens and state

Layouts that adapt to a small Android phone and a large iPhone, then state: what the app knows right now, what changes it, and which screen must redraw when it does. Most app bugs live here.

Data and the network

Saving data on the phone, calling a web service, handling a slow or failed reply, and storing records in a database. Learners write their own small server so the app has something real to talk to.

Privacy as a feature

Asking only for the data the app needs, explaining why in plain words, letting users delete their account, and knowing what Peru's data regulation says about minors and about leaks.

Testing and release

Automated tests for the parts that must not break, a build signed for release, store listings, screenshots and the review questions both stores now ask before an app goes live.

Working like a team

Git from the first month, small commits with clear messages, and code reviews with the mentor. A portfolio of three or four apps on GitHub says more to an employer than any certificate.

The order matters. We see many adults in Peru who have followed long video playlists and can assemble a screen, yet freeze when the app must do something the video did not show. Our mentors keep the learner's hands on the keyboard and ask them to predict what a line will do before running it. That habit, think first and then type, is what lets someone build an app nobody has filmed yet.

Why phones

Why learn app development in Peru?

Because in Peru the phone is the internet for most people. The national statistics office measures it every quarter.

What INEI measured in 2026

In its bulletin on household technology for the second quarter of 2026, INEI reports that 90.2% of internet users aged six and over went online through a mobile phone. In the same quarter, only 36.4% of households in the country had at least one computer.

Put those two figures side by side and the case for apps writes itself. A Peruvian business that wants to reach customers reaches them on a phone screen, often a modest Android phone, and often with a patchy connection.

What that means for a learner

Building for that reality is a skill in itself: small downloads, screens that still make sense on a narrow display, and an app that behaves well when the signal drops between two districts. We build those constraints into the projects rather than treating them as an afterthought.

One practical point for learners themselves: you write apps on a computer, not on the phone. If your home is among the many without one, tell us at the free class and the mentor will suggest the lightest setup that still works.

Our Spanish-language page on the same subject, curso de desarrollo de aplicaciones en inglés, looks at the weak-signal problem in detail and teaches a retry lesson. This English page takes a different route: the rules an app must obey before anyone in Peru can install it.

Store rules, 2026

What do Google Play and the App Store require of a new app in 2026?

Both stores now publish dated technical floors. An app that misses them is refused at upload, or quietly disappears for new users. Here are the 2026 rules in the stores' own words, summarised.

StoreSince or fromWhat the store requiresWho it affects
Google Play31 August 2026New apps and updates must target Android 16 (API level 36) or higherPhone and tablet apps
Google Play31 August 2026Existing apps must target Android 15 (API level 35) or higher to stay available to new users on newer Android versionsApps already in the store
Google Play31 August 2026Wear OS and Android Automotive OS: Android 15 (API level 35); Android TV and Android XR: Android 14 (API level 34)Watch, car, TV and XR apps
Apple App Store31 January 2026Answers to the updated age rating questions for each appEvery app

Sources: Google, Android Developers, "Target API level requirements for Google Play apps"; Apple Developer, "Upcoming Requirements". Both pages read on 9 November 2026. Dates are the stores' own; requirements change every year, so we check them again before each cohort reaches its release module.

What a "target API level" is

Every Android version has a number, its API level. When you build an app you declare which level you have tested against. Google uses that number to decide whether your app has been kept up to date with newer privacy and battery rules, and refuses uploads that point too far into the past.

For a learner this has one practical meaning: tutorials written two or three years ago often produce an app that targets an old level. Our mentors start every project on a current template, so the first app a learner builds could actually be submitted.

Why Apple asks about age

Apple redesigned its age ratings and asked developers to answer new questions for every app by the end of January 2026. A developer answers questions about the app's content and features; the store turns the answers into a rating shown to families.

This links straight to Peru's own rules on minors, below. An app that a 12 year old might use is a different product, legally and ethically, from one meant only for adults, and the developer is expected to know which one they are building.

Peru's data rules

What does Peru's data protection law mean for an app developer?

Peru updated the regulation of its personal data law, Ley 29733, with Supreme Decree DS 016-2024-JUS, published in El Peruano on Saturday 30 November 2024. Several of its articles land directly on the desk of whoever builds an app. This is a teaching summary, not legal advice.

Five parts of DS 016-2024-JUS an app developer meets, in plain English
Where in the regulationWhat it saysWhat it means when you build an app
Final provision on entry into forceIn force 120 calendar days after publicationOur arithmetic: from 30 March 2025, so every app launched in Peru today falls under it
Article VI, 3.1Covers a controller outside Peru that offers goods or services aimed at people located in PeruA developer abroad who targets Peruvian users is not outside the rules
Article 25, 25.3Digital services aimed at children under fourteen need the consent of a parent or guardianKnow your audience before you design the sign-up screen
Article 34, 34.1Serious data incidents are notified to the national authority within 48 hours at mostLogging and alerting are part of the app, not extras
Recitals, citing DL 1412The principle of privacy from the design stage for digital servicesCollect less, explain why, and build deletion in from the start

Source: Decreto Supremo N.° 016-2024-JUS, Reglamento de la Ley N.° 29733, Ley de Protección de Datos Personales, official copy on gob.pe. The 30 March 2025 date is our own calculation from the 120-day rule. We summarise for teaching; for a real product, ask a Peruvian lawyer.

In class this turns into habits rather than law lectures. A sign-up form that asks for a birthday has to do something with the answer. A login screen should not store a password in plain text on the phone. A crash report should not contain the user's phone number. Each of these is a few lines of code, and each is easier to write on the first day than to retrofit after launch.

The same regulation is why we design our own forms carefully: a learner under 14 joins our classes with a parent's consent, and adults on this page sign up for themselves.

A lesson from month one

Why does an app sometimes show an order as cancelled after it was delivered?

Because somewhere in the code, any status is allowed to replace any other. Our learners meet this bug in their first delivery-app project, and fix it with one of the oldest ideas in computing: the state machine.

The situation

A delivery app tracks an order through five statuses: placed, accepted, on the way, delivered and cancelled. The rider taps "delivered" at the door. A second later, a customer who has not refreshed the screen taps "cancel", and the request reaches the server after the rider's.

Version 1 below does what many first apps do: whatever status arrives last wins. The order that was handed over ends up marked as cancelled, and a real system might then refund it.

The question for the learner

Before running anything, the mentor asks: which changes of status should be possible at all? Can a delivered order be cancelled? Can a cancelled order go back on the road? Learners write the answer on paper as arrows between boxes.

Those arrows are the program. Version 2 turns them into a dictionary, and the update function refuses every move that is not one of the arrows.

The last line is the one learners remember. With five statuses there are 20 possible changes from one status to a different one, and only 5 of them make sense. Version 1 accepted all 20. Version 2 accepts exactly the 5 on the diagram and keeps a history, so a support agent can see what happened to order A-17 without guessing.

The examples are written in Python so the idea is visible without any framework around it. In the Flutter course the same dictionary becomes a Dart map, in Kotlin a sealed class with a when expression, and in Swift an enum. The thinking is identical, which is the point: a learner who understands why the refusal belongs in the code can write it in any language.

  1. Draw it. Add a status called ready for pickup between accepted and on the way. Which arrows change?
  2. Break it. Allow a cancel from on the way. Which real situation is this useful for, and what must the app also do about the rider?
  3. Test it. Write a check that tries all 20 changes and prints how many are refused. It should print 15 before you change anything.
  4. Store it. Save the history with a time for each step, and show it on a screen in the app.

The code, its output and the practice tasks are ours, written for this page and run with Python 3 on 9 November 2026. Order A-17 is invented.

A common path in Peru

Many of our adult learners start with Flutter, because a single project reaches both stores and a portfolio app can be shown on whatever phone the interviewer carries. Once comfortable, some move to Kotlin to understand what Flutter is doing underneath on Android.

Learners without a Mac usually leave Swift for later. That is not a judgement on iOS, only a practical point about what the store requires to build.

Where Python fits

Python is not used to build most phone apps, but it is how we teach the first logic, as in the lesson above, and it is the language of the server many apps talk to. MINEDU's Fábrica de Programadores pilot taught Python to 1,000 students in 4° and 5° de secundaria, so some younger adults arrive already knowing it.

If you have that background, we skip ahead. If you do not, the first weeks of any track teach programming itself.

Language of the classes

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. English is the only teaching language, in every live online session, with a mentor based in India. Better to know that now than after enrolling; the mentor adapts to you.

How the mentor teaches in English

Mentors speak at a measured pace, rephrase when you hesitate and get used to your accent within a couple of classes. You can ask a question in imperfect English and be answered patiently; nobody is marked down for grammar.

Most of a class is spent looking at the same code on a shared screen. Error messages, documentation, store consoles and almost every framework tutorial are written in English anyway, so the language of the class matches the language of the work.

What the English figures say about Peru

In the 2025 edition of EF's English Proficiency Index, Peru is placed 52nd among 123 countries on 519 points, which EF calls moderate. Lima reaches 555, inside EF's high band, and Peruvians in IT jobs average 522.

For many adults in Peru, then, English is a working language that is not yet fully comfortable. Studying app development in it is practice that pays twice: in the code, and in the interviews and calls that come after.

If you are deciding between this and a Spanish-language course, try the free class first. It is a short coding task and a conversation, and the mentor tells you honestly whether the English will be a help or a hurdle at your current level.

Clock facts: Peru runs on UTC minus 5 in every month, without daylight saving since 1994 (zone America/Lima in the tz database; INACAL publishes the official hour). India runs on UTC plus 5:30. The gap is therefore 10 hours 30 minutes, every day.

Working adults often choose an early slot before the office or a late one after it; university students fit classes between lectures. You pick the time in Peru time when you book, the mentor sees the India time, and because neither country moves its clocks, the slot never drifts between March and October.

Questions from Peru

App development course in Peru: questions and answers

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. We teach in English only, live and online, and our mentors in India keep their speech clear, follow your pace, get used to your accent and say it again more simply whenever you ask. The code, the documentation and the store consoles are English as well.

Can I learn app development from zero?

Yes. All three starting courses on this page assume no programming. The first weeks teach the language itself, Dart, Kotlin or Swift, before any app screen appears.

Flutter or Kotlin: which should I learn first in Peru?

Flutter if you want one app on both Android and iPhone; Kotlin if you want to work on Android apps in depth. Many learners start with Flutter and add Kotlin later. The free class is a good place to decide with a mentor.

Do I need a Mac?

Only for iPhone apps built with Swift, because Apple requires Xcode for App Store builds and Xcode runs on macOS. Flutter and Kotlin work on Windows, macOS or Linux; Flutter can still produce an iPhone build later on a Mac.

What does Google Play require in 2026?

From 31 August 2026, new apps and updates must target Android 16 (API level 36) or higher, and existing apps need Android 15 (API level 35) or higher to stay available to new users on newer Android versions. We start every project on a current template.

Does Peru's data protection law apply to my app?

If your app collects personal data from people in Peru, very likely: DS 016-2024-JUS covers even controllers outside Peru that offer services to people there, and it has been in force since 2025. We teach privacy habits, not legal advice; ask a lawyer for a real product.

What time are the classes?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. Peru is 10 hours 30 minutes behind India all year, so 08:00 in Peru is 18:30 for the mentor.

How long does it take to build a first app?

Most learners have a small working app within the first few months and a store-ready project towards the end of the course. Our app courses run 10 to 12 months for a complete beginner; the pace depends on your weekly practice.

How much does it cost?

See the monthly fee in US dollars in the fees section; that is the currency your card pays in, with your bank handling the switch to soles. Browsing from India shows rupees. Your first class costs nothing and no card is taken.

Will I get a certificate?

The course ends with our own certificate of completion when you pass the final exam. It is not issued by a Peruvian university or by any ministry; the apps on your GitHub are the stronger evidence.

Is there an app course for teenagers?

Yes, a separate one for ages 13 to 18, taught in its own groups. Our Spanish page for Peruvian teenagers explains it, and the teen course appears in the list above.
