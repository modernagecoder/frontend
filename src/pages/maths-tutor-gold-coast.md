# Maths tutor on the Gold Coast

On a winter afternoon in Surfers Paradise, the tall towers along the beachfront throw long shadows towards the sand. How long? The answer needs just two numbers, the height of the building and the angle of the sun above the horizon, and one ratio from trigonometry. A 158 metre tower on the June solstice at 3:00 pm casts a shadow about 417 metres long; at noon in December, barely 13. Working it out is a perfect Year 10 problem with a real place behind it. ## How long are the shadows of Surfers Paradise towers?

If the sun is a certain angle above the horizon, a building of height h casts a shadow of length h divided by the tangent of that angle. The hard part is finding the angle.

We took building heights from OpenStreetMap for a small area of Surfers Paradise; six buildings there are tagged as 50 metres or taller. Then we calculated the sun's height in the sky at latitude 28 degrees south using the standard approximation published for solar position, for three dates in 2026 and two times of day: solar noon, when the sun is highest, and 3:00 pm Queensland time.

**Sun elevation above the horizon at latitude 28 degrees south, 2026 (our calculation)**

| Date | Solar noon (AEST) | Elevation at noon | Elevation at 3:00 pm | Shadow per metre at 3:00 pm |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 21 June (winter solstice) | 11:48 | 38.55&deg; | 20.73&deg; | 2.64 m |
| 23 September (equinox) | 11:39 | 61.58&deg; | 34.12&deg; | 1.48 m |
| 21 December (summer solstice) | 11:44 | 85.41&deg; | 45.9&deg; | 0.97 m |

**Shadow lengths for the six tallest tagged buildings (our calculation; heights as mapped)**

| Building | Height (m) | June noon (m) | June 3 pm (m) | December noon (m) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Circle on Cavill South Tower | 158 | 198 | 417 | 13 |
| Unnamed tower | 150 | 188 | 396 | 12 |
| Unnamed tower | 130 | 163 | 343 | 10 |
| Unnamed tower | 130 | 163 | 343 | 10 |
| Focus Apartments | 103 | 129 | 272 | 8 |
| Spectrum Holiday Apartments | 57 | 72 | 151 | 5 |

### The simple rule wins at noon

At solar noon, the sun's elevation is simply 90 degrees minus the gap between your latitude and the sun's declination. That shortcut gives 38.55 degrees in June, exactly matching the full calculation. For noon questions, the short rule is all you need.

### Afternoon needs the full method

At 3:00 pm the sun has moved west and dropped to 20.73 degrees in June. No simple rule gives that; it needs the hour angle. The shadow then stretches to 2.64 metres for every metre of height.

### Summer shrinks shadows

Near the December solstice the noon sun is almost overhead at 85.41 degrees, so a 158 metre tower casts a noon shadow of only about 13 metres.

### What students practise

Right-angled triangles, the tangent ratio, angles of elevation, rearranging a formula, and reading the effect of the seasons from a table.

Senior students can derive the noon rule from a diagram of the Earth and sun, and explore the full formula with the hour angle. Younger students can check the idea outdoors: measure a friend's height and shadow at lunchtime, divide, and compare the ratio with the table.

### Honest limits

Heights are as tagged on the map, not official figures, and we assume flat ground in front of each building. Real shadows also depend on direction and on what they fall across.

### Can you help with Year 11 and 12 maths on the Gold Coast?

### How do you find the length of a shadow?

Divide the object's height by the tangent of the sun's elevation angle. On the June solstice at 3:00 pm on the Gold Coast, each metre of height casts about 2.64 metres of shadow.

### Is there a quick way to find the noon sun angle?

Yes: 90 degrees minus the difference between your latitude and the sun's declination. It matched the full calculation exactly at noon in our tests.
