## A three-year certificate, weighted 10, 20 and 70

Kuwait's school system runs in three stages and the last one is not a single examination year.

State education in Kuwait is organised as five years of primary covering Grades 1 to 5, four years of intermediate covering Grades 6 to 9, and three years of secondary covering Grades 10 to 12. Schooling is compulsory from age 6 to age 14, which is to say through the end of the intermediate stage. The Ministry of Education supervises secondary education in both government and private schools.

At the end of Grade 12 a student is awarded the **Shahadat Al-Thanawiya Al-A'ama**, the general secondary education certificate. From Grade 11 onward learners choose between a Science and an Arts specialisation, with religious and special education routes also available.

The part most families outside the system do not expect

The final certificate mark is not decided in one year. It accumulates: **Grade 10 carries 10 per cent, Grade 11 carries 20 per cent and Grade 12 carries 70 per cent**. A Grade 10 student who thinks the serious years start later has already begun contributing to the number that follows them to university admissions.

Read the other way round, that weighting is good news for anyone deciding when to add a skill. Ninety per cent of the certificate is still open at the start of Grade 11, and Grade 10 is the cheapest year in the whole sequence to spend two evenings a week on something that is not revision. By Grade 12, when 70 per cent of the mark is live, adding anything is expensive. That is the argument for starting early, and it is a structural argument rather than a sales one.

It also changes what an honest provider should say about workload. We publish two lessons a week as the normal pattern. For a Grade 12 learner in Kuwait, the sensible pattern is fewer, and telling a family that in advance is worth more than promising them that everything fits.

## Counting the terms left before the weighting bites

Every market page here has one analytical trap the class actually works through. Kuwait's is the one that has broken more real software than any exotic statistical error.

The task looks trivial. A learner is asked to write a small program that answers a question a Kuwaiti family genuinely asks: how many school terms are left before the next tranche of the certificate closes? Grade 10 opens the 10 per cent. Grade 11 doubles it. Grade 12 decides the rest. Given a learner's current grade and term, count what remains.

Almost every first attempt is wrong by exactly one, and it is wrong in a way that still looks right.

The trap

Inclusive ranges, and the fence that needs one more post than it has panels

Ask how many terms from the start of Grade 10 to the end of Grade 12 and the instinct is to subtract: 12 minus 10 is 2, times three terms, gives 6. The real answer counts both ends, so it is 3 years, not 2, and 9 terms. Subtracting two boundary numbers gives you the gaps between them, never the things themselves.

It is the same error as building a straight fence with ten panels and ordering ten posts. You need eleven. Programmers meet it as the off-by-one: a loop that runs one time too few, a range that quietly drops its last element, a report that is short by a single row and therefore never questioned.

What makes it a good first lesson in checking is that the wrong answer is plausible. Six terms is not absurd. Nobody notices it the way they would notice six hundred. The learner only finds it by doing the thing the class is really about: testing the smallest case by hand. How many terms from the start of Grade 12 to the end of Grade 12? If your formula says zero, it is broken, and now you know before it matters.

From there the work gets real. The learner writes the test first, decides whether a range should be inclusive at both ends and documents which, then discovers that the answer also depends on where in the term you ask. Every figure in the classroom exercise is the learner's own; the certificate weighting is the only fact taken from the system, and it is stated above with its source.

## Does coding still matter when AI can write it?

It is the fair question and it deserves a specific answer rather than reassurance.

Ask an AI to count the terms between Grade 10 and Grade 12 and it will hand you a confident, well-formatted, plausible answer. Sometimes it will be nine. Sometimes it will be six. It will not tell you which, and it will not sound less certain when it is wrong. The bottleneck was never typing the loop. It is knowing that an inclusive range needs checking, testing the smallest case by hand, and being unwilling to publish a number you have not verified.

That is what the project above trains, and it is why we teach logic before syntax. A learner who has personally been wrong by one, found it, and fixed it will check the machine's arithmetic for the rest of their life. A learner who has only ever accepted a generated answer has no reason to.
