## What does IB maths tutoring look like? The derivative as a limit, in Python

This is a lesson we use with AA students before they meet differentiation from first principles, and with AI students who want to know what their calculator is doing. It shows a limit being approached, predicts the error with algebra, and ends with a surprise about computers.

### The set-up

Take f(x) = x³ at x = 2. The derivative is 3x², so the exact gradient there is 12. A difference quotient estimates it from two nearby points: the forward quotient uses x and x + h; the central quotient uses x - h and x + h.

Version 1 computes both for h = 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001. Before running it, we ask the student to expand (2 + h)³ by hand and predict the forward quotient exactly.

### The algebra that predicts it

Expanding gives a forward quotient of 12 + 6h + h², so its error shrinks in step with h. The central quotient simplifies to 12 + h², so its error shrinks with h squared: ten times smaller h, a hundred times smaller error.

When the output matches the algebra to every printed digit, the student has seen the definition of the derivative behave exactly as the theory says. That is a far stronger memory than a formula in a textbook.

**Version 1: forward and central quotients approach 12** (Python)
```python
def f(x):
    return x ** 3

x = 2  # exact gradient of x cubed at x = 2 is 3 * 2**2 = 12
for h in (0.1, 0.01, 0.001):
    forward = (f(x + h) - f(x)) / h
    central = (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)
    print(f"h={h}: forward {forward:.6f}, central {central:.6f}")
```
It prints h=0.1: forward 12.610000, central 12.010000, then h=0.01: forward 12.060100, central 12.000100 and h=0.001: forward 12.006001, central 12.000001, exactly 12 + 6h + h² and 12 + h².

Now the question every good student asks: if smaller h is better, why not make h tiny? Version 2 tries h from 10-4 down to 10-15 with the forward quotient.

**Version 2: shrinking h until the computer pushes back** (Python)
```python
def f(x):
    return x ** 3

x = 2
for k in (4, 8, 12, 15):
    h = 10 ** -k
    forward = (f(x + h) - f(x)) / h
    print(f"h=1e-{k}: forward {forward:.10f}, error {abs(forward - 12):.1e}")
```
It prints h=1e-4: forward 12.0006000100, error 6.0e-04, h=1e-8: forward 11.9999999271, error 7.3e-08, h=1e-12: forward 12.0010668070, error 1.1e-03 and h=1e-15: forward 10.6581410364, error 1.3e+00.

The error falls until h is about 10-8, then rises again, and at 10-15 the estimate is off by more than one whole unit. The computer stores numbers to about sixteen significant digits, so subtracting two almost equal values of f throws most of those digits away. **A limit is a mathematical idea, not a button you can press forever.**

For an AA student this is first principles made visible, with the algebraic error terms checked by experiment. For an AI student it explains why a calculator's numerical derivative is an approximation and how to judge it. For both, it is the kind of question, "where does this method stop working?", that turns an ordinary exploration into a thoughtful one, provided the student chooses and writes it themselves.

1. **Before the Diploma.** Compute (f(2 + h) - f(2))/h by hand for f(x) = x² and h = 0.1. What number is it heading towards?
2. **SL.** Expand (2 + h)³ and show that the forward quotient equals 12 + 6h + h². Explain in English why the error is roughly proportional to h.
3. **HL.** Show that the central quotient for x³ at any x is 3x² + h². Then predict which h gives the smallest error on a computer, and test it.
4. **AI students.** Repeat Version 1 with f(x) = eˣ at x = 0, where the exact answer is 1, and report the errors in a table with one honest sentence of interpretation.

## What does an IB maths tutor do across the two Diploma years?

Less lecturing than parents imagine and more careful practice. A tutor's value is in the gaps a school class has no time for, and in the written English that the papers reward.

1. **Repair the base early.** Algebra, functions and trigonometry from the MYP or from secundaria are checked in the first weeks. A weak base costs marks in every later topic.
2. **Follow the school's order.** We teach topics when the school teaches them, so tutoring deepens class work instead of competing with it.
3. **Train each paper differently.** As our course teaches it, AA work includes non-calculator fluency and AI work includes efficient calculator routines. Both include writing working that an examiner can follow.
4. **Teach the command words.** "Show that", "hence", "justify" and "interpret" each ask for something specific. We practise answering exactly what was asked, in full English sentences where needed.
5. **Support the exploration honestly.** We teach the mathematics a student's idea needs, the assessment criteria and how to critique a draft of their own. We never write, edit or ghost-write the real exploration; the school's rules decide what help is allowed.
6. **Rehearse under time.** From August of the final year, full timed papers, then careful correction of every lost mark, sorted by cause: knowledge, method, reading or rushing.
7. **Keep November calm.** In the final weeks, short sessions on the student's three weakest question types, and nothing new.

One tutor stays with the student across both years. That matters in IB maths, where the same student may be confident in calculus and lost in probability, and a teacher who knows their pattern of mistakes saves months.

Predict before you calculate. A prediction you can check is worth more than a result you cannot.
When a method fails, write down why. That sentence is often the best line in an exploration.
