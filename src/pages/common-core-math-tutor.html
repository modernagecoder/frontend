Grade by grade

What does Common Core math cover in each grade?

Each grade from kindergarten to grade 8 opens with a short introduction naming two to four critical areas, the topics where instructional time should go first. Knowing your child's critical areas tells you which homework matters most this year.

Critical areas named in the Common Core grade introductions, kindergarten to grade 8 (thecorestandards.org, read 31 October 2026; wording shortened by us except where quoted)
GradeNumber of critical areasWhat the standards put first
KTwoRepresenting and comparing whole numbers; describing shapes and space. "More learning time in Kindergarten should be devoted to number than to other topics."
1FourAddition and subtraction with "strategies for addition and subtraction within 20"; place value in tens and ones; measuring length; composing shapes
2Four"extending understanding of base-ten notation"; fluency with addition and subtraction; standard units of measure; shapes
3FourMultiplication and division within 100; fractions, "especially unit fractions (fractions with numerator 1)"; arrays and area; two-dimensional shapes
4ThreeMulti-digit multiplication and division; "fraction equivalence" and adding fractions with like denominators; classifying figures by their properties
5ThreeAdding and subtracting fractions fluently; dividing by 2-digit divisors and decimals to hundredths; "developing understanding of volume"
6Four"connecting ratio and rate to whole number multiplication and division"; negative numbers and rational numbers; expressions and equations; statistical thinking
7FourProportional relationships; operations with rational numbers and linear equations; scale drawings, area, surface area and volume; "drawing inferences about populations based on samples"
8ThreeLinear equations and systems; "grasping the concept of a function"; distance, angle, similarity, congruence and the Pythagorean Theorem

Domains: the folders inside each grade

Inside each grade the standards are grouped into domains. Kindergarten to grade 5 use Operations and Algebraic Thinking, Number and Operations in Base Ten, Measurement and Data, and Geometry, with Counting and Cardinality only in kindergarten and Number and Operations, Fractions from grade 3 to grade 5.

Grades 6 and 7 switch to Ratios and Proportional Relationships, The Number System, Expressions and Equations, Geometry, and Statistics and Probability. Grade 8 drops ratios and adds Functions. A homework code such as 4.NF.B.3 reads as grade 4, Number and Operations, Fractions, cluster B, standard 3.

Limits written into the standards

Some standards carry footnotes that narrow them. The grade 4 fractions domain says "Grade 4 expectations in this domain are limited to fractions with denominators 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 100." Grade 3 is limited to halves, thirds, fourths, sixths and eighths.

So a fourth grader adding sevenths is working beyond the grade, and a teacher who sticks to twelfths and hundredths is following the text exactly. When we plan lessons we check these limits first, so practice matches what the class will be assessed on.

The eight practices

What are the Common Core math practice standards, and how do they show up in homework?

There are eight Standards for Mathematical Practice, and they apply in every grade. They are why a worksheet says "explain how you know" or "solve it another way". Here is how each one tends to appear on paper, and what a tutor does with it.

The eight Standards for Mathematical Practice (names from thecorestandards.org, read 31 October 2026); the homework and tutoring columns are ours
PracticeHow it often appears on homeworkWhat our teacher asks the child
MP1 Make sense of problems and persevere in solving themA word problem with no hint about the operation"What is the question asking for, before any numbers?"
MP2 Reason abstractly and quantitativelyWrite an equation for a story, then say what the answer means"Your answer is 12. Twelve what?"
MP3 Construct viable arguments and critique the reasoning of others"A classmate says 3/8 is bigger than 3/4. Agree or disagree?""Convince me, using a drawing."
MP4 Model with mathematicsTape diagrams, bar models, tables from a situation"Draw what is happening, then write the math."
MP5 Use appropriate tools strategicallyChoose a number line, a ruler or a table"Which tool makes this quicker?"
MP6 Attend to precisionLabel units; use the equal sign correctly"Is 3 + 4 = 7 + 2 = 9 true? Fix it."
MP7 Look for and make use of structureSplit 7 x 8 into easier facts"Which fact you already know is hiding inside this one?"
MP8 Look for and express regularity in repeated reasoningComplete a pattern, then describe the rule"What stays the same each time?"

Checking by a second route

The MP1 text says mathematically proficient students "check their answers to problems using a different method", and that they keep asking whether the answer makes sense. That single habit explains most "show another way" questions.

In our lessons it becomes a routine: solve once, check once by a different route, and only then move on. A child who learns that early catches a large share of their own mistakes without anyone telling them.

The equal sign and the hidden fact

MP6 asks students to state what their symbols mean, "including using the equal sign consistently and appropriately". Children who write running chains such as 3 + 4 = 7 + 2 = 9 are recording steps, but the line is false, and the standards want them to notice.

MP7 gives its own example: students "will see 7 x 8 equals the well remembered 7 x 5 + 7 x 3", which is the distributive property years before it gets a name. We teach facts that way, so a forgotten fact can be rebuilt from two known ones.

Why the models come first

In the grade 2 example, 368 + 457 is solved by adding 300 and 400, then 60 and 50, then 8 and 7. Standard 2.NBT.B.7 asks children to add within 1000 using place-value strategies and to "relate the strategy to a written method". The written method comes next, and the regrouping in it is the 110 and the 15 above, compressed.

Place value is the foundation the whole sequence rests on. Standard 2.NBT.A.1 says "706 equals 7 hundreds, 0 tens, and 6 ones". A child who can say that sentence for any three-digit number rarely struggles with carrying.

Why fractions trip children up

The grade 4 example 4 x 2/3 is often answered as 8/12, because the child multiplies both parts. Reading it as four groups of two thirds, or eight thirds, makes the right answer obvious and lets the child estimate: eight thirds is a little under three.

Our teachers ask for that estimate before any calculation. It is MP2 in practice, and it turns a fraction rule that is easy to forget into a picture that is hard to forget.

The grade 8 example shows where the elementary models lead. Expanding 3(x - 2) uses the same distributive idea as the area model for 4(3 + y), which in turn is the 7 x 8 = 7 x 5 + 7 x 3 idea from grade 3. When a tutor can show a child that thread, algebra feels like an old friend in new clothes rather than a new subject.

With your curriculum

How do we tutor alongside a Common Core curriculum?

The standards are goals, not lesson plans, so every district picks its own materials. Our job is to teach the goal behind each page your child brings home, in the representation the school uses, and to keep the homework the child's own work.

Standards and curriculum are different things

The Common Core FAQ puts it in one line: "the Common Core is what students need to know and be able to do, and curriculum is how students will learn it." It adds that the standards "do not mandate the use of any particular curriculum."

That is why two children in different districts can meet the same standard with very different worksheets. We ask families which program their school uses, look at a recent page together, and teach in that program's language.

What a lesson looks like

A typical hour opens with five minutes of facts or mental math, moves to the week's critical-area topic with a model and its equation, and closes with a short mixed set the child does alone while the teacher watches the reasoning.

If there is homework, the child reads the question aloud and explains the first step. The teacher asks questions and draws, but the pencil stays with the child. We never complete graded work for a student.

  1. Share one recent worksheet or a photo of it. It tells us the program, the unit and the representation in use.
  2. Book the free first class. The teacher finds where the child's understanding breaks, usually one or two grades back in the same domain.
  3. Agree a short plan. Most plans pair the current unit with one older gap, for example place value under a multi-digit unit.
  4. Choose a format. A group of 15 to 20 children at the same grade, or 1-on-1 lessons paced to your school's calendar.
  5. Review each month. We look at recent class work with you and adjust the next month's focus.

At home

Can parents help with Common Core math homework?

Yes, and the initiative itself expects that it may feel unfamiliar. Its FAQ says parents "may find the methods and approaches different or have difficulty helping their children with homework", and suggests starting with a conversation with the teacher.

Four questions that work at the kitchen table

Ask "what is the problem about?" before "what is the answer?". Ask "can you draw it?" when a child is stuck. Ask "how could you check it a different way?" once there is an answer. And ask "what do you already know that looks like this?" when a fact or step has been forgotten.

None of these needs you to know the method on the page. Each one matches a practice standard, so you are helping with exactly what the homework is aiming at.

Which states use the standards

The initiative reports that forty-one states, the District of Columbia, four territories and the Department of Defense Education Activity have adopted the standards, and that states may change or add to them. Some states publish their own standards under another name.

Our teachers work from whichever set your state uses. The big ideas, place value, fractions, ratio and early algebra, appear in every version we have taught to.

Where your child is

Four stages we see in Common Core math

Our description of how children move from models to fluent, flexible methods. Most children are in different stages for different domains.

Stages of understanding we use when planning lessons (ours)
StageWhat we see in the workWhat we do next
1. CountingCounts every object; fingers for every factTen frames, making ten, fact families
2. ModelsDraws the strip, the array or the table, slowlyConnect each picture to its equation
3. RecordsWrites the equation; the model is optionalMixed practice; check by a second route
4. FlexiblePicks the quickest route and explains itHarder word problems and the next grade's ideas

For parents

A child in stage 2 is not behind. The standards spend a year on models before asking for a fast written method, and that year is where understanding is built.

The warning sign is a child who jumps to stage 3 without stage 2 and cannot say why a step works. That is where we usually start.

For students

Drawing a quick picture is not babyish. Strong mathematicians sketch all the time.

When you know a fact, ask how you could rebuild it if you forgot. That question is your safety net.

Questions from parents

Common Core math tutoring: common questions

What does a Common Core math tutor do?

A Common Core math tutor teaches the goals in the Common Core State Standards for your child's grade, in the methods the school uses, and builds the eight practices such as explaining and checking. Our teachers also find older gaps, often in place value or fractions, that make the current unit hard.

Is there a Common Core math tutor near me?

Our lessons are live online, so distance does not matter. Your child joins by video from home and works with the same teacher each week, in a group or one to one.

What are the Common Core math practice standards?

They are eight Standards for Mathematical Practice that apply in every grade, from making sense of problems and persevering (MP1) to looking for regularity in repeated reasoning (MP8). They describe habits of mind rather than topics.

Which grades do you tutor in Common Core math?

Kindergarten to grade 8 on this page. For high school, our algebra, geometry, Algebra 2 and precalculus tutoring continue from where grade 8 ends.

Will the tutor do my child's homework?

No. The teacher explains the idea with a fresh example, asks questions and checks the reasoning, but the child writes their own homework. Graded work stays the student's own.

My child's school uses its own Common Core curriculum. Is that a problem?

No. The standards do not prescribe a curriculum, so districts choose their own. Send us a recent worksheet and the teacher will work in the same representations your child sees in class.

Why does Common Core homework use drawings and models?

Many standards ask children to use models such as ten frames, arrays, fraction strips or ratio tables before moving to a faster written method. The model shows why the method works, which makes later topics easier.

How quickly will my child catch up?

It depends on the gap and on practice between lessons, so we do not promise a timescale or a grade. After the free first class the teacher explains what is missing and suggests a plan, and we review progress with you each month.

When are your Common Core math classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

Are you connected with the Common Core initiative or my school district?

No. Modern Age Coders is an independent online school. We quote the published standards and link to them, and we are not affiliated with the initiative, any state or any district.
