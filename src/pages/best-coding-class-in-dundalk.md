## A category is not a measurement

Internet access in Dundalk, Census 2022, with every answer the table offers. Counts are published and shares are ours.

**Household internet access, Dundalk and all towns combined**

| Answer as published | Dundalk households | Share | All towns combined |
|---|---|---|---|
| Broadband | 12,686 | 79.7% | 82.2% |
| Other | 310 | 1.9% | 1.8% |
| No | 1,454 | 9.1% | 7.3% |
| Not stated | 1,473 | 9.3% | 8.7% |
| Total households | 15,923 | 100% | 1,317,616 |

### What the variable holds

One answer for each household, chosen from four, recorded on one day in April 2022, about the household rather than the people in it. That is the whole of it, and the four rows sum exactly to the 15,923 households in the town.

### What it cannot hold

Speed. Reliability. Behaviour at busy times. Whether the signal reaches the room a learner works in. Cost. Whether the household would call the connection good. None of those questions was asked, so none has an answer here, for Dundalk or anywhere else.

### What would answer it

Measurements rather than categories: recorded speeds over time, for identified locations. We have not gathered any such data for this town, and so this page makes no claim at all about how fast anything in Dundalk is.

### The proxy problem

Page views standing in for interest. Time in the building standing in for work done. Tickets closed standing in for customers helped. Exam marks standing in for understanding. Lines of code standing in for progress. Each proxy is easier to collect than the thing it represents, which is why it gets collected, and each one drifts away from that thing the moment somebody starts managing the proxy instead. Naming what a column measures, and naming what it was chosen to stand in for, is the difference between a number that informs a decision and one that quietly replaces it.

The project is a data dictionary rather than a chart. For this one variable the learner writes down the question asked, the four permitted answers, the unit counted, the date, and then three questions the variable cannot answer, each with the kind of data that could. They finish by taking a measurement of their own, the connection test we run in the free lesson, and writing one paragraph on what the measurement tells them that the census category does not. It is the shortest project in this cluster and the one that travels furthest, because every dataset a learner meets later will have a column whose name promises more than the column contains.

## Ask whether Dundalk has good internet

An answer will arrive, built from a table that never asked.

A model handed this table and asked about internet quality will usually convert 79.7% into a verdict, sometimes with words like strong or solid attached. The number is right and the verdict is unsupported, because the underlying question was about a category and the answer is about performance. Nothing in the reply marks the swap.

A learner who has written the data dictionary answers in two parts instead: here is what the census counted, and here is the measurement you would need for the question you asked. They can also say which one we run before a course starts, and why it is the one that matters for a live lesson.

The interesting skill in 2026 is not getting a number out of a tool. It is knowing what the number was ever able to mean. The longer argument is in [why learning to code holds its value](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
