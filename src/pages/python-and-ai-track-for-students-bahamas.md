# Four quarters. Four finished things. One teacher for the whole year.

> The twelve-month Python and AI track is a live online programme of weekly ninety-minute sessions for learners in the Bahamas aged six to sixty-seven, organised into four quarters. Each quarter ends in a finished project running at a public address, with the code readable on GitHub. Underneath it sit four things a camp cannot offer: the same teacher for twelve months, an individual written progress record, competitions used as external deadlines, and a monthly written update to the parent. Group teaching is USD 100 a month, private teaching USD 150, and the first class is free.

## Twelve months, four quarters, four finished things

The unit of this programme is a year, not a term and not a week, because the things it is trying to build do not happen faster than that. Here is the shape.

| Quarter | The work | What exists at the end |
|---|---|---|
| One | Python by hand: files, errors, functions, the habit of reading a traceback | A working program the student wrote from a blank file |
| Two | Real data: missing rows, wrong units, a number stored as text | Charts they made, from a file they cleaned |
| Three | Machine learning: a baseline first, then a model, then an error in units | A model that beat doing nothing clever, with the figure stated |
| Four | Agents and publication: bounded, logged, deployed, documented | A repository a stranger can open and assess |

Four quarters, four finished projects. Not one large project improved continuously, which is the failure mode of ambitious students everywhere, but four things that were finished, written up and left alone.

## The three things a year buys that a term cannot

**The same teacher throughout.** This is the mechanism, not a comfort feature. Somebody has to be in a position to notice, in month seven, that a student never really understood something in month two. Nobody can do that across four different instructors.

**Fifty-one gaps.** Between the sessions is where the work happens. We set something to build rather than something to complete, because the build is what keeps last week alive. A concept met in week three and needed in week five gets consolidated by use rather than by revision.

**A commit history.** Twelve months of small dated commits is the one thing about student work that cannot be manufactured at the end, and it is consequently the thing a technical reader trusts most.

## Individual progress tracking, described precisely

Once a month, in writing, per student: what they built, what they got stuck on, what was fixed, and what the next month is aimed at. Not a grade and not a percentage, because neither carries information about a fourteen year old writing software.

It exists for two practical reasons. It lets a family see whether a real thing is being built, and it lets a student who pauses for an examination year resume from where they actually were rather than being re-diagnosed from scratch.

## What we will not claim about the year

That it leads to a job, a university place or a competition result. That it appears on a Bahamian school syllabus, because it does not. That the completion certificate is a credential recognised by anybody but us, because it is not.

What we will claim is the portfolio, and the portfolio is the part with external value. A repository containing four projects, each with a stated error, a baseline and a written limitation, is something an admissions reader or an interviewer can open and assess directly. That is a materially different asset from a certificate, and it is the actual deliverable of the twelve months.
