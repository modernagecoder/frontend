### How to read Not named

Not named means the course does not appear in that university&rsquo;s published list of required courses for that program on that day. It does not mean the university considers the course unimportant, and it does not mean a student can skip it. Ontario&rsquo;s own math curriculum (revised 2007) says &ldquo;Advanced Functions (MHF4U) must be taken prior to or concurrently with Calculus and Vectors (MCV4U).&rdquo; So any student with MCV4U on the transcript has MHF4U too, whichever course a university chooses to name.

Every row also sits inside a general rule about how many Grade 12 U or M courses a student needs. Waterloo and McMaster both say six, and the central U of T page asks for six 4U or M subjects with English among them.

### The UBC row is a different kind of entry

UBC&rsquo;s computer science department states it directly: &ldquo;there is no direct admission into the Computer Science program&rdquo;. Students first join a faculty with computer science specializations, such as Science, and seek the computer science specialization later. The row above is therefore UBC&rsquo;s Science list for Ontario students, the usual first step toward computer science there.

Hence the science courses, at both Grade 12 and Grade 11 level, in that row. UBC says the Grade 11 courses &ldquo;may be waived with scores of 86% or higher in senior-level Math and Chemistry&rdquo;.

For students in British Columbia and Alberta the calculus course is the swing factor. SFU asks for at least five approved academic Grade 12 courses.

SFU also sets a general rule for every program: a minimum of 70 per cent in Language Arts 11, English 12 or an equivalent. UBC&rsquo;s general requirements for British Columbia include a 70 per cent minimum in senior English.

## Why computer science asks for this math: eight problems

These eight problems are ours, written for this page. Each one takes a Grade 12 topic from the lists above and shows where it reappears in computing. Questions 1 to 3 use Advanced Functions and Pre-calculus ideas; 4 to 8 use Calculus and Vectors. Try them before reading the answers.

1. Logarithms and search. A sorted list holds 1,000,000 names. Binary search checks the middle item and keeps only the half that can still contain the target. What is the largest number of comparisons it can need?
2. Exponential growth. A brute-force program&rsquo;s running time doubles each time the input grows by one item. With 30 items it takes 1 second. Roughly how long does it take with 40 items?
3. Sums and nested loops. A loop runs i from 0 to n &minus; 1, and inside it a second loop runs j from 0 to i &minus; 1, adding 1 to a counter each time. Find a formula for the final count, then evaluate it for n = 100.
4. Rates of change from data. A sorting routine takes 0.02 seconds for 1,000 items and 0.08 seconds for 2,000. Assume time is k times n squared. Find k and predict the time for 5,000 items.
5. Vectors and motion. A game character starts at (2, 3) and moves with velocity (4, &minus;1) units per second. Where is it after 2.5 seconds, and what is its speed?
6. The dot product. A wall in a game runs along the direction (3, 4). Is the direction (4, &minus;3) perpendicular to it? Why would a game engine care?
7. Derivatives and learning. A model&rsquo;s error is f(x) = x&sup2; &minus; 6x + 10. Starting at x = 0, gradient descent repeatedly replaces x with x &minus; 0.25 f&prime;(x). Give the first two new values of x, and the value the process approaches.
8. Limits and approximation. Without a calculator, estimate the slope of f(x) = x&sup3; at x = 2 using the points x = 2 and x = 2.1. Compare it with the exact derivative.

## Worked answers to the eight problems

Each answer shows the reasoning, because the reasoning is what first-year courses build on. Three answers are also checked by a short program at the end.

1. 20 comparisons. After k comparisons at most n divided by 2 to the k items remain, so we need the smallest k with 2 to the k at least 1,000,001. Since 2 to the 19 is 524,288 and 2 to the 20 is 1,048,576, k is 20. Twenty steps for a million items is why logarithms matter in computing.
2. About 17 minutes. Ten more items means ten doublings: 2 to the 10 is 1,024, so 1,024 seconds, a little over 17 minutes. Ten more after that would be about 12 days. Exponential algorithms stop being usable very quickly.
3. n(n &minus; 1)/2, so 4,950. The inner loop runs 0 times, then 1, then 2, up to n &minus; 1 times: an arithmetic series whose sum is n(n &minus; 1)/2. For n = 100 that is 100 times 99 divided by 2, or 4,950. Doubling n roughly quadruples the work.
4. k = 0.00000002, and 0.5 seconds. From 0.02 = k(1,000)&sup2;, k = 2 &times; 10 to the &minus;8. The second measurement agrees: k(2,000)&sup2; = 0.08. Then k(5,000)&sup2; = 2 &times; 10 to the &minus;8 &times; 25,000,000 = 0.5 seconds. Checking a model against a second data point before predicting is the habit to keep.
5. (12, 0.5), speed about 4.12. Position is start plus time times velocity: (2 + 4 &times; 2.5, 3 &minus; 1 &times; 2.5) = (12, 0.5). Speed is the length of the velocity vector, the square root of 16 + 1, which is the square root of 17, about 4.12 units per second.
6. Yes, perpendicular. The dot product is 3 &times; 4 + 4 &times; (&minus;3) = 12 &minus; 12 = 0, so the directions are at right angles. A game engine uses exactly this to find the wall&rsquo;s normal, the direction a ball bounces off along.
7. x = 1.5, then 2.25, approaching 3. The derivative is 2x &minus; 6. From 0: 0 &minus; 0.25 &times; (&minus;6) = 1.5. Then 1.5 &minus; 0.25 &times; (&minus;3) = 2.25. Each step halves the distance to 3, where the derivative is zero and the error is smallest. This is the idea behind how many machine learning models are trained.
8. About 12.61, against an exact 12. The secant slope is (2.1&sup3; &minus; 2&sup3;)/0.1 = (9.261 &minus; 8)/0.1 = 12.61. The derivative 3x&sup2; at 2 is 12. Shrinking the gap to 0.01 gives 12.0601, closer still: the limit idea computers use when they estimate derivatives numerically.

**Python · checking answers 1, 3 and 7 (ours)** (python)
```python
# Checking three of the practice answers by brute force (ours)
import math

def search_steps(n):
    """Worst-case comparisons for binary search on n sorted items."""
    steps, size = 0, n
    while size > 0:
        steps += 1
        size //= 2                 # each comparison keeps at most half
    return steps

def nested_count(n):
    count = 0
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i):         # j runs 0 .. i-1
            count += 1
    return count

def descend(x, rate, steps):
    """Gradient descent on f(x) = x**2 - 6x + 10, whose derivative is 2x - 6."""
    path = [x]
    for _ in range(steps):
        x = x - rate * (2 * x - 6)
        path.append(x)
    return path

print("Q1:", search_steps(1_000_000), "comparisons; log2 gives", math.ceil(math.log2(1_000_001)))
print("Q3:", nested_count(100), "and n(n-1)/2 gives", 100 * 99 // 2)
print("Q7:", descend(0, 0.25, 4))
```
Output: Q1: 20 comparisons; log2 gives 20. Q3: 4950 and n(n-1)/2 gives 4950. Q7: [0, 1.5, 2.25, 2.625, 2.8125]. The brute-force counts agree with the formulas, which is the point of checking a formula with code.

### What the eight problems have in common

None of them needs advanced code; all of them need the math to be understood rather than memorised. A student who can say why binary search takes 20 steps, or why the nested loop gives a triangular number, is doing first-year computer science reasoning already.

That is the standard we teach to. In our Grade 12 math classes, a formula comes with its reason and a second way to check it, and the checking is often a three-line program.

### Using the problems

Students can work them as written, then change the numbers: a list of a billion items, a loop that starts at 1, a learning rate of 0.6 instead of 0.25. Predict first, then run the code above with the new values.

Question 7 with a learning rate of 1.1 is worth trying in particular. Predict what happens, then run it. The result explains why choosing that number carefully matters in machine learning.

## Four stages from Grade 12 math to computing math

Our stages, from teaching Grade 12 students in Ontario and the western provinces who went on to computing degrees.

**Stages from school math to computing math (ours)**

| Stage | What a teacher notices |
|---|---|
| 1. Calculator | Gets numerical answers, but cannot say what they would mean in a program |
| 2. Translator | Writes a formula as code and code as a formula, for simple cases |
| 3. Estimator | Predicts roughly how long an algorithm will take before running it |
| 4. Modeller | Chooses the math a problem needs: logs, sums, rates or vectors |

### For parents

Ask your teen to estimate how many steps a search through a million items takes. Their answer, and how they reach it, shows the stage quickly.
Stage 2 is the hinge. Once a student can move between a formula and a loop, both subjects start reinforcing each other.

### For students

Every time you learn a formula, write a five-line program that tests it on real numbers. You will catch your own mistakes and remember the idea.
Practise estimating before calculating. Within a factor of ten is a good first target; then get closer.

## Computer science prerequisites in Canada: questions

### What high school courses do I need for computer science in Canada?

In the lists we read on 22 October 2026, every program named a senior English course and Grade 12 math. For Ontario applicants, Waterloo, U of T and McMaster named Calculus and Vectors, and UBC Science named Advanced Functions and a Grade 12 science. Each university and province has its own list.

### Is MCV4U required for computer science?

For five of the six Ontario routes we read, yes: Waterloo, all three U of T campuses and McMaster listed MCV4U. UBC Science's Ontario list named MHF4U and a Grade 12 science, not MCV4U. Universities we could not read may differ.

### Is MHF4U required for computer science?

Waterloo, U of T Mississauga, U of T Scarborough and UBC Science listed MHF4U for Ontario applicants. U of T's central page did not name it for St. George, and McMaster's list named ENG4U and MCV4U. Ontario's math curriculum requires MHF4U to be taken before or at the same time as MCV4U, so in practice students who take MCV4U take MHF4U as well.

### Do I need a computer science course in high school to study CS?

None of the required-course lists we read named one. Waterloo describes its recommended computer science courses as helpful background that does not affect the likelihood of admission. Taking one where the school offers it still makes first-year programming easier.

### What does a BC student need for computer science?

Calculus 12 or AP Calculus appears on the Waterloo and McMaster lists we read, and Waterloo adds Pre-Calculus 12. UBC Science and SFU name Pre-Calculus 12 instead, with SFU taking Calculus 12 as the alternative, and both add a Grade 12 science. English Studies 12 is accepted on all four lists.

### What does an Alberta student need for computer science?

Math 31 is on the Waterloo and McMaster lists, McMaster also taking AP Calculus, and Waterloo adds Mathematics 30-1. UBC Science accepts Math 30-1 or Math 31 together with a 30-level science and the 20-level chemistry and physics courses. English Language Arts 30-1 is on all three lists.

### Does UBC admit high school students straight into computer science?

UBC's computer science department says it does not. Students first join a faculty with computer science specializations, usually Science, and seek the specialization once first year is under way.

### Why is Queen's or the University of Alberta missing?

Their websites refused our request on 22 October 2026, so we stopped and did not use them. We also could not read usable requirement pages for Calgary, McGill or uOttawa that day. Please check those universities' own pages.

### Why does computer science need so much math?

Because first-year computing uses it immediately: logarithms in searching, sums in counting loop steps, rates of change in training models, vectors in graphics. The eight practice problems on this page show each link with a worked answer.
