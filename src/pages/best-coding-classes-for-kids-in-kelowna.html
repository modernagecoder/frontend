Coding classes for kids in Kelowna

What should a Kelowna family ask before choosing an online coding class for a child aged 6 to 12?

Ask whether every lesson is live with a real teacher, how many children share that teacher, whether class times are set in Pacific time, and what your child will have built after a term. Modern Age Coders teaches Kelowna children aged 6 to 12 live online, with a batch of 15 to 20 or a teacher alone, starting with Coding for Kids or Python and AI for Kids. The first class is free.

In a city the size of Kelowna, many families end up comparing online coding classes, because the choice of rooms to drive to is smaller than in Vancouver or Calgary. Online removes the drive but adds new questions: is the teacher really live, is the class built for Pacific time, and will a six-year-old cope with a screen? This page sets out those questions for Kelowna parents. It also shows what British Columbia’s Grade 6 and 7 technology modules can include, from Scratch to the history of Ada Lovelace and Alan Turing, and it walks through a small project our older students run on a City of Kelowna open data file: thirteen kilometre markers on the Okanagan Rail Trail, each with an elevation. We name and rank no other provider, and every question here applies to our classes too.

Questions to ask

Six questions for any online kids’ coding class, from a Kelowna parent

Our questions, written for Kelowna families comparing online classes for a child aged 6 to 12. A good provider will answer every one plainly; ask us the same.

Q1Is every lesson live, with a teacher who can see my child’s screen?
Recorded videos are useful for practice, but a child under twelve needs someone who notices confusion and changes the next question. Ask what share of lessons is live.
Q2How many children share one teacher, and how are they grouped?
Ask for the maximum, not the average, and whether a six-year-old could end up in the same group as an eleven-year-old.
Q3Are class times set in Pacific time, and do they change in November?
BC stopped changing its clocks in 2026. A provider that still talks about falling back on 1 November 2026 has not caught up, and may book your child at the wrong hour.
Q4How long is each lesson for my child’s age?
Screen attention grows with age. A short, lively lesson for a six-year-old and a full hour for an eleven-year-old are both signs of a class that thinks about children.
Q5What will my child have built after a term, and can they explain it?
A finished project the child can run and talk through is the clearest evidence of learning you will get.
Q6Is the monthly fee published before I book a trial?
You should be able to compare fees before anyone calls you.

Online is not one thing

A live class with a small group, a live class with a crowd, and a library of recorded videos are all called online. They are very different for a child of seven.

Try before you commit

Most providers offer a first class without charge. Use it to watch your child’s face as much as the screen: interest, frustration and pride are all visible.

Ask your child later

An hour after the trial, ask what they made. Children who answer with a story about a bug they fixed have been taught; children who shrug have watched.

Kelowna data project

Is the Okanagan Rail Trail downhill? Thirteen markers, read in Python

The City of Kelowna publishes the kilometre markers it placed on the Okanagan Rail Trail, from the Airport to the North End of Kelowna, as open data. Each marker has a label, an elevation and a map position. Our students aged about 10 to 12 turn those into an answer. The numbers below are ours.

Our script. The label 38KM is text, so replace("KM", "") and int() turn it into the number 38 before sorting; zip then pairs each marker with the next one:import json, math with open("km_markers.json", encoding="utf-8") as f: features = json.load(f)["features"] markers = [] for feature in features: a = feature["attributes"] km = int(a["Distance_KM"].replace("KM", "")) markers.append((km, a["Elevation"], a["X"], a["Y"])) markers.sort() for here, there in zip(markers, markers[1:]): drop = here[1] - there[1] gap = math.dist(here[2:], there[2:]) print(here[0], "to", there[0], "km: down", round(drop, 1), "m, straight line", round(gap), "m")The elevation falls at every step, from 411.473 at km 38 to 345.182 at km 50, a total drop of 66.291 over twelve kilometres. So in the direction the kilometres count, the trail is gently downhill all the way.

Why text sorts wrongly

Sorted as text, labels compare letter by letter, so a label like 100KM would come before 38KM because 1 comes before 3. Converting to numbers first is the fix. It is a small bug with a big lesson.

A units check

The file does not state its units. The straight-line gaps come out between 961 and 1,016, which only makes sense if the map coordinates are in metres, since the markers are a kilometre apart. That reasoning is ours, and it is how scientists catch unit mistakes.

A gap longer than a kilometre

A straight line can never be longer than the path it shortcuts, yet one gap is 1,016. So the markers cannot all be exactly 1,000 apart along the trail. Children love finding that the data proves itself slightly imperfect.

What the file cannot tell you

Thirteen points only cover the Kelowna section, and an elevation at each marker says nothing about the slopes in between. The file also never says which end is uphill on the ground; our answer is about the direction the kilometre labels count. Students write those limits down before they draw a chart.

Kelowna on Pacific time all year: class times without a November change

Coding and AI

An AI tool can draw the rail trail chart. Can it tell you the units?

Not from this file, because the file does not say. Working that out is a human skill.

Give an AI assistant the thirteen markers and it will happily plot elevation against distance. It may also sort 38KM and 100KM as text, or announce the units with confidence it has not earned. A child who has reasoned from the gaps between markers, about a thousand each, to the conclusion that the coordinates are in metres, knows the difference between evidence and a guess.

That is the centre of our classes for children: look closely, reason, write the code, test it and explain it. Children learn how AI tools work and how they fail, and they use that knowledge to question answers. No chatbot does their thinking in class. At home, OpenAI asks that anyone under 18 has a parent’s permission before using ChatGPT.

BC’s Grade 6 and 7 module asks children to learn how programming languages evolved; knowing how a tool came to be is the first step to knowing what it cannot do. The longer argument is in why learning to code still matters in 2026.

FAQ

Kelowna parents’ questions about kids’ coding

What Kelowna families ask before a first class.

Do you have coding classes for kids in Kelowna?

Yes, live online. There is no Kelowna centre; your child joins from home and learns with a real teacher, either in a batch of 15 to 20 or one to one.

Is online coding suitable for a 6-year-old?

It can be, if lessons are short, live and full of talking and doing. Avoid long recorded videos for children this young.

What do BC schools teach about coding in elementary school?

In Grades 6 and 7 children take at least three ADST modules a year, chosen by the school. One option, Computational Thinking, includes visual programming such as Scratch and the history of programming languages.

Will class times change when other provinces change their clocks?

No. BC moved to permanent Pacific time, UTC-7, in 2026, so a Kelowna class time stays the same all year, while Ontario and Quebec still change on 1 November 2026.

When are classes held?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Every time is given in Pacific time.

When should my child move from Scratch to Python?

Usually when typing feels easy, which for many children is about age 9 or 10. The teacher watches for the signs and makes the move gradually.

Can my child enter a coding contest?

The Beaver Computing Challenge, run by CEMC, has levels for Grade 6 or below and Grade 8 or below, and needs no coding. Children write it through their school.

Do you teach robotics?

We teach the programming side, not robot hardware. BC high schools may offer Robotics 11, and our coding classes are good preparation for it.

How should I compare online classes?

Ask the six questions on this page: is it live, how big is the group, is it on Pacific time, how long are lessons, what will my child build, and what is the monthly fee.

What do the classes cost?

The fees section above lists a free first class and the monthly fees for a batch place and for one-to-one lessons. Nothing is charged before the free class.
