AI course for teens in Peru

This page is for secundaria students and their parents who want the deeper version: writing Python, training small models on real or invented data, and, above all, testing them so the score means something. It covers what such a course should include, what school in Peru already gives, which AI tools a 13 to 17 year old may legally use, and a lesson about a model that cheats without meaning to.

What teens learn

What should a teenager learn in an AI course?

Three things, in this order: enough Python to build a model, enough understanding of data to know what the model learned from, and enough discipline in testing to know whether it works. Most short courses teach only the first.

Build

Write Python that loads data, trains a simple model and makes predictions. Students start with models small enough to understand by hand, such as a single threshold, before they meet libraries that hide the arithmetic.

Understand the data

Where did the examples come from, who labelled them, what is missing? A model can only repeat the patterns in its data, so a student who cannot describe the data cannot explain the model.

Test honestly

Keep some data back, never let it leak into training, and report the result on examples the model has never seen. This is the habit that separates a project that works from one that only scores well.

Google's free Machine Learning Crash Course, written for developers, puts the third point bluntly: "The only fair test of a model is against new examples, not duplicates." It also warns that test sets "wear out" when the same one is used again and again to make decisions. We teach those two sentences to teenagers early, because almost every surprising result in a student project comes from breaking one of them.

By the end of the core course a student should be able to take a small dataset, train two different models, compare them fairly, and write a short report in English explaining which is better and why they believe it. That report, more than any certificate, shows what they learned.

Secundaria and AI

Does secundaria in Peru teach AI or programming?

Not as a timetabled subject. The national curriculum, the CNEB, has no computing area in secundaria; digital skills are a transversal competency spread across other subjects. MINEDU has, however, started several projects around programming and AI.

What this means for a student

A pilot for 1,000 students in one part of Lima is a real start, and its choice of Python matches what we teach. But most secundaria students in Peru will finish 5th year without a single timetabled lesson on how a program or a model works.

INEI's household survey shows the audience is ready: 92.5% of Peruvians aged 12 to 18 used the internet in the second quarter of 2026, up from 89.7% in 2023. Almost every teenager is online; very few are taught what is happening inside the apps they use.

Where an outside course fits

An AI course works well alongside school, not instead of it. School maths (percentages, graphs, a little algebra) is exactly what a first machine learning course needs, and teenagers often find that maths makes more sense once they use it to judge a model.

For students in Spanish-medium schools, the English is a second benefit; for those at bilingual or IB schools, it is simply the language they already study in. Our Spanish-language page curso de IA para adolescentes explains the same courses for parents who prefer to read in Spanish.

There is a teaching reason as well as a legal one. A student who asks a chatbot to write a model never learns why the model fails. A student who writes the twenty lines below, and then breaks them on purpose, learns something no tool can hand them: a sense of when to be suspicious of a number.

A real lesson

How do you know a model is good? A lesson on test-set leakage

Here is a lesson from our teen course. Two models predict whether a student passes a weekly quiz. One of them looks better on a test set that quietly shares examples with its training data. On a clean test set, the verdict flips.

  1. The data. Twelve invented training rows: how many practice problems a student solved that week, on how many days they practised, and whether they passed. Two test sets: a "leaky" one with five of its six rows copied from training, and a "clean" one built from new rows.
  2. Model A, the memoriser. It stores every training example exactly. Asked about an example it has seen, it repeats the answer; asked about anything new, it says "fail".
  3. Model B, the rule. It tries every threshold from 0 to 10 on the number of problems solved and keeps the one that gets most training rows right. It learns: pass if 4 or more problems.

What went wrong

The memoriser scores 1.0 on its own training data, which proves nothing; any lookup table can do that. The leaky test set looks like a fair exam, but five of its six questions were already in the textbook, so the memoriser simply recalls them.

Two of those copied rows are odd cases, a student who solved 7 problems and still failed, another who solved 4 and failed. The rule gets those "wrong" because it learned the general pattern; the memoriser gets them "right" because it remembers the exceptions. A leaky test rewards remembering exceptions.

What the clean test shows

On six new students, the memoriser falls to 0.33: it has never seen any of them, so it answers "fail" every time. The rule, which learned a pattern instead of a list, keeps its 0.83.

Google's guidance says a good test set "contains no duplicates from the training set" and recommends deleting duplicates before testing. The last line of our program, duplicates in leaky test: 5 of 6, is the check that would have caught the problem before anyone believed the score.

  1. Predict first. Before running the code, write down which model you expect to win on each test set, and why.
  2. Break it differently. Change the memoriser so that unseen examples get "pass" instead of "fail". What happens to its clean score, and what does that tell you about guessing?
  3. Wear out the test. Try thresholds by hand and pick the one that scores highest on the clean test set. Explain in two English sentences why that score is now too optimistic.
  4. Write the report. One paragraph, in English, recommending a model to a teacher who will use it. Say what data you tested on and what you are still unsure about.

When it matters

Why does honest testing matter outside the classroom?

Because models are already used to make decisions about people, including students. Peru's 2025 AI regulation treats some of those uses as high risk.

Article 24.1 of DS 115-2025-PCM, the regulation of Peru's AI law published in El Peruano on 9 September 2025, lists uses of AI it considers high risk. One of them is evaluation in the processes of children and adolescents in education, including "evaluación de resultados de aprendizaje", the assessment of learning outcomes, unless the system plays a complementary role "y no reemplace la evaluación pedagógica humana", that is, unless it does not replace a teacher's judgement.

We quote this as context, not as legal advice. Its relevance to a teenager is simple: if a model ever marks your work, predicts your results or flags you during an exam, someone should have tested it on clean data and a human should still be checking. A student who has seen a memoriser win on a leaky test set understands why, and is better placed to ask the right questions.

Questions worth asking of any model

What data was it trained on, and who chose it? Was it tested on people like me? Were any of the test examples also in the training data? Who checks its decisions, and can I ask for a person to look again?

What we ask of our students

Every project ends with a short written note: the data, the test, the score on unseen examples, and one way the model could fail. It takes ten minutes and changes how students think about every number they see afterwards.

Competitions

Can AI help in programming olympiads in Peru?

Not during the contest. UTEC runs the OPI, Peru's computing olympiad, at no cost to any secundaria student in the country, and its 2025 rulebook forbids generative AI for solving the problems.

What the OPI 2025 rules say

Competitors must reason and code alone. The same rules explain that the top four of the second phase were selected for the International Olympiad in Informatics, the IOI, held in Sucre, Bolivia, from 27 July to 3 August 2025, and that Peru has taken part in the IOI since 2022.

Participants had to be at most 20 years old on 1 July 2025. Neither UTEC nor the OPI has any link with us, and we never reproduce its problems; we write our own practice problems and label them as ours.

How an AI course helps anyway

Olympiad problems reward clear thinking about data and edge cases, which is exactly what careful model testing trains. A student who habitually asks "what have I not tested?" writes better competition code as well.

Students who want competition depth can add our problem solving and DSA course, below, alongside the AI course. For a gentler start, our clases de programación para adolescentes page covers coding for teens in general.

In English

Is my English good enough for an AI course in English?

If you can follow a YouTube tutorial in English, yes. Our classes use clear, unhurried English, and the vocabulary of AI (data, model, train, test, accuracy) is the same short list of words used everywhere.

Why English helps

The documentation, error messages and research papers in AI are overwhelmingly in English. A student who learns "overfitting" and "test set" in English can read the original sources the day they need them.

EF's English Proficiency Index 2025 gives Peruvian adults aged 18 to 20 a score of 469, in the "low" band, while those aged 21 to 25 score 540, "moderate". Those are adults who chose to take EF's test, not school students, but the pattern suggests the late teens are a good time to build working English.

How we support it

Mentors repeat instructions in simpler words and write key terms in the chat. Students explain their code aloud in short sentences, and their written project notes are in English too, corrected gently.

We do not teach in Spanish. For a student who finds the first weeks hard, one-to-one classes let the mentor slow down without holding back a group. The free first class will show quickly whether it suits.

AI course for teens: common questions

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. The course is taught live online in English and only in English. Mentors keep their pace steady, rephrase when something is unclear and adjust to each student's accent, so a teenager with school-level English can usually follow and improves quickly.

Does my teenager need to know how to code already?

No. The core course starts with Python from the beginning. Students who already code skip ahead after the trial class. School maths, such as percentages and simple graphs, is all the maths needed to start.

Will my teenager use ChatGPT in the course?

Only with your permission and only where the terms allow. OpenAI's terms require a parent or guardian's permission under 18, and Anthropic's Claude is for adults only, so it is never used by students under 18. Most of the course uses Python itself, with no AI account.

What will my teenager actually build?

Small models trained on datasets they can understand, such as a threshold rule or a decision tree, then projects with real libraries. Every project is tested on examples the model has never seen and written up in a short English report.

At what times are classes held from Peru?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. Peru is on UTC minus 5 all year and India on UTC plus 5:30; 07:00 in Lima, before school, is 17:30 in India.

Does secundaria in Peru teach AI?

Not as a subject. The CNEB has no computing area in secundaria. MINEDU has run initiatives such as the Fábrica de Programadores Python pilot for 1,000 students in Lima Norte (announced July 2025) and an annual Hackathon, but most students meet neither.

Can the course help with the OPI programming olympiad?

It builds the right habits, but the OPI 2025 rules ban generative AI during the contest, so students must solve problems alone. For competition depth we suggest adding our problem solving and DSA course. We are not connected with UTEC or the OPI.

Is there a certificate?

Students who complete a course receive our own certificate of completion. It is not a Peruvian school qualification, and we are not connected with MINEDU, SUNEDU or any school. The projects and reports a student builds are usually the stronger evidence.

What equipment does a student need?

A laptop or desktop computer, a stable internet connection and a headset with a microphone. A phone is fine for watching but not for writing Python. All the software we use in the first months is free.

Can my teenager switch from a group to one-to-one?

Yes. Some students start in a group and move to one-to-one before exams or for a big project, and some do the reverse. Tell us and we arrange it from the next month.
