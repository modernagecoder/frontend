From the curriculum

What MCV4U covers, strand by strand

Calculus and Vectors is described in The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Mathematics (2007). Its description begins: “This course builds on students’ previous experience with functions and their developing understanding of rates of change.” Three strands follow, and the third one, on vectors, is often the one families did not expect.

How the calculus half is framed

Limits appear, but lightly. Students look at “graphical and numerical examples of limits, and explain the reasoning involved”, such as a function approaching an asymptote. The formal epsilon definition belongs to university; MCV4U uses limits to explain where the derivative comes from.

The derivative rules are named precisely: students solve problems “using the product and chain rules”, and a rational function can be handled as a product with a negative exponent. A footnote adds that “The emphasis of this expectation is on the application of the derivative rules and not on the simplification of resulting complex algebraic expressions.” Many students lose time simplifying answers that were already acceptable.

How the vectors half is framed

The strand starts from a definition students can say aloud: “recognize a vector as a quantity with both magnitude and direction”. It then moves into three dimensions, dot and cross products, and finally equations of lines and planes.

One sentence explains why three-space feels different: students learn that “a line in three-space cannot be represented by a scalar equation”. In two dimensions a single equation such as y = 2x + 1 is enough. In three it is not, so lines are written in vector or parametric form, and the final expectation asks for distances “between a point and a plane; between two skew lines”.

Who the course is meant for

The course description is direct about its audience: careers in science, engineering, economics and some business, “including those students who will be required to take a university-level calculus, linear algebra, or physics course.” Vectors are the linear algebra preview; derivatives are the calculus preview.

The programme overview says the same from the university side: the course prepares students for programs “that include a calculus or linear algebra course in the first year”.

The rule about Advanced Functions

MCV4U has no Grade 11 prerequisite of its own. Instead its course page carries a note: “The new Advanced Functions course (MHF4U) must be taken prior to or concurrently with Calculus and Vectors (MCV4U).” The course table in the same document puts it as Advanced Functions “must be taken prior to or concurrently with Calculus and Vectors.”

MCV4U is worth one credit, and the curriculum’s 70 and 30 split applies: most of the grade comes from the term, the rest from a final evaluation near the end. Ask the teacher early whether that final is one exam or an exam plus a task.

Two halves, two skills

The calculus half and the vectors half need different habits

Students who do well on the first half are sometimes surprised by the second, and the reverse also happens. The table below is our own comparison, drawn from what we see in class.

What each half of MCV4U asks of a student (ours)
Calculus half (strands A and B)Vectors half (strand C)
Main questionHow fast is this changing, and where is it largest or smallest?Where is this object in space, and how does it meet another?
Core skillAlgebra with functions: rules applied in order, without slipsSpatial reasoning: picturing directions, normals and intersections
Typical toolA derivative, set equal to zero or checked for signA dot product, a cross product, or a system of equations
Where marks goDomain left out of an optimization; a chain rule step forgottenDot and cross product confused; parallel and skew lines mixed up
Self-checkTest a value on each side of a critical pointSubstitute the answer back into every equation it must satisfy
DrawingA sketch of the function and its derivativeA rough 3D sketch, even an untidy one, before any algebra

Chain rule drift

Students differentiate the outside of a composite function and forget the inside. We make them name the inner function out loud before writing anything.

Optimization without a domain

A box cannot have a negative side. Writing the allowed interval first decides which critical point counts and whether the endpoints matter.

Dot or cross?

One gives a number, the other a vector. Asking “do I want an angle or a perpendicular direction?” settles it every time.

Why the second derivative causes trouble

The curriculum calls the second derivative “the rate of change of the rate of change”, which is accurate and, for many students, hard to feel. Our way in is motion: position, then velocity, then acceleration. A car can be moving forward while slowing down, and that one picture separates the sign of the first derivative from the sign of the second.

Curve sketching then becomes a table: where f prime changes sign, where f double prime changes sign, and what the function does in between. Practice question 8 asks for exactly that.

Why three-space problems feel slippery

In two dimensions, two lines either meet or are parallel. In three, they can also miss each other entirely without being parallel. Students who carry the 2D picture into 3D answer questions about skew lines as though they were impossible.

We use the corner of a room as a model: the floor, two walls, and the edges where they meet. A line along the floor and a line up the opposite wall never meet and are not parallel. Once that is in a student’s head, the algebra has something to describe.

Worked problems, ours

One optimization problem and one problem in three-space

Our own problems, set at the level of an MCV4U test, one from each half of the course. The emphasis is on the decisions: what to name, what to write before differentiating, and how to know the answer is right. SymPy confirmed every number.

Problem 1, optimization. A box with a square base and no lid must hold 32 000 cm3. The base is made from a double layer of cardboard; the four sides are a single layer. What dimensions use the least cardboard?

  1. Name the variables and the quantity to minimise. Let x be the base edge and h the height, both in centimetres. Cardboard used: the base counts twice, so C = 2x2 + 4xh.
  2. Use the constraint to remove one variable. The volume gives x2h = 32 000, so h = 32 000 / x2. Then C(x) = 2x2 + 128 000 / x. Calculus only starts once the quantity depends on one variable.
  3. Write the domain before differentiating. x must be positive, and there is no upper limit, so the domain is x > 0. With no endpoints to check, the answer must come from a critical point.
  4. Differentiate and solve. C′(x) = 4x − 128 000 / x2. Setting this to 0 gives 4x3 = 128 000, so x3 = 32 000 and x is the cube root of 32 000, about 31.75 cm.
  5. Prove it is a minimum. C″(x) = 4 + 256 000 / x3, which is positive for every x > 0, so the graph is concave up everywhere and the critical point is the minimum. A numerical check agrees: C(30) is about 6066.7, C(35) about 6107.1, and the critical value is about 6047.6 cm2.
  6. Answer the question that was asked. h = 32 000 / x2, and since x3 = 32 000, h equals x. The optimal box is a cube of edge about 31.75 cm, using about 6048 cm2 of cardboard.

The surprise is worth a sentence in a written answer: the double base pushes the shape towards a taller box, and with exactly twice the cardboard per square centimetre on the base, the height ends up equal to the edge. A student who writes that has shown they read their own result, which is what the communication marks reward.

Problem 2, a line and a plane. The line L has vector equation r = (1, 2, −1) + t(2, −1, 3). The plane Π has equation 2x + y − z = 7. Where does L meet Π? If it does not, find the distance between them.

  1. Write the line in parametric form. x = 1 + 2t, y = 2 − t, z = −1 + 3t. Each coordinate is now a function of the single parameter t.
  2. Substitute into the plane. 2(1 + 2t) + (2 − t) − (−1 + 3t) = 7. Expanding gives 2 + 4t + 2 − t + 1 − 3t = 7, and the t terms cancel: 5 = 7.
  3. Read what the contradiction means. No value of t works, so the line never meets the plane. The t terms cancelling is the signal: it means the direction of the line is perpendicular to the plane’s normal, so the line runs parallel to the plane.
  4. Confirm with a dot product. The direction is d = (2, −1, 3) and the normal is n = (2, 1, −1). d · n = 4 − 1 − 3 = 0, so d is perpendicular to n, as step 3 said.
  5. Find the distance. Every point of the line is the same distance from the plane, so use the point (1, 2, −1). Distance = |2(1) + 1(2) − 1(−1) − 7| divided by the length of n, which is the square root of 6. That is |−2| / √6 = 2/√6, about 0.816 units.
The three things a substitution like step 2 can tell you (ours)
After substituting, you getIt meansExample result
One value of tThe line crosses the plane at one pointPut t back into the line to get the point
A false statement, such as 5 = 7The line is parallel to the plane and never meets itFind the distance from any point on the line
A true statement, such as 7 = 7Every point of the line is in the planeThe line lies in the plane

What the two problems share

Both start by reducing the number of unknowns. In the box, the volume removes h. In the line, the parametric form turns three coordinates into one parameter. MCV4U questions that look long usually become short once that reduction is made.

Both also end with a check that uses a different method: a numerical comparison in the first, a dot product in the second. A second method catches errors that rereading the first method never will.

How a marker reads these

Ontario achievement charts give marks for knowledge, thinking, communication and application. In problem 1 the domain sentence and the concavity argument are thinking and communication marks; the derivative itself is knowledge. Students who show only the derivative leave most of the marks on the table.

In problem 2 the sentence that interprets 5 = 7 matters more than the arithmetic. A student who writes “no solution” and stops has answered half the question.

Practice, ours

Twelve MCV4U practice questions

Our own set, written for this page: nine on the calculus half and three on vectors, in the order a course usually teaches them. None is copied from a textbook, school or board. Answers with reasons are in the next section.

  1. Limits. Find the limit of (√(4 + h) − 2) / h as h approaches 0.
  2. Power rule. Differentiate f(x) = 3x4 − 5x2 + 7x − 2.
  3. Chain rule. Differentiate y = (2x + 1)5.
  4. Product rule. Differentiate y = x2 sin x.
  5. A rational function. Differentiate f(x) = (x2 + 1)/(x − 1) by writing it as a product.
  6. Exponential and trigonometric. Differentiate y = e3x cos x.
  7. Tangent line. Find the equation of the tangent to f(x) = x3 − 3x2 − 9x + 5 at x = 1.
  8. Curve sketching. For the same f, find the local maximum, the local minimum and the point of inflection.
  9. Motion. A particle moves along a line with position s(t) = t3 − 6t2 + 9t metres at t seconds. When is it at rest, and what is its acceleration at t = 2?
  10. Dot product. For u = (3, −1, 2) and v = (1, 4, −2), find u · v and the angle between the vectors to the nearest degree.
  11. Cross product. Find u × v for the same vectors, and the area of the parallelogram they span.
  12. Equation of a plane. Find the scalar equation of the plane through A(1, 0, 2), B(3, 1, 0) and C(0, 2, 1).

Answers, ours

Answers and the reasoning behind them

Each answer gives the result and the one idea that gets you there. All twelve were checked with SymPy.

  1. 1/4. Substituting h = 0 gives 0/0, so rationalise: multiply top and bottom by (√(4 + h) + 2). The top becomes h, which cancels, leaving 1/(√(4 + h) + 2), which approaches 1/4.
  2. f′(x) = 12x3 − 10x + 7. Apply the power rule term by term; the constant disappears.
  3. y′ = 10(2x + 1)4. Outside: 5( )4. Inside: the derivative of 2x + 1 is 2. Multiply: 5 × 2 = 10.
  4. y′ = 2x sin x + x2 cos x. First times the derivative of the second, plus second times the derivative of the first.
  5. f′(x) = (x2 − 2x − 1)/(x − 1)2. Write f = (x2 + 1)(x − 1)−1. The product rule gives 2x(x − 1)−1 − (x2 + 1)(x − 1)−2, which combines over (x − 1)2. Leaving it uncombined is also correct, as the curriculum footnote says.
  6. y′ = 3e3x cos x − e3x sin x. Product rule, with the chain rule inside the exponential: the derivative of e3x is 3e3x.
  7. y = −12x + 6. f(1) = 1 − 3 − 9 + 5 = −6, and f′(x) = 3x2 − 6x − 9, so f′(1) = −12. The line through (1, −6) with slope −12 is y + 6 = −12(x − 1).
  8. Local maximum (−1, 10), local minimum (3, −22), inflection point (1, −6). f′(x) = 3(x − 3)(x + 1) is zero at −1 and 3 and changes from positive to negative at −1. f″(x) = 6x − 6 changes sign at x = 1. The inflection point is also the tangent point from question 7.
  9. At rest at t = 1 s and t = 3 s; acceleration 0 m/s2 at t = 2. v(t) = 3t2 − 12t + 9 = 3(t − 1)(t − 3). a(t) = 6t − 12, so a(2) = 0. From t = 1 to t = 2, velocity and acceleration are both negative, so the particle speeds up while moving backwards; at t = 2 its speed peaks at 3 m/s, and from t = 2 to t = 3 it slows down until it stops.
  10. u · v = −5; the angle is about 107°. |u| = √14 and |v| = √21, so cos θ = −5/(√14 × √21). A negative dot product always means an angle greater than 90°.
  11. u × v = (−6, 8, 13); area √269, about 16.40 square units. The magnitude of the cross product is the area of the parallelogram. Check: (−6, 8, 13) · u = −18 − 8 + 26 = 0, so the result is perpendicular to u, as it must be.
  12. 3x + 4y + 5z = 13. AB = (2, 1, −2) and AC = (−1, 2, −1); their cross product (3, 4, 5) is a normal. Substituting A gives 3 + 0 + 10 = 13. Check B: 9 + 4 + 0 = 13. Check C: 0 + 8 + 5 = 13.

Final exam, ours

Preparing for the MCV4U final exam

Our plan for the last five weeks. Because the course has two halves with different habits, the plan alternates them rather than revising in textbook order, so neither half goes cold.

Question types on a typical MCV4U final, and what a complete answer contains (ours)
Question typePractise itA full answer includes
Derivative rulesDaily, ten short itemsThe rule named, the inner function identified, no forced simplification
Curve sketchingTwice a weekA sign table for f′ and f″, the key points, a sketch that matches the table
OptimizationTwice a weekVariables named, the constraint used, the domain stated, a test that it is a max or min
Rates and motionOnce a weekUnits on every answer, and a sentence on what the sign means
Vector operationsThree times a weekThe right product chosen, with a check such as a dot product of 0
Lines and planesThree times a weekA rough sketch, the equations written in one form, the result interpreted geometrically
  1. Week five: find the leaks. Go through every test of the term and tally lost marks by row of the table above. That tally, not a feeling, decides where the time goes.
  2. Weeks four and three: alternate halves. One calculus day, one vectors day. Start each session with five minutes of derivative drills so the rules stay automatic.
  3. Build a one-page formula sheet by hand. Derivative rules, the dot and cross product formulas, the distance from a point to a plane, and the three outcomes of substituting a line into a plane.
  4. Week two: one full paper under time. Mark it strictly, then redo every question that lost marks on a blank page the next day.
  5. Week one: a second full paper, then only the error list. Read the list of your own mistakes every day. Stop learning new methods.
  6. On the day: answer the question that was asked. If it asks for dimensions, give dimensions with units, not just x.

Reading these requirements correctly

A listing of MCV4U alone still implies Advanced Functions, because the curriculum will not let a student take Calculus and Vectors without MHF4U before or alongside it. When a page lists both, it is being explicit rather than asking for more.

Software Engineering adds two sciences and a 70% minimum on each listed course. That floor sits on top of the program average, so a strong average does not rescue a weak Calculus and Vectors mark.

What we can and cannot do

We can make the course understood: the derivative rules automatic, the optimization method reliable, the three-dimensional picture clear. We cannot promise a mark or an offer, and we do not. Admission decisions belong to the universities.

Requirements are revised between application cycles. Check the university’s page in the autumn of Grade 12, and treat any table on a tutoring site, ours included, as a starting point.

Questions from Ontario families

MCV4U: frequently asked questions

What is MCV4U?

MCV4U is Calculus and Vectors, the Grade 12 university-preparation course set out in Ontario's 2007 senior mathematics curriculum. Its three strands are rate of change, derivatives and their applications, and the geometry and algebra of vectors, including lines and planes in three-dimensional space.

Do you grant the MCV4U credit?

No. We are a tutoring service working alongside the student's school course, and we do not grant Ontario credits. The credit is granted by the school that teaches and evaluates MCV4U, such as a day school, a board summer program or a credit-granting private school.

Do I need MHF4U before MCV4U?

You need it before or at the same time. The 2007 curriculum lets students take Advanced Functions first or in the same term as Calculus and Vectors, but not after. Doing both at once is allowed, but MCV4U uses Advanced Functions ideas such as logarithms, rational functions and radians from its early units.

Is MCV4U harder than MHF4U?

Many students find it harder because it has two different halves. The calculus half rewards careful algebra, and the vectors half rewards spatial reasoning. A student strong in one can struggle in the other, which is why we assess the two halves separately.

Does MCV4U teach integration?

The 2007 Ontario course is about rates of change and derivatives, plus vectors. Its three strands do not include integration, which most students meet in first-year university calculus. Our calculus course covers integrals for students who want a head start.

Which programs require MCV4U?

Many engineering, mathematics, science, economics and business programs list it. On 22 October 2026, Waterloo Software Engineering listed Calculus and Vectors with a 70% minimum, and the University of Toronto Mathematics listings for St. George and Scarborough both named MCV4U.

How should a student prepare for the MCV4U exam?

Start about five weeks before. Tally the lost marks from every term test, alternate calculus days and vectors days, keep derivative rules automatic with short daily drills, and end with two papers sat under exam timing, followed by work on the error list only.

Can you help with the vectors unit only?

Yes. One-to-one classes can focus on a single unit, such as dot and cross products or lines and planes, for as long as it takes. Batch classes follow the course order, so a student joining mid-semester picks up wherever the batch is.

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Times are set in Eastern time, and we confirm them again when the clocks change on 1 November 2026 and 14 March 2027.
