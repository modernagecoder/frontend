## From "level 6" to "excluding Year 6": a clearer target

The change in wording between the two cycles is small on the page and large for families.

### Last cycle

Poole Grammar School described the GL maths paper as covering "most aspects of Key Stage 2 up to level 5, with some questions aimed at level 6". National curriculum levels were withdrawn from primary assessment some years ago, and "level 6" was always a loose description. Families understandably read it as "some secondary maths", and preparation drifted into Year 6 and beyond.

For a test sat in the September of Year 6, that drift cost time that Year 5 topics needed.

### Next cycle

For 2028 entry, Bournemouth School describes the maths as Key Stage 2 "but excluding work covered in Year 6", and says no content beyond the Year 5 curriculum will be tested. That is a definite ceiling: everything in the Year 5 programme of study, and nothing after it.

Paired with a forty-minute paper instead of fifty, it means fewer minutes on narrower content. The way to use that is depth: every Year 5 topic secure and quick, including the ones classrooms rush.

Maths also moves to the front. Quest lists it as Paper One, the first of three in the session. A child starts the morning with maths, fresh, rather than meeting it after an hour of English and reasoning. We practise cold starts so the first questions of the day are as accurate as any.

## Regular and irregular polygons, by reasoning

The Year 5 curriculum asks children to "distinguish between regular and irregular polygons based on reasoning about equal sides and angles". It is a small statement with plenty of room for good multiple-choice questions.

**Year 5 polygons, with questions of our own**

| Idea | Our question | Answer and reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| What regular means | A shape has all sides equal and all angles equal. What is it called? | A regular polygon: both conditions are needed |
| Equal sides only | A rhombus has four equal sides. Is it regular? | Not unless its angles are also equal, which makes it a square |
| Equal angles only | A rectangle has four equal angles. Is it regular? | Only if its sides are equal too: then it is a square |
| Perimeter from one side | A regular hexagon has sides of 7 cm. What is its perimeter? | 42 cm: six equal sides |
| Working backwards | A regular polygon has perimeter 48 cm and sides of 6 cm. How many sides? | 8: it is a regular octagon |
| Irregular | A triangle has sides of 5, 5 and 8 cm. Regular or irregular? | Irregular: not all sides are equal |

The key word in the curriculum is "reasoning". A child should not recognise a regular shape by how it looks, since a drawing on a test page may not be accurate, but by what is marked or stated: equal sides, equal angles, or both. Regular needs both.

The rhombus and rectangle rows are the heart of it. Each has one kind of equality but not the other, so each is irregular, except in the special case where it becomes a square. Children who understand those two examples can handle any polygon question.

The perimeter rows connect shape to measurement, which Year 5 also covers. Once a polygon is known to be regular, one side length gives the whole perimeter, and the perimeter plus a side gives the number of sides. On a forty-minute paper, those are quick marks for a child who knows what regular means.

On multiple-choice questions, the wrong options usually come from seeing rather than reasoning: calling a rhombus regular because it looks neat, or an irregular pentagon regular because it is drawn nearly symmetrical. We train the habit of checking the labels before trusting the picture.

Source: [national curriculum in England, mathematics](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study), Year 5 geometry, properties of shapes (statutory requirements). The questions are our own.
