## Is the trip home just the trip out, reversed?

The Fremantle line as two lists in OpenStreetMap, one for each direction, compared position by position after flipping one of them.

**What we found on 7 October 2026 (our comparison of OpenStreetMap route data)**

| Check | Result |
|---|---|
| Stations listed from Perth to Fremantle | 17 |
| Stations listed from Fremantle to Perth | 17 |
| Positions that match after reversing the outbound list | 16 of 17 |
| The mismatch | Cottesloe going out, but Cottlesloe on the way home |
| A second slip in the same data | The return route is named Fremante instead of Fremantle |
| First station out, last station home | Perth, both times |
| The outbound list | Perth, City West, West Leederville, Subiaco, Daglish, Shenton Park, Karrakatta, Loch Street, Showgrounds, Claremont, Swanbourne, Grant Street, Cottesloe, Mosman Park, Victoria Street, North Fremantle, Fremantle |

### 1. Reverse the list

In Scratch, a loop reads the outbound list from the last item to the first. In Python, one short slice does the same job. Either way, Fremantle moves to the front and Perth to the back.

### 2. Compare position by position

Item 1 against item 1, item 2 against item 2, and so on. A loop counts how many pairs are identical and prints any pair that is not.

### 3. Read the odd one out

Position 4 shows Mosman Park against Mosman Park, then position 5 shows Cottesloe against Cottlesloe: the same station, with an extra letter in one list.

### Why the computer was right to complain

To a person, Cottesloe and Cottlesloe look like the same word. To a program they are different strings, so the comparison fails. That is not the code being fussy; it is the code being honest. A list of names typed by people almost always contains a slip or two, and a reverse-and-compare check is one of the cheapest ways to find them.

These comparisons are ours, made on an OpenStreetMap snapshot taken on 7 October 2026. OpenStreetMap is edited by volunteers, so the spelling slips we found may already have been corrected by the time you read this. We report only what the two route lists said that day and make no claim about timetables or which trains stop where.

## Backwards and forwards at every age

Reversing an order is one of the first ideas a young child can act out and one of the handiest a programmer uses.

**Reversing and comparing, by year level**

| Year level | What children try | What it builds |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-primary to Year 2 | Line up toy carriages for the trip out, then rebuild the line for the trip home | Order, sequence and undoing steps |
| Years 3 to 6 | Read a Scratch list from the end with a counter that goes down instead of up | Lists, indexes and loops that count backwards |
| Years 7 and 8 | Reverse and compare the two Fremantle lists in Python, printing every mismatch | Slicing, comparing strings and testing real data |

### Off by one

Counting down from 17 to 1 or from 16 to 0? Children meet the most common bug in programming, the one-step slip at the end of a list, and learn to check the first and last items every time.

### Same length first

Before comparing two lists, check they are the same length. If one had 18 stations, a position-by-position check would shift every name after the extra one.

### Report, do not fix

The program prints the mismatch rather than quietly changing a spelling. Deciding which spelling is correct is a job for a person who checks a source.

## Would an AI tool have spotted Cottlesloe for a Perth child?

Maybe, maybe not, and that uncertainty is exactly why the child needs to be able to check for themselves.

AI tools are very good at reading past small spelling slips, which is helpful when you are chatting and unhelpful when you are checking data. A tool asked to compare the two station lists might decide Cottesloe and Cottlesloe are obviously the same and report a perfect match. The five-line program our children write cannot be that generous: different letters mean different strings, so it flags the slip every time.

We want young Perth learners to know when to want a strict check and when a friendly guess is fine. In our lessons AI tools are used only in age-appropriate ways and with a teacher present, and children practise asking whether an answer was checked or simply assumed.

That is why coding is still worth learning in 2026: it gives a child a way to check, not just a way to ask. A Perth child who found Cottlesloe by reversing a list already knows the difference. The longer argument is in [the case for learning to code in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).

## Questions from Perth parents

### What age can my Perth child start coding?

Live classes start at age 6, which in Perth is usually Pre-primary or Year 1. The first lessons use blocks and pictures, with typing added later.

### What does reversing a list mean in coding?

Reversing a list means putting its items in the opposite order, so the last becomes first. Programs reverse lists to undo steps, to read things from the end, or, as in our Perth project, to check a return journey against the outward one.

### What is the Fremantle line project?

Children take the 17 Fremantle line stations stored in OpenStreetMap for each direction, reverse the outbound list and compare it with the return list. Sixteen positions match; the seventeenth reveals a spelling slip, Cottlesloe for Cottesloe.

### Is a spelling mistake really a coding lesson?

Yes. Real data is typed by people and contains slips. Learning to compare lists exactly, print what differs and leave the decision to a person who checks is a skill used by every data-minded programmer.
