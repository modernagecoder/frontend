Online maths summer school in the UK

The UK's best known maths summer school, the UKMT National Mathematics Summer School, is a free week away that students cannot apply for: invitations go by lottery to a small share of top scorers in the Intermediate Mathematical Challenge. Modern Age Coders is not connected with UKMT. Over the summer we teach the same kind of maths, number theory, combinatorics, graphs, induction and proof writing, live online through our olympiad and competition course, for pupils aged 11 to 17, as a group or 1-on-1. This page explains the UKMT summer school from UKMT's own pages, lists other programmes, and lays out a five-day online summer plan with worked examples.

What is the UKMT maths summer school, and how do students get in?

The National Mathematics Summer School is a one-week residential event run by the UK Mathematics Trust for students aged 14 to 16. Nobody applies: UKMT sends invitations through schools in March to students chosen at random from the top 1.5 per cent of Intermediate Mathematical Challenge results, in Years 10 to 11 in England and Wales, S3 to S4 in Scotland and Years 11 to 12 in Northern Ireland, with only one student per school. UKMT says it usually runs three events a year from the start of August. Modern Age Coders has no link with UKMT; we teach summer school topics live online on our olympiad course to any pupil aged 11 to 17, in groups of 15 to 20 or 1-on-1.

Seniors: going back a second time

UKMT invites past Juniors to apply to return as Seniors, with "one Senior per group of six Juniors". Volunteers highlight suitable students, and Senior applications "usually go out in December".

Seniors get more specialised sessions that push beyond the secondary curriculum into university topics.

If the school does not enter the IMC

UKMT's answer is that "entering IMC as a visiting student may be possible" at another local school, which is under no obligation to agree.

For pupils whose school does enter, the first job each year is simply to sit the Intermediate Challenge, which our intermediate olympiad page covers.

Who is invited

How many students get a UKMT summer school place?

Very few, and luck plays a large part. UKMT publishes its own 2024 figures, and they explain why a high scorer can miss out.

  1. UKMT starts with all Intermediate Mathematical Challenge results: "somewhere around 226,000 in 2024".
  2. It takes the top 1.5 per cent of scorers in the eligible years.
  3. It selects one student at random from each school, which in 2024 gave 843 students, leaving out schools that sent someone the previous year.
  4. A second lottery chooses the final invitations: "108 Juniors in 2024".
  5. If a student declines, the place goes by lottery to another school and student.

Our arithmetic on those figures

108 invitations against about 226,000 results is roughly one invitation for every 2,100 results, by our calculation. Even among the 843 shortlisted after the one-per-school step, about one in eight was invited.

UKMT is open about why: it wants wider access, so it spreads places across schools rather than taking the top scores.

What that means for a family

A summer school invitation is a lovely surprise, not a plan. A strong scorer from a school with several strong scorers may never be the one drawn.

If your child loves the kind of maths on that topic list, it makes sense to find a way to do it every summer, invited or not.

A five-day plan

What could an online maths summer school week cover?

Here is a five-day plan we wrote around topics on UKMT's own list, with one worked example a day. It is our programme, not UKMT's, and it suits ages 13 to 17; younger pupils take each day more slowly.

Our five-day online summer plan
DayTopicThe ideaOne problem (ours)
1Combinatorics: the pigeonhole principleMore objects than boxes forces a shared boxPick any six numbers from 1 to 10; show two of them add to 11
2Logic: invariantsFind what never changesThe difference game on 1 to 10, below
3Graph theoryCount from both endsShow the number of people with an odd handshake count is even
4Proof by inductionOne step, then every stepShow 2 to the power n exceeds n squared from n = 5 onwards
5Writing mathsA solution is for a readerRewrite a correct but muddled solution so a stranger can follow it

Day 1 and Day 2, worked

Pigeonhole: split 1 to 10 into the five pairs 1 and 10, 2 and 9, 3 and 8, 4 and 7, 5 and 6. Each pair adds to 11. Six numbers in five pairs means two land in the same pair, and those two add to 11.

Invariant: write 1 to 10 on a board. Repeatedly rub out two numbers and write their difference. Can the last number be 0? The sum starts at 55, which is odd. Replacing a and b by their difference changes the sum by an even amount, so the sum stays odd, and the last number is odd. It can never be 0.

Day 3 and Day 4, worked

Graphs: add up everyone's handshake count. Each handshake is counted twice, once by each person, so the total is even. A sum of whole numbers is even only if it contains an even number of odd terms. So an even number of people shook an odd number of hands.

Induction: at n = 5, 32 beats 25. If 2 to the n exceeds n squared, then 2 to the n plus 1 exceeds 2n squared, and 2n squared minus (n plus 1) squared equals n squared minus 2n minus 1, which is positive for n of 3 or more. So the claim passes from each n to the next.

Day 5 matters most. UKMT lists Writing Maths as a session of its own, and olympiad markers reward a clear argument over a bare answer. On our Day 5 each pupil takes one of their own solutions from the week, rewrites it for a reader who was not in the room, and has it read and returned by a teacher. A pupil who does that once a week for a summer writes very differently by September.

The plan, the problems and the worked solutions are ours. The topic names in the second column echo UKMT's published topic list.

How should a keen pupil spend a maths summer?

Our suggestion, not a rule: a little maths often, one long problem a week, and something written down. Holidays should still feel like holidays.

If an invitation does come

Go. A residential week with other keen students and volunteers who love maths is something online lessons cannot copy, and UKMT's pages describe evening activities, a quiet room and pastoral leads.

Summer school questions

What families ask about maths summer schools

What is the UKMT summer school?

The National Mathematics Summer School, a one-week residential event run by the UK Mathematics Trust for students aged 14 to 16. UKMT says it runs three events a year, Sunday to Friday, usually from the start of August.

How do you get into the UKMT summer school?

By invitation only. UKMT chooses students at random from the top 1.5 per cent of Intermediate Mathematical Challenge results in the eligible years, one per school, and sends invitations through schools in March.

Can I apply for the UKMT national maths summer school?

No. There is no application for Juniors; the selection is by lottery from IMC results. Past attendees can later apply to return as Seniors, with applications usually going out in December.

How many students go to UKMT summer school?

UKMT says it invited 108 Juniors in 2024, chosen from 843 students shortlisted one per school from the top 1.5 per cent of about 226,000 IMC results. By our arithmetic that is roughly one invitation per 2,100 results.

What do students do at the UKMT summer school?

Maths sessions on topics such as number theory, geometry, combinatorics, graph theory, proof by induction and writing maths, plus group problem solving and evening activities. Volunteers, often maths teachers, lead the sessions.

Are there other maths summer schools in the UK?

Yes. PROMYS Europe, a partnership including the University of Oxford's Mathematical Institute, runs a six-week number theory programme for students aged 16 and over living in Europe, and King's College London Mathematics School runs free summer schools including a five-day programme for Year 11.

Is there an online maths summer school for UK students?

We teach summer school topics live online for ages 11 to 17, open to anyone and not by invitation. It is our own programme, not a UKMT event, and it can continue into term if a pupil enjoys it.

My child is 12. Is there anything for them?

The UKMT summer school is for ages 14 to 16, so not yet. A summer of puzzles and junior competition maths builds the habits a later Intermediate Challenge score needs, and that is what our junior groups do.

Are you linked to UKMT?

No. We describe UKMT's summer school from its own pages so families understand it. We are not connected with UKMT, PROMYS Europe, the University of Oxford or King's.

When do summer lessons run?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Holiday times are agreed in UK time.
