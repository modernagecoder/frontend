Coding curriculum by province in Canada

Canada has no national school curriculum. Each province and territory writes or adopts its own, so "does my child learn coding at school?" has thirteen different answers. This page gives all thirteen in one table, taken from each government's own curriculum pages as they read on 22 October 2026: the ministry or department, where coding or computer science appears, and one fact we checked word for word. Several government sites were offline or blocked our requests that day. For those rows we say "not verified" and leave the cell empty of guesses, because a wrong course name is worse than a missing one.

Ontario

Ontario: coding from Grade 1 math to Grade 12 computer science

Ontario has the most detailed published coding expectations in the country. Its 2020 elementary math curriculum puts coding inside the algebra strand for every grade, and each grade adds one idea to the last. The table quotes the expectation C3.1 as the ministry's curriculum service returns it.

What Ontario's C3.1 coding expectation adds in each grade (Mathematics, 2020), quoted from the Ontario curriculum service, read 22 October 2026
GradeC3.1 asks for code that involvesIn plain words (ours)
1"sequential events"Steps that run one after another
2"sequential and concurrent events"Two things happening at the same time
3"sequential, concurrent, and repeating events"Loops
4"sequential, concurrent, repeating, and nested events"A loop inside a loop
5"conditional statements and other control structures"If this, then that
6"efficient code" with "conditional statements and other control structures"The same result in fewer steps
7"events influenced by a defined count and/or subprogram and other control structures"Counted loops and reusable pieces
8"the analysis of data in order to inform and communicate decisions"Code that answers a question from data

Grade 9 and Grade 10

The de-streamed Grade 9 course MTH1W (issued 2021) keeps coding in math. Its course description says "Students will use mathematical processes, mathematical modelling, and coding to make sense of the mathematics they are learning", and expectation C2.3 asks students to "read code to predict its outcome, and alter code to adjust constraints, parameters, and outcomes".

In Grade 10 the current list adds ICD2O, Digital Technology and Innovations in the Changing World, issued in 2023 as an open course. Its description has students "investigate artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, and other emerging digital technologies", and its strands are "Computational Thinking and Making Connections", "Hardware, Software, and Innovations" and "Programming".

Grades 11 and 12

The senior courses date from 2008: ICS3U and ICS3C in Grade 11, ICS4U and ICS4C in Grade 12, university and college preparation respectively. ICS4U needs ICS3U first; ICS3U itself has no prerequisite. Our ICS3U help page takes that course strand by strand.

Two facts sit side by side and we do not join them: the 2008 curriculum PDF describes a Grade 10 course ICS2O, and the current course list on the ministry's site does not include it. Neither source says one course replaced the other.

Science has coding too. The 2022 Science and Technology curriculum asks Grade 6 students to "use coding in investigations and to model concepts, and assess the impact of coding and of emerging technologies on everyday life". And at graduation, computer studies is one of the subjects in the STEM group of the Ontario diploma's compulsory credits: Ontario.ca says "Of the 17 compulsory credits, you must complete 1 from the following group", and computer studies is on that list alongside business studies and cooperative education, among others.

Sources: Ontario Ministry of Education curriculum service (elementary math by grade, Science and Technology Grade 6, MTH1W, the computer studies course list) and Ontario.ca graduation requirements, read 22 October 2026.

British Columbia and the NWT

BC teaches coding through design and technology, and the NWT is adopting it

British Columbia files most of its coding under Applied Design, Skills, and Technologies (ADST), a subject of its own, and keeps a second, separate pair of computer science courses under Mathematics. The Northwest Territories has chosen to adopt and adapt the BC curriculum.

Two routes to programming in BC high schools

A BC student can meet programming in ADST (Computer Programming 11 and 12) or in Mathematics (Computer Science 11 and 12). They are different courses in different subject areas on the curriculum site, and a school may offer one, both or neither.

For families, the useful question to ask the school is which of the two it runs, and in which language. The curriculum describes concepts, not a required programming language.

The Northwest Territories

ECE, the territorial department, says "Curriculum in the Northwest Territories is managed by the Department of Education, Culture and Employment (ECE)", and that it is "partnering with the British Columbia Ministry of Education to start using that province's curriculum", with every subject reviewed and adapted where needed.

Its reasons include that BC's curriculum "teaches students the ability to design, make, acquire, and apply skills and technologies". ECE also records that 2022-23 was the "final year that NWT students take the Alberta Achievement Tests (AATs)".

Sources: BC ADST and BC Mathematics curriculum pages; BC Gov News, Education and Child Care; NWT JK-12 Curriculum Renewal; all read 22 October 2026. For BC families, our British Columbia page covers classes in Pacific time.

Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba

The Prairies: renewal in Alberta, named courses in Saskatchewan and Manitoba

The three Prairie provinces are at different points. Alberta is rewriting its curriculum grade band by grade band; Saskatchewan and Manitoba each publish computer science courses for the senior grades.

Alberta

Alberta Education and Childcare's curriculum history page says "By integrating topics like artificial intelligence, computer science and money management, Alberta's renewed curriculum will help students prepare", for the draft Grades 7 to 9 subjects, which schools may pilot in 2026/27 "ahead of mandatory implementation in September 2027". The page files the new K to 6 math under 2022/2023. We could not verify Alberta's high school computing courses from a primary source.

Saskatchewan

The Ministry of Education's curriculum site lists Computer Science 20 and Computer Science 30 as provincial curricula. On the Computer Science 20 page, the ministry notes that "teachers developed, adapted or identified resources/materials for Computer Science 20 and other science curricula", which places the course in the science family.

Manitoba

Manitoba Education and Early Childhood Learning publishes a Computer Science framework for "Senior 2 (20S), Senior 3 (30S), and Senior 4 (40S) Computer Science". The department also posted, on 14 September 2026, that two new Grade 9 math courses are in pilot for 2026/27, "with full implementation planned for school year 2027/28".

The course numbers follow each province's own system. Manitoba's framework spells its own labels out: Senior 2 is 20S, Senior 3 is 30S and Senior 4 is 40S. The Saskatchewan pages we read give course names without a grade beside them. We print the names exactly as the ministries do and leave the grade mapping to each school's course calendar, which is the document a student actually registers from.

Sources: Alberta, curriculum: how we got here; Saskatchewan Computer Science 20; Manitoba Computer Science framework; all read 22 October 2026. Alberta families: see our Alberta page.

Quebec and the Atlantic provinces

Quebec, New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, PEI and Newfoundland and Labrador

East of Ontario the published picture is thinner, partly because of the curricula and partly because several government sites could not be read on the day.

Quebec

The Québec Education Program at secondary level, Québec.ca says, "comprises programs of study for students from Secondary I to Secondary V". Its subject list, read on 22 October 2026, groups mathematics with science and technology courses and names no stand-alone computer science course.

Programming appears formally at college. Québec.ca's list of pre-university programs includes "Computer Science and Mathematics (200.C0)", and its technical programs include "420.B0 - Computer Science Technology". Both are CEGEP programs that follow secondary school.

New Brunswick and Nova Scotia

New Brunswick's curriculum site lists Computer Science 110, Computer Science 120, Cybersecurity 120, Cybersecurity and Technical Support 110 and Technology 9. The Computer Science 110 page also mentions AI-assisted debugging tools alongside coding environments, one of the few provincial pages we read that does.

Nova Scotia's Virtual School describes Computer Programming 12 by its "Modules: Problem Solving in Computer Programming, Fundamentals of Programming, Applied Problem Solving, Project Development." and lists it on CodeHS in Java. The province's own curriculum site did not connect, so we cannot say how widely schools offer it.

Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland and Labrador: not verified. On 21 October 2026 the PEI government site showed an offline notice, and on 22 October it put up a bot check, so we stopped rather than work around it. Newfoundland and Labrador's government site returned server errors on both days. We print nothing about either province's coding courses until a primary page can be read.

Sources: Québec Education Program, secondary and pre-university programs; New Brunswick Computer Science 110; Nova Scotia Virtual School, Computer Programming 12; all read 22 October 2026.

A worked example

One task, two programs: what "efficient code" means in practice

Ontario's Grade 6 expectation asks students to "describe how changes to the code affect the outcomes and the efficiency of the code", and BC's Computer Programming 11 names decomposition, patterns, abstraction and algorithmic thinking. This example is ours, written for this page, and shows both ideas on a very Canadian data set: days that thaw by afternoon and freeze again overnight.

The task (ours). Given ten days of high and low temperatures, count the freeze-thaw days (high above zero, low below zero), then find the longest run of them in a row. We solve the run question twice, once slowly and once efficiently, and count how many checks each version makes.

Where the four ideas show up

Decomposition: the job is split into three functions, one to judge a day, one to mark every day, one to find the run. Pattern: the same test runs on every day. Abstraction: is_freeze_thaw hides the rule, so changing "below zero" to "below minus two" touches one line.

Algorithm: the fast version keeps a running count and resets it on a non-freeze-thaw day, so it looks at each day once. That one idea is the difference between 7260 checks and 120.

How a student should study it

Predict the output before running anything. Then change one value, say the high on day 5 from 0 to 1, and predict again. Day 5 becomes a freeze-thaw day, days 3 to 6 join into one run, and the longest run jumps from 2 to 4 (we ran it: the slow version then makes 22 checks, the fast one still 10).

That habit, predicting and then checking, is close to what Ontario's MTH1W calls reading code "to predict its outcome", and it is how we teach in every class: think it through first, then let the computer confirm or correct you.

For parents

How to find out what your child's school actually teaches

A provincial curriculum says what may be taught. A school's course calendar says what is. Four steps, in the order we suggest them to families.

  1. Find your row above and note the course names your province uses, exactly as written. Searching a school calendar for "coding" often misses a course called Computer Studies 10 or Computer Science 110.
  2. Open the school's course calendar for 2026 to 2027 and look for those names, then check which grade they open in and what they require first. In Ontario, for example, ICS4U requires ICS3U.
  3. Ask which language the class uses. Provincial pages describe concepts; schools choose Python, Java, JavaScript or a block language. Learning the same language at home saves a student weeks.
  4. Match outside learning to the school's timing. A student who meets loops at home a month before the class does will find the course calmer. One who learns something unrelated may simply be busier.

Questions from Canadian families

Coding in Canadian schools: common questions

Is coding part of the school curriculum in Canada?

It depends on the province or territory, because each sets its own curriculum. Ontario includes coding in math for every grade from 1 to 8; BC teaches it through Applied Design, Skills, and Technologies and Computer Science 11 and 12; Saskatchewan and Manitoba publish computer science courses for the senior grades.

Which Canadian province teaches coding the earliest?

Among the curricula we could read, Ontario's is the earliest in writing: its 2020 elementary math curriculum includes a coding expectation from Grade 1, starting with code that involves sequential events.

What computer science courses does Ontario offer in high school?

The current Ontario course list shows ICD2O in Grade 10, ICS3U and ICS3C in Grade 11, and ICS4U and ICS4C in Grade 12. ICS4U requires ICS3U. Schools choose which of these they run.

Does BC have computer science in high school?

Yes. BC lists Computer Programming 11 and 12, Computer Information Systems 11 and 12 and Robotics 11 and 12 under Applied Design, Skills, and Technologies, plus Computer Science 11 and 12 under Mathematics. Computer Studies 10 and Web Development 10 come earlier.

Does Alberta teach coding in school?

Alberta's draft Grades 7 to 9 curriculum integrates computer science and artificial intelligence, with mandatory implementation planned for September 2027. We could not verify Alberta's current high school computing courses from a primary source on 22 October 2026.

Is there a computer science course in Quebec high schools?

The Québec Education Program subject list for Secondary I to V names no stand-alone computer science course. Computer science appears as a college program after secondary school, for example Computer Science and Mathematics (200.C0) at CEGEP.

Why do some rows say not verified?

On 22 October 2026 the government sites for Prince Edward Island, Newfoundland and Labrador, Yukon and Nunavut were offline, returned errors or refused automated requests. We stopped rather than work around a block, and we print nothing we could not read at the source.

Can online classes count for school credit?

Not ours. We teach alongside the school course and do not grant Ontario credits or any other provincial credit. Credits come from the school, under each province's rules.

What language should my child learn to match their school?

Ask the teacher which language the class uses. Provincial curricula describe concepts rather than one language. Python is a common first typed language; Nova Scotia's virtual school course runs in Java.

When are your classes for families across Canada?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. We confirm the time in your own province's clock.

Do you teach students to use AI to do their coding homework?

No. We teach students to reason a program through and then check it. AI tools can explain an error, but the thinking has to be the student's. ChatGPT needs a parent's permission for anyone under 18.
