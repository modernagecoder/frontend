Coding and AI classes in Regina

Who teaches coding, AI and senior math to Regina students live?

Modern Age Coders teaches coding and AI live online to Regina students from age 6 to adult, privately or alongside 15 to 20 classmates. Teens take Python for Teens, then AI and machine learning; senior students can add our Grade 12 math course beside Pre-Calculus 30. Every lesson is timed in Saskatchewan time, and the opening one costs nothing.

By Grade 12, a Regina student who enjoys both computing and math is often carrying three demanding courses at once: Computer Science 30, with its large capstone program, and Pre-Calculus 30 or Calculus 30. They draw on one another more than the timetable suggests. Sorting algorithms and recursion need the same careful step-by-step reasoning as a limit or a counting problem. This page sets out what those three courses ask for as Saskatchewan publishes them, takes a student through two City of Regina map layers that disagree about playgrounds, lists the Waterloo contests a Regina school can enter, and explains the province’s fixed clock.

Read side by side, the courses reinforce each other. A student who has written a recursive function finds the fundamental counting principle easier to trust, because they have watched a program enumerate the cases. A student comfortable with limits reads an algorithm’s running time with less fear. Our job is to give the time that a busy Grade 12 timetable cannot, in one subject or in all three.

The capstone is the hard part

CS30-CAP2 asks students to “Document and reflect on the process of creating a large-scale computer program.” Planning, splitting into modules and keeping notes are skills we practise long before the project starts.

Working with others

Computer Science 30 also asks students to “Engage in collaborative programming practices.” In our group classes students review one another’s code, which is the gentlest way to learn that.

Libraries, used wisely

CS30-FP3 has students use libraries to simplify solutions. We teach the plain version first, so a student knows what the library is doing on their behalf.

Regina data project

Two City of Regina layers, two answers about playgrounds

The City of Regina publishes its parks as open data in two layers: park areas with yes or blank columns for each amenity, and a separate set of amenity points. Both describe playgrounds. Our students check whether they agree. The counts are ours.

Our reconciliation of the City of Regina ParksAndAmenities layers, downloaded 22 October 2026 (998 park areas, 977 amenity points), matched on the NAME column
MeasureOur count
Park areas in layer 0998, under 442 different names
Park areas with PLAYGROUND marked Yes210, under 200 names
Playground points in layer 1254, under 220 names
Names in both lists175
Names flagged Yes but with no playground point25
Names with a playground point but not flagged45, of which 35 do not appear in layer 0 at all

Here is the script. It uses only the json module in Python 3, with both downloaded files in the same folder, and Python sets do the matching:import json with open("parks-layer0.geojson", encoding="utf-8") as f: parks = [feat["properties"] for feat in json.load(f)["features"]] with open("amenities-layer1.json", encoding="utf-8") as f: points = [feat["attributes"] for feat in json.load(f)["features"]] flagged = {p["NAME"] for p in parks if p["PLAYGROUND"] == "Yes"} pointed = {a["NAME"] for a in points if a["ASSET_TYPE"] == "PLAYGROUND"} print("park areas:", len(parks), "distinct names:", len({p["NAME"] for p in parks})) print("playground points:", sum(1 for a in points if a["ASSET_TYPE"] == "PLAYGROUND")) print("names flagged Yes:", len(flagged)) print("names with a point:", len(pointed)) print("in both:", len(flagged & pointed)) print("flag only:", len(flagged - pointed)) print("point only:", len(pointed - flagged))

One park, many shapes

The first surprise is that 998 areas carry only 442 names. A large park can be drawn as many polygons, so counting rows counts pieces, not parks. Turning names into a set fixes that before any comparison.

Set operations

With two sets of names, the questions become one line each: the intersection gives names on both lists, and the two differences give names on only one. A Grade 9 student can follow it, and it is exactly the kind of data structure work Computer Science 30 names.

Why the key fails

Of the 45 point-only names, 35 never appear among the park areas. That points to names spelled or recorded differently in the two layers rather than missing playgrounds. A text name is a weak key; the stronger next step is a spatial test of which area each point falls inside.

What a careful student writes

Not “the city has 254 playgrounds”, and not “45 playgrounds are missing from the map”. A fair sentence is that the two layers agree on 175 park names and differ on 70, and that most of the differences look like naming mismatches. The file supports that, and nothing stronger.

Regina’s winters show up in the same file: the amenity layer lists 34 outdoor rink shelters, 31 unboarded and 24 boarded outdoor rinks, 19 toboggan hills and 7 cross-country ski trails, a good second exercise in grouping categories. Contests from Regina

Full-solution and team contests a Regina school can enter

CEMC, the University of Waterloo’s Centre for Education in Mathematics and Computing, offers more than multiple-choice papers. These three suit students who like to explain their reasoning. Facts are from CEMC’s pages, read 22 October 2026.

Fryer, Galois and Hypatia

For Grades 9, 10 and 11 respectively. CEMC lists them for Wednesday 7 April 2027: four questions mixing answer-only and full-solution parts, in 75 minutes. Writing a clear solution is the skill being tested.

The team contest

The Canadian Team Mathematics Contest takes teams of six students from any mix of Grades 9 to 12. CEMC lists it at the University of Waterloo on Tuesday 13 April 2027, and in schools on Wednesday 14 April 2027 or later.

Problem of the Week

CEMC emails a Problem of the Week to educators for students in Grades 3 to 12, with new problems every Thursday and solutions a week later. Ask a teacher to pass them on.

All of these run through schools: CEMC does not register students on their own, so a Regina student needs a teacher to order the contest, or a nearby school willing to host. We prepare students with original practice problems that we write and label as ours, and send them to CEMC’s past contests for genuine papers, which are free. See our Fryer, Galois and Hypatia guide and the Canadian contest calendar. We are not affiliated with CEMC or the University of Waterloo.

Most of Canada changes its clocks on 1 November 2026. Regina does not. Here is what the province’s own bill says, and how our class times follow from it.

The Time Act, 2026

Bill No. 58 defines central standard time as UTC-6 and says the time to be observed in Saskatchewan is central standard time, including in Lloydminster. The bill comes into force by order of the Lieutenant Governor in Council; we have not confirmed that order, and nothing on this page depends on it.

Coding and AI

Can a Regina student let AI write the Computer Science 30 capstone?

It could write a lot of the code. It could not do the part the course actually assesses.

Ask an AI tool to compare the two Regina layers and it may report 45 missing playgrounds with total confidence. It has no way of knowing that 35 of those names simply never appear in the other layer. A student who wrote the set operations, then went looking at why the names differed, has done the thinking that CS30-CAP2’s documentation and reflection are meant to capture.

We teach that thinking: understand the problem, build the solution yourself, then challenge it. Students meet AI assistants as they grow, inside each tool’s age rules, as a tutor to question, never as the author of work they submit.

A capstone you cannot explain line by line is not proficiency, and Saskatchewan’s outcome asks for proficiency. The longer argument is in our longer piece on why the reasoning cannot be outsourced.
