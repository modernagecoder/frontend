## What does AI mean in our classes for children, teenagers and adults?

Something different at each age, always with the learner in charge. We teach how AI works and where it fails before anyone relies on it.

1. **Children, 9 to 12: AI from the inside.** Children write tiny programs that learn from examples, such as a guesser that improves with each answer, and see it get things wrong. No chatbot accounts; the point is understanding, not using a product.
2. **Teenagers, 13 to 18: models and honest testing.** Teenagers train simple models on real data, then test them on data the model has not seen. They may use AI coding tools only where the tool's own age rules allow; some need a parent's permission until 18 and some, Claude among them, are for adults only.
3. **University students and adults: AI as a colleague to check.** Generative AI, prompting, automation and agents, with one rule on top: if the AI produced it, a person reads, tests and explains it before it is used.

At every age we teach thinking over copying. Paste in something you cannot explain and the mentor will have you reconstruct it piece by piece; those ten minutes tend to teach more than the rest of the week. The glacier project below is a good example: the first answer is easy to compute and wrong in an interesting way, and only reasoning about the data shows why.

## What is the real daily temperature on the Llaca glacier?

INAIGEM, Peru's national institute for glacier and mountain-ecosystem research, publishes daily readings from an automatic weather station on the Llaca glacier, in the district of Independencia. Our learners use it to test a shortcut that weather records often rely on.

### The data

The dataset is INAIGEM's Registro meteorológico en alta montaña: temperatura y humedad relativa en el glaciar Llaca, part of a research project called PASTURE, published on datosabiertos.gob.pe under the ODC-By licence (Open Data Commons Attribution). INAIGEM says the measurements have been taken on the glacier itself since December 2024.

We saved the file on 10 November 2026 as llaca-inaigem.csv. It holds 147 days, from 2 December 2024 to 27 April 2025, each with the day's average, lowest and highest temperature and the same three figures for relative humidity. Every row passes a first sanity check: the lowest is never above the average, and the average never above the highest.

### The question

Many weather records do not keep a true daily average. They keep only the lowest and highest reading and take the middle: (minimum + maximum) / 2. It is quick, and on a day that warms and cools evenly it is close to the truth.

Is it close on a glacier? Version 1 computes both for every day and compares the averages over the whole record. Learners predict the result first; most say the two should be about the same.

**Version 1: the shortcut against the station's own average** (Python)
```python
import csv
import statistics

shortcut, measured = [], []
with open("llaca-inaigem.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f, delimiter=";"):
        low = float(row["TEMPERATURA_MINIMA"])
        high = float(row["TEMPERATURA_MAXIMA"])
        shortcut.append((low + high) / 2)
        measured.append(float(row["TEMPERATURA_PROMEDIO"]))

print("Days:", len(measured))
print("Shortcut average:", round(statistics.mean(shortcut), 2))
print("Station average:", round(statistics.mean(measured), 2))
```
It prints: Days: 147, then Shortcut average: 3.22 and Station average: 2.73.

The shortcut comes out half a degree warmer. On a glacier where the average day sits under three degrees, half a degree is a lot. But an average of averages can hide what is happening day by day, so Version 2 compares the two on every single day, counts how often the shortcut is too warm, and finds the days where it misses most.

**Version 2: day by day, and humidity too** (Python)
```python
import csv

warmer, gaps = 0, []
with open("llaca-inaigem.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as f:
    rows = list(csv.DictReader(f, delimiter=";"))

for row in rows:
    low = float(row["TEMPERATURA_MINIMA"])
    high = float(row["TEMPERATURA_MAXIMA"])
    mean = float(row["TEMPERATURA_PROMEDIO"])
    gap = (low + high) / 2 - mean
    if gap > 0:
        warmer += 1
    gaps.append((round(gap, 2), row["FECHA_REGISTRO"], low, mean, high))

gaps.sort()
print("Days the shortcut runs warm:", warmer, "of", len(rows))
print("Largest gap:", gaps[-1])
print("Smallest gap:", gaps[0])

humid = [(float(r["HUMEDAD_RELATIVA_MINIMA"]) + float(r["HUMEDAD_RELATIVA_MAXIMA"])) / 2
         - float(r["HUMEDAD_RELATIVA_PROMEDIO"]) for r in rows]
print("Humidity shortcut, average gap:", round(sum(humid) / len(humid), 2))
```
It prints: Days the shortcut runs warm: 140 of 147, Largest gap: (1.44, '20250212', 0.37, 1.87, 6.26), Smallest gap: (-0.2, '20250109', -0.05, 3.78, 7.2) and Humidity shortcut, average gap: -2.98.

The shortcut is too warm on 140 of the 147 days, so this is a pattern, not bad luck. The worst day, 12 February 2025, had a minimum of 0.37 and a maximum of 6.26, so the shortcut says 3.32, while the station's own average was 1.87. Our reading of the shape: the warmest moments are short, perhaps when the sun breaks through, while most of the day stays near the cold end, so the middle of the two extremes sits above the real average. For humidity the bias flips: the shortcut runs almost three points too dry, which on the same reading would mean the air stays close to saturation most of the day and dries out only briefly.

**Llaca glacier station by month, December 2024 to April 2025 (°C, our calculation)**

| Month | Days | Average minimum | Station average | Average maximum | Shortcut |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| December 2024 | 30 | 1.28 | 3.07 | 5.96 | 3.62 |
| January 2025 | 31 | 0.73 | 2.57 | 5.40 | 3.07 |
| February 2025 | 28 | 0.86 | 2.42 | 5.11 | 2.99 |
| March 2025 | 31 | 0.86 | 2.57 | 5.11 | 2.99 |
| April 2025 | 27 | 1.35 | 3.05 | 5.55 | 3.45 |

1. **Children, 9 to 12.** Draw a day as a line of 24 dots, most of them cold and three of them warm. Work out the middle of the coldest and warmest dot, then the true average of all 24. Which is bigger?
2. **Teenagers, 13 to 15.** Run both versions and explain in four sentences why the shortcut runs warm for temperature and dry for humidity.
3. **Teenagers, 15 to 18.** Group the gaps by month. Is the shortcut worse in the wettest months? What would you need from INAIGEM to test the sunshine explanation?
4. **University students and adults.** Fit a simple correction, shortcut minus a constant, using December to February, and test it on March and April. How much of the error does it remove, and is it honest to use it on another station?
