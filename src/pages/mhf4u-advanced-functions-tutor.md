### The details that shape a unit test

The specific expectations are more exact than most families expect. Polynomial factoring is limited to expressions &ldquo;of degree no higher than four&rdquo;, using common factoring, grouping, and the remainder and factor theorems. Students investigate division by x minus a &ldquo;to verify the remainder theorem and the factor theorem&rdquo;, which is why our second worked problem below starts there.

In the trigonometry strand, the compound angle formulas are explored and used, but the document adds that &ldquo;student reproduction of the development of the general case is not required&rdquo;. Students must be able to use the formulas for exact values and identities; deriving them from scratch is not the target.

### Two ideas students meet for the first time

The programme overview names what is new: Grade 11&rsquo;s exponential and trigonometric functions are revisited &ldquo;to address related concepts such as radian measure and logarithmic functions&rdquo;. Radians and logarithms are the two words that change the feel of the course, because the old habits from degrees and powers stop working.

The fourth strand is a quiet preview of calculus. An instantaneous rate of change is approximated by average rates over shrinking intervals, and composition gets its own expectation, including showing that f(g(x)) &ldquo;is not always equal to&rdquo; g(f(x)). Question 9 in our practice set checks exactly that.

### Who the course is for

The description names two groups. MHF4U is &ldquo;intended both for students taking the Calculus and Vectors course as a prerequisite for a university program and for those wishing to consolidate their understanding of mathematics before proceeding to any one of a variety of university programs.&rdquo;

The programme overview adds that Advanced Functions &ldquo;satisfies the mathematical prerequisite for some universities in areas that include business, social science, and health science programs&rdquo;. So the course is not only a stepping stone to calculus; for some programs it is the math requirement on its own.

### Prerequisite, credit and the final mark

The MHF4U course page gives its prerequisite as &ldquo;Functions, Grade 11, University Preparation, or Mathematics for College Technology, Grade 12, College Preparation&rdquo;, which is MCR3U or MCT4C. The ministry&rsquo;s online curriculum record says the same, with 2007 as the issue year.

## Where MHF4U students usually get stuck

These are the errors we see most often when Grade 12 students bring us their Advanced Functions tests. None of them is about talent. Each is a habit from an earlier course that no longer fits, and each has a check the student can learn to run.

**Common MHF4U mistakes by strand, and the check that catches each one (ours, from teaching the course)**

| Strand | The mistake | The check |
|---|---|---|
| Logarithms | Splitting log(a + b) into log a + log b | The product law is about log(ab). Try a = b = 1: log 2 is not 0 |
| Logarithms | Keeping a solution that makes the argument zero or negative | Substitute every answer back into each original log before writing it down |
| Trigonometry | A calculator left in degrees while the question is in radians | Check sin(&pi;/2) gives 1 before the first question, every time |
| Trigonometry | Finding one angle and stopping | Ask which quadrants share the sign, then list every solution from 0 to 2&pi; |
| Polynomials | Dividing both sides of an inequality by an expression that can be negative | Move everything to one side, factor, and use a sign chart instead |
| Rational functions | Calling every zero of the denominator a vertical asymptote | Factor first: a factor that cancels leaves a hole, not an asymptote |
| Combining functions | Working f(g(x)) from the outside in | Read it as: do g first, then feed the answer to f |
| Rates of change | Mixing up average rate over an interval with the rate at a point | An average needs two points; an instantaneous rate needs one point and a shrinking interval |

### Why the logarithm unit feels so different

Until Grade 12, most algebra moves forward: expand, simplify, substitute. A logarithm asks a backward question: what exponent turns the base into this number? Students who memorize the laws without that question in mind apply them to sums, to products of logs, and to anything else that looks close.

We teach every log rule by translating it back into exponents first. Once a student sees that log2 8 = 3 says the same thing as 23 = 8, the laws stop being a list and become consequences they can rebuild during a test.

### Why rational functions cost marks

A rational function hides its behaviour in its factors. Students who skip factoring miss holes, put asymptotes in the wrong place, and draw graphs that cross where they should not. The sketch is never the hard part; reading the factored form is.

Our routine has five lines, written before any drawing: domain, intercepts, vertical asymptotes or holes, horizontal behaviour, and the sign in each interval. With those five written down, the graph almost draws itself, and a marker can follow the reasoning.

## Two MHF4U problems, solved with the reasoning shown

We wrote both problems ourselves, pitched like a question on an MHF4U unit test. Read the decisions, not just the algebra: each step says why it comes next. A computer algebra system confirmed every answer.

Problem 1, exponential equations. Two short videos are uploaded on the same day. Clip A starts with 2000 views and doubles every 3 days. Clip B starts with only 500 views but triples every 4 days. After how many days does clip B catch up with clip A?

1. Write each clip as a function of t, the number of days. A(t) = 2000 &times; 2t/3 and B(t) = 500 &times; 3t/4. The exponent counts how many doubling or tripling periods have passed, which is why t is divided by the length of the period.
2. Set them equal and isolate the powers. 2000 &times; 2t/3 = 500 &times; 3t/4. Divide both sides by 500 and by 2t/3: 4 = 3t/4 &divide; 2t/3. Collecting the constants on one side first keeps the next step clean.
3. Decide between a common base and logarithms. 2 and 3 share no common base, so a common base is out. Take the logarithm base 10 of both sides; the curriculum itself notes, &ldquo;recognizing that logarithms base 10 are commonly used&rdquo;. The power law then gives log 4 = (t/4) log 3 &minus; (t/3) log 2.
4. Factor out t. log 4 = t (log 3 / 4 &minus; log 2 / 3), so t = log 4 &divide; (log 3 / 4 &minus; log 2 / 3). Notice that t appears once on the right: that is the moment to stop rearranging.
5. Evaluate, then interpret. log 3 / 4 is about 0.1193 and log 2 / 3 is about 0.1003, a difference of about 0.0189. So t is about 0.6021 &divide; 0.0189, roughly 31.8 days. Clip B catches up during day 32. Any base gives the same t, because the base only rescales both sides.
6. Check with numbers, not algebra. At day 31, A has about 2.58 million views and B about 2.49 million: B is still behind. At day 32, A has about 3.25 million and B about 3.28 million: B is ahead. The answer sits between them, as it should.

The habit worth keeping is the last step. A student who checks day 31 and day 32 will catch a sign error or a flipped fraction that the algebra alone hides. The difference 0.0189 is small, which also explains the story: B&rsquo;s growth rate is only slightly faster, so it takes a month to close a four-times gap.

Problem 2, a polynomial inequality. Solve x3 &minus; 2x2 &minus; 5x + 6 > 0, and explain how you know your answer is complete.

1. Look for a root among the factors of the constant term. By the rational root idea, any integer root divides 6, so test 1, &minus;1, 2, &minus;2, 3, &minus;3, 6, &minus;6. f(1) = 1 &minus; 2 &minus; 5 + 6 = 0, so by the factor theorem, (x &minus; 1) is a factor.
2. Divide to find what is left. Dividing by (x &minus; 1) leaves x2 &minus; x &minus; 6, which factors as (x &minus; 3)(x + 2). So f(x) = (x &minus; 1)(x &minus; 3)(x + 2), with zeros at &minus;2, 1 and 3.
3. Do not divide the inequality by anything. Dividing by (x &minus; 1) would flip the sign for some x and not others. Keep the product whole and ask where it is positive.
4. Build a sign chart. The three zeros split the number line into four intervals. Test one value in each: f(&minus;3) = &minus;24, f(0) = 6, f(2) = &minus;4 and f(4) = 18. The signs run negative, positive, negative, positive.
5. Read off the answer and check the boundaries. f(x) > 0 for &minus;2 < x < 1 or x > 3. The zeros themselves are excluded because the inequality is strict. A cubic with a positive leading coefficient ends positive on the right, which agrees with the last interval.

**The sign chart for (x &minus; 1)(x &minus; 3)(x + 2) (ours; every test value computed)**

| Interval | Test x | x + 2 | x &minus; 1 | x &minus; 3 | f(x) | Sign |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| x < &minus;2 | &minus;3 | &minus; | &minus; | &minus; | &minus;24 | negative |
| &minus;2 < x < 1 | 0 | + | &minus; | &minus; | 6 | positive |
| 1 < x < 3 | 2 | + | + | &minus; | &minus;4 | negative |
| x > 3 | 4 | + | + | + | 18 | positive |

### Why the sign chart beats a sketch alone

A quick sketch tells a student roughly where the graph is above the axis. The chart proves it, factor by factor, so the answer can be defended in writing. Ontario markers give communication marks for that kind of justification, and the curriculum asks students to &ldquo;justify conclusions&rdquo; as one of the reasoning skills.

The same chart solves rational inequalities too. The only change is that zeros of the denominator join the boundary list and are always excluded.

### What both problems have in common

Each one undoes something. In the first, a logarithm undoes the exponent so t can be reached. In the second, factoring undoes the expansion so the zeros can be read. Most of MHF4U has this shape: find the operation that is in the way, and apply its inverse.

That is also why we spend time on inverses early in the course. A student who understands that a log function is the inverse of an exponential function has one idea where others have two chapters.

## Twelve MHF4U practice questions

We wrote all twelve for this page and confirmed each by computer; none comes from a school, a board or a textbook. They follow the four strands in order, three per strand. Work each one on paper first; the answers follow in the next section.

1. Logarithms. Evaluate log2 32 &minus; log3 (1/9) without a calculator.
2. Logarithms. Solve log3 (2x + 1) = 2.
3. Exponential equations. Solve 4x = 8x &minus; 1 by finding a common base.
4. Radians. Convert 225&deg; to radians, then give the exact value of sin of that angle.
5. Transformations. For y = 3 sin(2(x &minus; &pi;/6)) + 1, state the amplitude, period, phase shift, maximum and minimum.
6. Trigonometric equations. Solve 2 sin2 x &minus; sin x &minus; 1 = 0 for x from 0 to 2&pi;.
7. Remainder theorem. Find the remainder when x3 + 2x2 &minus; 5x + 1 is divided by x &minus; 2, without long division.
8. Rational functions. For f(x) = (x + 1)/(x &minus; 2), give the vertical and horizontal asymptotes and both intercepts.
9. Composition. With f(x) = x2 + 1 and g(x) = 2x &minus; 3, find f(g(x)) and g(f(x)), and evaluate f(g(2)).
10. Rates of change. For f(x) = 2x, find the average rate of change from x = 1 to x = 3, then estimate the instantaneous rate at x = 2 using points 0.001 on either side.
11. Identities. Prove that (1 &minus; cos2 x) &divide; sin x = sin x wherever sin x is not 0.
12. Mixed. Solve log2 x + log2 (x &minus; 2) = 3, and explain why one algebraic answer must be rejected.

## Answers, with the reason behind each one

An answer without a reason earns little on an Ontario test, so each one below says how it was reached. All twelve were checked with SymPy, a computer algebra library.

1. 7. log2 32 = 5 because 25 = 32. log3 (1/9) = &minus;2 because 3&minus;2 = 1/9. So 5 &minus; (&minus;2) = 7. Each log is a question about an exponent, answered in your head.
2. x = 4. Rewrite in exponential form: 2x + 1 = 32 = 9, so x = 4. Check: the argument 2(4) + 1 = 9 is positive, so the answer stands.
3. x = 3. 4x = 22x and 8x &minus; 1 = 23x &minus; 3. Equal powers of the same base have equal exponents, so 2x = 3x &minus; 3 and x = 3.
4. 5&pi;/4, and sin(5&pi;/4) = &minus;&radic;2/2. Multiply by &pi;/180: 225 &times; &pi;/180 = 5&pi;/4. The angle lies in the third quadrant, where sine is negative, with a reference angle of &pi;/4.
5. Amplitude 3, period &pi;, phase shift &pi;/6 to the right, maximum 4, minimum &minus;2. The period is 2&pi; divided by k = 2. The vertical shift of 1 moves the centre line up, so the graph runs from 1 &minus; 3 to 1 + 3.
6. x = &pi;/2, 7&pi;/6, 11&pi;/6. Treat sin x as a single unknown: the quadratic factors as (2 sin x + 1)(sin x &minus; 1) = 0. So sin x = 1, giving &pi;/2, or sin x = &minus;1/2, which happens in the third and fourth quadrants at 7&pi;/6 and 11&pi;/6. Students who stop at one angle per factor lose a mark here.
7. 7. By the remainder theorem, the remainder is f(2) = 8 + 8 &minus; 10 + 1 = 7. No division is needed, which is the whole point of the theorem.
8. Vertical asymptote x = 2, horizontal asymptote y = 1, x-intercept &minus;1, y-intercept &minus;1/2. Nothing cancels, so x = 2 is a true asymptote. Numerator and denominator have the same degree, so the horizontal asymptote is the ratio of leading coefficients, 1 over 1.
9. f(g(x)) = 4x2 &minus; 12x + 10, g(f(x)) = 2x2 &minus; 1, f(g(2)) = 2. For f(g(2)), work inside out: g(2) = 1, then f(1) = 2. The two compositions differ, which is what the curriculum asks students to notice.
10. Average rate 3; instantaneous rate about 2.7726. The average is (23 &minus; 21) &divide; (3 &minus; 1) = 6 &divide; 2 = 3. Using x = 1.999 and x = 2.001 gives about 2.7726. In MCV4U this number turns out to be exactly 4 ln 2, which a student can check now on a calculator.
11. Proof. Start with the left side. By the Pythagorean identity, 1 &minus; cos2 x = sin2 x. So the left side is sin2 x &divide; sin x = sin x, which is the right side. Work on one side only; moving terms across both sides turns a proof into an equation.
12. x = 4; reject x = &minus;2. Combine with the product law: log2 (x(x &minus; 2)) = 3, so x2 &minus; 2x = 8, which gives (x &minus; 4)(x + 2) = 0. x = &minus;2 makes both arguments negative, and a logarithm of a negative number is undefined, so only x = 4 survives.

## A six-week plan for the MHF4U final exam

This plan is ours. It assumes a written final that covers all four strands, which is the common format, but confirm the details with the teacher first: weighting, whether a formula sheet is allowed, and which calculator.

**Six weeks to the Advanced Functions final (ours)**

| Week | Focus | Done when |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | Collect every unit test and quiz; sort each lost mark by strand and by type of mistake | One page lists the five mistakes you make most |
| 5 | Logarithms and exponential equations | You can solve a two-base equation and check it with numbers |
| 4 | Radians, trig graphs, equations and identities | You can list every solution from 0 to 2&pi; and prove three identities cleanly |
| 3 | Polynomial and rational functions, factoring, inequalities | You can sketch a rational function from its five lines and solve a cubic inequality with a sign chart |
| 2 | Rates of change and combined functions, then a first full practice paper | One timed paper done, every error logged |
| 1 | Second timed paper, then review of the error log only | No new topics after the second paper |

1. Ask the teacher three questions in week six. What share of the final is the exam, is there a formula sheet, and is a graphing calculator allowed. Each answer changes how you revise.
2. Rank strands by marks lost, not by how they feel. Many students fear trigonometry and then lose more marks on rational functions. The test papers already know which strand hurts.
3. Rewrite one problem per day from memory. Pick a problem you got wrong, close the book and solve it again with every step justified. Copying the correction does not count.
4. Keep a formula page in your own handwriting. Log laws, the special angle values, the compound angle formulas, the five-line rational routine. If a formula sheet is allowed, you will know where everything is on it; if not, you will have written it many times.
5. Practise in radians only. For the last three weeks, keep the calculator in radian mode and convert any degree question by hand.
6. Time the papers. Our two practice papers are full length. Finishing early on a practice paper is a sign to check answers by substitution, the habit from problem 1.
7. Sleep the night before. The last evening is for reading the error log once. Nothing new goes in after the second paper.

### The rule that makes MHF4U unavoidable for calculus

The Calculus and Vectors course page carries a note: &ldquo;The new Advanced Functions course (MHF4U) must be taken prior to or concurrently with Calculus and Vectors (MCV4U).&rdquo; So when a program lists MCV4U alone, as the St. George Economics listing does, a student who meets it has already taken MHF4U or is taking it alongside.

For families planning Grade 12, that means MHF4U goes in the first semester if the school allows it, or in the same semester as MCV4U if not. Taking calculus first is not an option the curriculum offers.

Some programs set a floor on individual courses as well as an overall average. Waterloo&rsquo;s Accounting and Financial Management page asks for a final grade of at least 75% in Advanced Functions, Calculus and Vectors and English, and it lists the program average in the mid-80s.

We do not promise any mark, and we would distrust anyone who did. Admission rules are revised from cycle to cycle, so reread each page in the autumn your teen applies.

## Four stages in Advanced Functions

Our own stages, drawn from teaching Grade 12 students through the course. Most students move between stages strand by strand, not all at once.

**Stages of an MHF4U student (ours)**

| Stage | What you notice |
|---|---|
| 1. Pattern matcher | Solves a question that looks like the textbook example, and freezes when the numbers or the order change |
| 2. Rule user | Knows the log laws and the trig identities, and sometimes applies them where they do not hold |

### For parents

Have your teen talk you through one solved problem, step by step, saying why each step is allowed. The &ldquo;why&rdquo; is where you will hear whether the course has landed.
A sudden drop in marks halfway through MHF4U is common. It often means the course moved from Grade 11 ideas to new ones, and the student&rsquo;s habits have not caught up yet.

### For students

Check before you hand in. Put every log answer back into the original equation, and every trig answer into a calculator in radian mode.
When a question looks unfamiliar, ask what operation is in the way and what undoes it. That one question solves more MHF4U problems than any formula sheet.

## MHF4U: common questions

### What is MHF4U?

MHF4U is Advanced Functions, Grade 12, University Preparation, an Ontario math course described in the 2007 curriculum for Grades 11 and 12. It has four strands: exponential and logarithmic functions, trigonometric functions in radians, polynomial and rational functions, and characteristics of functions, including rates of change and composition.

### Do you grant the MHF4U credit?

No. Our classes support the school course, and we do not grant Ontario credits. The credit comes from the student's school, a board summer or night school, or an Ontario credit provider. Our classes are the teaching and practice around that course.

### What is the prerequisite for MHF4U?

The MHF4U course page in the 2007 curriculum lists Functions, Grade 11, University Preparation (MCR3U), or Mathematics for College Technology, Grade 12, College Preparation (MCT4C). The current Ontario curriculum website gives the same two options.

### Can I take MHF4U and MCV4U at the same time?

Yes, the curriculum allows it. The Calculus and Vectors course page says Advanced Functions must be taken prior to or concurrently with Calculus and Vectors. Taking them together is heavy, because MCV4U leans on MHF4U ideas from its first unit, so many students prefer MHF4U first when the timetable allows.

### Is MHF4U hard?

It is a step up from MCR3U because two ideas are new: logarithms and radian measure. Most lost marks come from a few habits, such as misusing log laws, leaving the calculator in degrees, and dividing an inequality by an expression that can be negative. Those habits can be fixed with practice. We do not promise marks, and no honest tutor can.

### Which universities require MHF4U?

Many list it directly. On 22 October 2026, Waterloo's Accounting and Financial Management page asked for Advanced Functions with a minimum final grade of 75%, and Waterloo Computer Science listed it among the required Grade 12 courses. Programs that list only MCV4U still need MHF4U first or alongside, by the ministry's own rule.

### How is the MHF4U final mark built?

The 2007 curriculum splits the grade 70 to 30. Work during the term counts for the larger share, and an end-of-course evaluation, usually a written exam in MHF4U, counts for the rest. Schools set the details, so ask the teacher about formula sheets and calculators.

### How do you revise for the MHF4U final exam?

Start six weeks out. Sort every lost mark from the term by strand, work through logarithms, trigonometry, polynomial and rational functions, then rates of change, and finish with two timed practice papers. Check every answer by substitution and practise with the calculator in radian mode.

### Do you teach from the student's textbook?

We teach from the student's own course outline, tests and homework, whatever textbook the school uses. The curriculum is the same across Ontario, but the unit order and the emphasis differ between schools, so we follow the teacher's plan.

### When do classes run for Ontario students?

We set class times in Eastern time for most of the province and reconfirm them in the weeks of 1 November 2026 and 14 March 2027, when the clocks move.
