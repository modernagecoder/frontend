# Machine Learning for School Students in Oman

> Modern Age Coders teaches machine learning to school-age learners in Oman as the third of five rungs, after Python written by hand and real data handling. Students train regression, classification, clustering and simple neural network models in Python on an ordinary laptop, using data they generate or collect, and every model is reported with its error in a unit a non-specialist can check. Group teaching is USD 100 a month, private USD 150, and the first class is free.

## Four things a school student can genuinely train

| What they train | Typical age | What it predicts | What makes it real |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regression | 12 upward | A number: a temperature, a time, a score | The error is in the same unit as the thing predicted, so a parent can check it |
| Classification | 13 upward | A category: spam or not, ripe or not | Two kinds of mistake with different costs, the first grown-up idea in the subject |
| Clustering | 14 upward | Nothing. It finds groups nobody labelled | The student has to argue the groups mean something, which is genuinely hard |
| A small neural network | 15 upward | Images or sequences, on modest data | Written layer by layer rather than imported |

Nothing on that list requires a graphics card, a paid account or a dataset somebody must grant permission for. All four run on an ordinary laptop, in Python, on data the student generates or collects. That is not a compromise for school-age learners: a student who knows the ground truth is the only student who can discover their model is confidently wrong.

## The mathematics, and honestly when it arrives

| The idea | What it is actually for | When a student meets it | What it is not |
|---|---|---|---|
| Averages and spread | Knowing whether a result is unusual | Immediately, rung two | A statistics syllabus |
| Coordinates and lines | What a model is fitting | Early in rung three | Analytic geometry |
| Arrays and shapes | How data is held and why sizes must agree | Middle of rung three | Formal linear algebra |
| Rate of change | Why a model improves in small steps | Late in rung three, if at all | A calculus course |
| Probability | How confident a classifier is entitled to be | Rung three into four | Combinatorics |

Nothing beyond school arithmetic is needed to start. Each idea is introduced at the moment it explains something the student has already watched happen on their own screen. A learner who has seen their model improve slightly each round, and asked why it does not just jump to the answer, is ready for the idea of a gradient in about ten minutes; the same learner handed it in advance as a chapter retains none of it.

**If maths is genuinely the blocker**, address the maths. We teach mathematics as its own subject at every level, and a student stalling on algebra rather than syntax is better served by a term fixing that than by struggling through a coding course that keeps hitting the same wall.

## What a term actually looks like

1. **Weeks one and two:** get data that is genuinely yours. Measuring something themselves is strongly preferred, because it means they can check the answer later.
2. **Weeks three and four:** look at it before you model it. Plots first. Students who model first and look later spend the term explaining a number they never understood.
3. **Week five:** write the stupidest model you can defend. Always predict the average, or always predict yesterday. One line, and it becomes the number everything else has to beat.
4. **Weeks six to eight:** train something real and measure it in a unit. The output that matters is a sentence: this is wrong by about four degrees, and the baseline was wrong by about nine.
5. **Weeks nine and ten:** try to break your own result. Data from a different day, place or person. Most first models degrade sharply, and finding out why is where the subject stops being a recipe.
6. **Weeks eleven and twelve:** publish it with its limits attached. Code, write-up, error, baseline, and an honest paragraph on where it fails.

## Four things sold as machine learning that are not

- **A pre-trained model behind a button.** The model existed before the child arrived and is unchanged after they leave.
- **Prompting a chatbot.** Valuable, and it is operating a finished product. See [vibe coding versus building AI](https://learn.modernagecoders.com/vibe-coding-vs-building-ai-oman).
- **Robotics with a line-following sensor.** Excellent for control and feedback. A threshold on a sensor is a rule the student wrote, not one a model learned.
- **A dataset that was tidied first.** Real modelling, with the actual work removed. Students trained only on tidy data are helpless at their first real file.

The question that cuts through all four, worth asking any provider including us: **can the student show you the code that trained the model, and tell you its error in a unit you understand?**
