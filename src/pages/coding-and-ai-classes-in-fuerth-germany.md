## Who are coding and AI classes in Fürth for?

Anyone from first grade to retirement who wants to understand how software and AI work, not only how to click them. The order of learning is the same at every age; the speed and the projects change.

### Children, 6 to 12

Younger children drag coloured Scratch blocks into stories and games, so spelling and typing never hold them back. From about nine they type real Python. AI enters as an idea, a program that learns a rule from examples, and never as a chatbot account.

### Teenagers, 13 to 18

Teenagers write Python for data and build small machine-learning models, then test them on data the model has not seen. Many also want help alongside the Informatik they meet at school, which in Bavaria differs by school type and grade.

### Adults

Adults learn Python to save hours at work, to read spreadsheets and reports with code, and to call AI services from their own scripts. The aim is judgement: knowing when an AI answer is right, and how to prove it.

### What this means for a Fürth family

A Fürth child spends grades 1 to 4 in Grundschule, which the Bavarian Informatik rules above do not cover. At a Gymnasium, the first fixed computing time is one lesson a week in grades 6 and 7, then a gap for most branches until grade 11.

That gap, roughly from age 13 to 16, is when many teenagers either lose interest in computing or teach themselves. A weekly live class keeps the thread going until the Oberstufe picks it up again.

### AI in the school framework

The Kultusministerkonferenz adopted its strategy "Bildung in der digitalen Welt" in 2016. Ten years later, in 2026, it added AI-related competences through an updated competence framework, after a 2024 recommendation to school administrations on handling AI in learning.

So AI is now an official part of what German schools are asked to teach. How it reaches a Fürth classroom is up to Bavaria and each school; our classes sit alongside that and never replace it.

The Bavarian Abitur detail matters for older pupils. Because the written Informatik exam is on paper, a pupil has to be able to read and trace code without running it. Our teenagers practise exactly that: before any program on this page is run, the learner writes down what they expect it to print.

## Can a computer learn to forecast rain from Fürth weather data?

A little, and the way it fails is the lesson. The Deutscher Wetterdienst publishes every day its Fürth station recorded from 1947 to 1955, free to reuse under CC BY 4.0. Our learners build a tiny rain forecast on it and discover that the score most people look at first can be fooled.

### The data

The station is called "Fürth/Bayern (A)", number 01522 in the DWD Climate Data Center. Its file of daily climate data runs from 1 June 1947 to 31 March 1955, 2,861 days, with the rain, air pressure, temperature and cloud cover for each one. We downloaded it on 3 November 2026.

The station metadata records a move: from 1 March 1954 it stood two metres higher, at 313 m instead of 311 m, a short distance away. Only one rain value is missing, on the very last day. Old data, carefully kept.

### The question

Given today's weather, will tomorrow be wet, with 1 mm of rain or more? We train on the years 1947 to 1952 and test on 1953 to 1955, so the program is judged on days it has never seen.

We give learners a small file, fuerth_1947_1955.csv, with the date, the rain, the station pressure in hectopascals, the mean temperature and the cloud cover. The first program tries two models that need no learning at all.

**Version 1: two models that learn nothing** (Python)
```python
import csv

days = []
with open("fuerth_1947_1955.csv", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        if row["rain_mm"] != "-999":
            days.append((row["date"], float(row["rain_mm"]), float(row["pressure_hpa"])))

pairs = []
for today, tomorrow in zip(days, days[1:]):
    pairs.append((today[0], today[1] >= 1.0, today[2], tomorrow[1] >= 1.0))

test = [p for p in pairs if p[0] >= "1953"]
wet = sum(1 for p in test if p[3])
print("Test days:", len(test), " wet tomorrow:", wet)

def score(name, guess):
    right = sum(1 for p in test if guess(p) == p[3])
    missed = sum(1 for p in test if p[3] and not guess(p))
    false_alarm = sum(1 for p in test if guess(p) and not p[3])
    print(name, "accuracy", round(100 * right / len(test), 1), "% missed wet:", missed, "false alarms:", false_alarm)

score("Always dry:", lambda p: False)
score("Same as today:", lambda p: p[1])
```
It prints: Test days: 818 wet tomorrow: 216, then Always dry: accuracy 73.6 % missed wet: 216 false alarms: 0 and Same as today: accuracy 69.6 % missed wet: 124 false alarms: 125.

Read that twice. A program that answers "dry" every single day is right 73.6% of the time, because in Fürth most days are dry. It beats the program that copies today's weather. And it never warned anyone about a single one of the 216 wet days. That is why "accuracy" alone is a dangerous number, in weather and in every AI system built to spot something rare.

So we count the two kinds of mistake separately: a wet day the model missed, and a false alarm on a dry day. Then we let the program learn something. Falling air pressure often comes before rain, so Version 2 tries every pressure threshold from 960 to 999.5 hPa on the training years and keeps the one that does equally well on wet and dry days.

**Version 2: learn a pressure threshold** (Python)
```python
train = [p for p in pairs if p[0] < "1953"]

def balanced(rows, t):
    wet = [p for p in rows if p[3]]
    dry = [p for p in rows if not p[3]]
    caught = sum(1 for p in wet if p[2] < t) / len(wet)
    calm = sum(1 for p in dry if p[2] >= t) / len(dry)
    return (caught + calm) / 2

candidates = [960 + x / 2 for x in range(80)]
t = max(candidates, key=lambda c: balanced(train, c))
print("Training days:", len(train), " learned threshold:", t, "hPa")
score("Pressure rule:", lambda p: p[2] < t)
```
Run after Version 1, it prints: Training days: 2041 learned threshold: 981.0 hPa and Pressure rule: accuracy 56.6 % missed wet: 52 false alarms: 303.

The learned rule catches 164 of the 216 wet days, which neither earlier model came close to, yet its accuracy falls to 56.6% because it raises 303 false alarms. Which model is better? There is no answer until someone decides which mistake costs more. For a farmer cutting hay, a missed shower is expensive. For a school trip, a false alarm only means an umbrella nobody needed.

**Version 3: low pressure and falling** (Python)
```python
falling = {}
for yesterday, today in zip(days, days[1:]):
    falling[today[0]] = today[2] < yesterday[2]

score("Low and falling:", lambda p: p[2] < t and falling.get(p[0], False))
```
Run after Versions 1 and 2, it prints: Low and falling: accuracy 65.5 % missed wet: 115 false alarms: 167.

**Four rain models on 818 test days in Fürth, 1953 to 1955 (our calculation)**

| Model | Accuracy, % | Wet days missed | False alarms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Always dry | 73.6 | 216 | 0 |
| Same as today | 69.6 | 124 | 125 |
| Pressure below 981.0 hPa | 56.6 | 52 | 303 |
| Low and falling | 65.5 | 115 | 167 |

Data: Deutscher Wetterdienst, Climate Data Center, daily climate data, station 01522 Fürth/Bayern (A), 1 June 1947 to 31 March 1955, licence CC BY 4.0, downloaded 3 November 2026. A wet day is our definition: 1.0 mm of rain or more. All models, thresholds and counts are our own.

**Wet days (1.0 mm or more) at the Fürth station in each full year (our count)**

| Year | Wet days |
|---|---|
| 1948 | 106 |
| 1949 | 81 |
| 1950 | 122 |
| 1951 | 106 |
| 1952 | 134 |
| 1953 | 89 |
| 1954 | 109 |

Our count from the same DWD file. 1949 had the fewest wet days of the seven full years and 1952 the most, so a model trained on some years and tested on others meets a different climate each time, one more reason to test on separate years.

1. **Ages 6 to 8, no screen.** For two weeks, guess each evening whether tomorrow will be wet, and keep a tally of right guesses, missed rain and false alarms. Then try "always dry" on the same days.
2. **Ages 9 to 12, Python.** Run Version 1 and explain to a parent why "always dry" wins on accuracy. Then change the wet-day line from 1.0 mm to 5.0 mm and predict, before running, what happens to the score.
3. **Teenagers.** Write Version 2 yourself before reading ours, then add a column for the share of wet days caught. Which model would you pick for a hay farmer, and which for a school trip?
4. **Adults.** Replace the two-way score with a cost: a missed wet day costs five, a false alarm costs one. Find the threshold with the lowest total cost on the training years and check it on the test years.

## Will the lessons be in German or English?

In English, always. We say so before anyone books, because it matters to Fürth families.

### What English-only means in practice

Every lesson is live and taught in English by a mentor based in India. We do not run German-language classes, and no lesson switches into German.

Mentors speak clearly, slow down for younger children, and get used to each learner's accent within a few lessons. When a child reaches for a German word mid-sentence, the mentor simply supplies the English for it and carries on.

### Why it works for coding

Python keywords such as if, for and print are English words already, so learning in English removes a layer of translation rather than adding one.

For teenagers and adults, the documentation, error messages and AI tools they will use later are mostly in English too. Coding and English improve together.

### For learners

Before you celebrate a high score, ask what the laziest possible program would score on the same data.
Always keep some data back for testing. A rule that only works on the days it learned from has learned nothing.

### For parents

Ask your child which mistake their program makes most often. A clear answer is a sign of real understanding.
Monthly notes describe what was built and what was hard, never just the hours spent.

## Coding and AI classes in Fürth: common questions

### Are the classes in German?

No. Lessons happen live in English and in no other language. The mentors work from India and adjust to how quickly each learner moves and to how they speak. There is no German-language option.

### Who are the classes for?

Children from 6, teenagers and adults. Children start with Scratch and move to Python, teenagers learn data and machine learning, and adults learn Python and applied AI. Age groups are always taught separately.

### Do Bavarian schools teach coding?

Partly. The Gesellschaft für Informatik records one compulsory Informatik lesson a week in grades 5 to 10 at the Mittelschule, one weekly Informatik-focused lesson in grades 6 and 7 at the Gymnasium, and two compulsory lessons of informatics in grade 11. Those rules start in secondary school.

### Will my child use ChatGPT in class?

Children do not. OpenAI sets 13 as its minimum and asks for a parent's permission up to 18, Google sets 16 for Gemini in Germany, and Claude is for adults only. Children learn how AI works by building small models in Python instead.

### When are classes in Fürth time?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Fürth afternoons and early evenings are evenings in India, so the hours fit well.

### What is the Fürth rain project?

An original Python project of ours on the daily record of the Deutscher Wetterdienst station Fürth/Bayern from 1947 to 1955. Learners find that a model which always says dry scores 73.6% accuracy on the test years while missing all 216 wet days, and learn to count each kind of mistake.

### Is the weather data free to use?

Yes. The Deutscher Wetterdienst publishes its climate data under the Creative Commons BY 4.0 licence, which allows reuse with credit. We name the source wherever the data appears.

### Can adults join, or is it only for children?

Adults are welcome and have their own classes. Most adult learners in Germany want Python for work, data analysis, or to understand and check the AI tools their employer has introduced.

### How big are the groups?

Group classes have fifteen to twenty learners of a similar age. A one-to-one plan gives two private classes a week at agreed times with the same mentor.

### How are fees charged?

Once a month. The page displays euro, converted with the European Central Bank reference rate, and the charge goes through in US dollars for your bank to convert. Trying the first class costs nothing, and no card details are asked for.
