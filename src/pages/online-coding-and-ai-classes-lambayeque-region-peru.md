## Why do four honest percentages add up to 101?

The Gobierno Regional de Lambayeque publishes the list of every school service in the region as open data. Our students use it to meet a problem that every newspaper, school report and chart maker faces: round each share to a whole number and the total may not come out at 100.

### The data

The Gobierno Regional de Lambayeque releases "Centros Educativos en la Región Lambayeque actualizados a junio 2025" through the national open-data portal (Plataforma Nacional de Datos Abiertos), with ODC-By, the Open Data Commons Attribution Licence, as its terms. Our copy, data_iiee_rl.csv, taken on 9 November 2026, holds 3,616 rows dated 1 July 2025.

Each row is one school service, not one building: a primaria and a secundaria that share a site are two rows with the same Código Local. The column "Nivel / Modalidad" says which kind of service it is, from Inicial No Escolarizado to Superior Tecnológica.

### The question

What share of the region's school services are inicial, primaria, secundaria and everything else? A student groups the sixteen kinds of service into those four stages, counts them, divides by the total and rounds each share to a whole percentage, the way a newspaper would print it.

Then they add the four percentages up as a check, expecting 100. The program below does exactly that.

**Version 1: round every share, then add them up** (Python)
```python
import csv

def stage(level):
    if level.startswith("Inicial"):
        return "Inicial"
    if level in ("Primaria", "Secundaria"):
        return level
    return "Other"

counts = {}
with open("data_iiee_rl.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as src:
    for service in csv.DictReader(src, delimiter=";"):
        s = stage(service["Nivel / Modalidad"])
        counts[s] = counts.get(s, 0) + 1

total = sum(counts.values())
shares = {s: round(100 * n / total) for s, n in counts.items()}
print("Services:", total)
print("Shares:", shares)
print("Adds up to:", sum(shares.values()))
```
It prints Services: 3616, Shares: {'Inicial': 50, 'Other': 6, 'Primaria': 31, 'Secundaria': 14} and Adds up to: 101.

Nothing is wrong with the arithmetic. The exact shares are 49.585% for inicial, 30.725% for primaria, 13.772% for secundaria and 5.918% for the rest. Every one of them rounds up, so each gains a little, and the little gains add up to a whole extra point. Change the grouping and the total can just as easily come out at 99. Rounded parts do not have to add up to a rounded whole.

There are two honest ways out. One is to print one decimal place and say that the parts may not sum exactly because of rounding. The other, when a chart or a table must show whole numbers that total 100, is the largest-remainder method: round every share down, see how many points are missing, and give them one at a time to the shares that lost the most when they were cut.

**Version 2: round down, then hand out the missing points by largest remainder** (Python)
```python
import csv

def stage(level):
    if level.startswith("Inicial"):
        return "Inicial"
    if level in ("Primaria", "Secundaria"):
        return level
    return "Other"

counts = {}
with open("data_iiee_rl.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as src:
    for service in csv.DictReader(src, delimiter=";"):
        s = stage(service["Nivel / Modalidad"])
        counts[s] = counts.get(s, 0) + 1

total = sum(counts.values())
exact = {s: 100 * n / total for s, n in counts.items()}
shares = {s: int(x) for s, x in exact.items()}          # round every share down
left = 100 - sum(shares.values())                       # points still to hand out
by_remainder = sorted(exact, key=lambda s: exact[s] - shares[s], reverse=True)
for s in by_remainder[:left]:
    shares[s] += 1                                      # biggest leftovers first
print("Points handed out:", left, "to", by_remainder[:left])
print("Shares:", shares)
print("Adds up to:", sum(shares.values()))
```
It prints Points handed out: 3 to ['Other', 'Secundaria', 'Primaria'], Shares: {'Inicial': 49, 'Other': 6, 'Primaria': 31, 'Secundaria': 14} and Adds up to: 100.

Rounded down, the four shares are 49, 30, 13 and 5, which make 97. Three points are missing. The leftovers are 0.918 for "other", 0.772 for secundaria, 0.725 for primaria and 0.585 for inicial, so the first three get a point each and inicial stays at 49. The total is now exactly 100. The price is that inicial shows as 49 even though 49.585 rounds to 50 on its own: no method can make every part look rounded the usual way and still hit 100, so a careful writer chooses one and says which.

Yes, by training careful reasoning on problems our own team writes; the organiser's papers are never copied. Entry is through the student's colegio, and the 2026 school round took place on 8 July. Modern Age Coders has no link with MINEDU, the GRE Lambayeque or the olympiad itself.

### Can university students and adults join?

Yes. Adults have their own groups. Most begin with Python, then branch into spreadsheets and data or AI tools, scheduled around lectures or a working day.

### How are fees charged?

Once a month, by card, in US dollars, with your bank doing the conversion to soles. Prices shown to someone browsing from India appear in rupees instead. Nothing is charged for the first class.

### Will my child get a certificate?

Where a course includes one, it is a Modern Age Coders completion certificate that records the work done. No Peruvian body issues or backs it: not MINEDU, a UGEL, a university or a school, and it carries no school credit.

### What is the Lambayeque project on this page?

An original Python lesson on the regional government's open list of school services: rounding four shares gives 101, and the largest-remainder method fixes the total at 100. Older children and teenagers work through it in class.
