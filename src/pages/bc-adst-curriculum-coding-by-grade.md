# BC ADST curriculum: where coding appears, grade by grade

In British Columbia, coding at school lives mostly inside Applied Design, Skills, and Technologies, usually shortened to ADST. The curriculum changes shape as children grow: in the early grades it is design thinking woven through other subjects, in Grades 6 and 7 it comes as short modules, and in Grades 8 and 9 it becomes the equivalent of a full-year course, with programming named outright. This page sets out what each grade&rsquo;s curriculum page actually says about coding, quoted from curriculum.gov.bc.ca, with an activity of ours for each stage and two short programs to try at home.

At a glance: Covers: Kindergarten to Grade 9, then 10 to 12; Subject: Applied Design, Skills, and Technologies; K to 5: Design thinking across subjects; 6 and 7: At least three modules a year; 8 and 9: A full-year course equivalent; Our classes: Block coding to Python, live online; Format: A batch, or one to one; Clock: Pacific time, UTC-7 all year. ### What the table means for a family

Coding is not a separate subject in BC elementary schools. Before Grade 6, the ADST pages list no content at all, only big ideas and curricular competencies to be used with other subjects. Whether a Grade 3 class meets block coding depends on the teacher&rsquo;s choices, not on a provincial requirement to code.

From Grade 6 the picture sharpens. The curriculum lists Computational Thinking and Robotics among its modules, and by Grade 8 programming is named content. Even then, the school chooses which modules to run.

### Why the same grade can look different

The Grade 6 and 7 page says schools &ldquo;may choose from among the modules listed below or develop new modules&rdquo;, and that &ldquo;Locally developed modules can be offered in addition to, or instead of, the modules in the provincial curriculum.&rdquo; The Grade 8 and 9 pages say the same of their modules.

So two Grade 7 students in BC can have very different ADST years, both fully within the curriculum. The useful question for the school is which modules your child&rsquo;s class is taking this year.

## Kindergarten to Grade 5: design thinking, not coding lessons

The early ADST pages are short. They give big ideas and curricular competencies, and they say plainly that these are to be used with content from other subjects.

**ADST big ideas and two competencies for each early band, quoted from the BC curriculum (fetched 23 October 2026)**

| Band | Big ideas | Two competencies that apply to coding |
|---|---|---|
| K to 3 | &ldquo;Designs grow out of natural curiosity.&rdquo; &ldquo;Skills can be developed through play.&rdquo; &ldquo;Technologies are tools that extend human capabilities.&rdquo; | &ldquo;Use trial and error to make changes, solve problems, or incorporate new ideas from self or others&rdquo;; &ldquo;Use materials, tools, and technologies in a safe manner in both physical and digital environments&rdquo; |
| 4 and 5 | &ldquo;Designs can be improved with prototyping and testing.&rdquo; &ldquo;Skills are developed through practice, effort, and action.&rdquo; &ldquo;The choice of technology and tools depends on the task.&rdquo; | &ldquo;Make changes and test again, repeating until satisfied with the product&rdquo;; &ldquo;Demonstrate a willingness to learn new technologies as needed&rdquo; |

### Coding fits these competencies well

Trial and error, testing and changing, choosing a tool for the task: these are exactly the habits a young coder builds. A Grade 2 child who programs a character to walk across a screen, sees it stop short, and adds one more step is doing what the K to 3 competencies describe.

A teacher may bring block coding or floor robots into another subject, a math lesson on counting steps or a story told through animation. The curriculum makes room for that without requiring it.

### Our unplugged grid activity (K to 3)

Draw a 4 by 4 grid and place a toy in the bottom-left square, facing up. Two commands only: F moves one square forward, R turns right a quarter turn. The program F F R F F moves the toy two squares up, turns it to face right, and moves it two squares right.

Now let your child write a program to reach a target square, run it with the toy, and fix it when it misses. That is sequencing and debugging with no screen at all, and the questions in our practice set start here.

### Three ideas worth practising at home

Sequence, selection and repetition are named together, and they are the three building blocks of every program: do this, then this; if something is true, do this; repeat this. A child who can spot all three in a short block script is ready for the Grade 8 content.

Cryptography appears twice, as an algorithm topic and in the history list beside Enigma and Alan Turing. That makes a shift cypher an ideal home project: it is an algorithm, it has a history, and breaking one teaches searching. Our worked example below does exactly that.

### Not every Grade 6 or 7 class meets these modules

Three modules a year is the minimum, and the list also includes drafting, food studies, media arts, metalwork, textiles, woodwork and more. A school may run Computational Thinking one year and not the next, or develop its own module.

If your child&rsquo;s modules this year are woodwork and textiles, they are getting a proper ADST year with no coding in it. Home practice or a class outside school is the way to keep coding going in that case.

The Grade 8 debugging item is one of the most useful lines in the whole ADST curriculum. Breaking a broken program into parts, and testing each part on its own, is how professional programmers find faults too.

## A cypher for Grades 6 and 7, then the code to break it

This example is ours. It starts with the Grade 6 and 7 idea of &ldquo;cryptography and code breaking&rdquo; done on paper, then turns it into a Python program that a Grade 8 or 9 student can read and change.

1. On paper first. Write the alphabet in a row. To encode with key 3, replace each letter with the one three places later: A becomes D, B becomes E, and X, Y, Z wrap round to A, B, C.
2. Encode a message. MEET AT THE LIBRARY AT NOON becomes PHHW DW WKH OLEUDUB DW QRRQ. Spaces stay as they are.
3. Decode by shifting back. Move each letter three places earlier. Anyone who knows the key can read the message.
4. Break it without the key. There are only 25 useful keys. Try each one and look for real words. That is a search, one of the simple algorithms the curriculum lists.
5. Let the computer search. The program below encodes, decodes, then tries all 25 keys and keeps any guess containing the word THE.

**Python · shift cypher and code breaker (ours)** (python)
```python
# A shift cypher, then breaking it by trying every key (ours)
ALPHABET = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ"

def shift(message, key):
    out = ""
    for ch in message.upper():
        if ch in ALPHABET:
            out += ALPHABET[(ALPHABET.index(ch) + key) % 26]
        else:
            out += ch                      # spaces and punctuation pass through
    return out

secret = shift("MEET AT THE LIBRARY AT NOON", 3)
print("encoded:", secret)
print("decoded:", shift(secret, -3))

# Code breaking: try all 25 keys and keep the ones that contain a common word
for key in range(1, 26):
    guess = shift(secret, -key)
    if " THE " in guess:
        print("key", key, "->", guess)
```

**Output, Python 3.13** (text)
```text
encoded: PHHW DW WKH OLEUDUB DW QRRQ
decoded: MEET AT THE LIBRARY AT NOON
key 3 -> MEET AT THE LIBRARY AT NOON
```
Only key 3 produces a message with THE in it, so the search finds the key on its own.

### What each part teaches

The shift function uses all three building blocks the Grade 6 and 7 page names: a sequence of steps, a selection (is this character a letter?) and repetition (do it for every character). The % 26 handles the wrap-around from Z back to A.

The code breaker is a search: try every possibility, test each one, keep the matches. It works because the key space is tiny, which is exactly why this cypher is not safe for real secrets.

### Extensions by age

Grade 6 or 7: do the paper version, then build it in Scratch with a list of letters. Grade 8: type the Python, change the key, and make the breaker look for a different common word. Grade 9: count which letters appear most often in a long encoded message, and use that to guess the key without trying all 25.

The history list on the same curriculum page names Enigma and Alan Turing. The story of how wartime codes were broken is a natural next read.

## Binary for Grade 8, written as sub-problems

Grade 8 lists the &ldquo;binary number system (1s and 0s) to represent data&rdquo;. This short program of ours converts whole numbers to binary and checks every answer against Python&rsquo;s own converter.

1. Sub-problem 1: find the next bit. The remainder when a number is divided by 2 is its last binary digit. 13 divided by 2 is 6 remainder 1, so 13 ends in 1.
2. Sub-problem 2: build the answer in order. Each new remainder goes in front of the bits found so far, because remainders come out right to left.
3. Repeat until nothing is left. 13 gives remainders 1, 0, 1, 1, so 13 is 1101 in binary.
4. Handle the edge case. Zero would give an empty answer, so the function returns 0 straight away.
5. Check every answer. Python&rsquo;s int(text, 2) reads binary back, so each result can be tested automatically.

**Python · binary converter (ours)** (python)
```python
# Whole number to binary, one sub-problem at a time (ours)
def to_binary(n):
    if n == 0:
        return "0"
    bits = ""
    while n > 0:
        bits = str(n % 2) + bits    # the remainder is the next bit, right to left
        n = n // 2
    return bits

for n in [0, 5, 13, 26, 255]:
    print(n, "->", to_binary(n), "check:", int(to_binary(n), 2))
```

**Output, Python 3.13** (text)
```text
0 -> 0 check: 0
5 -> 101 check: 5
13 -> 1101 check: 13
26 -> 11010 check: 26
255 -> 11111111 check: 255
```

Every number comes back unchanged, so both sub-problems work. If one did not, testing each sub-problem alone, as the Grade 8 debugging content describes, would find it. ## Ten practice questions, from Kindergarten level to Grade 9

We wrote these questions for this page, two for each stage. Younger children can do the first ones aloud with a parent; teens can go straight to the end.

1. K to 3. On the 4 by 4 grid, the toy starts bottom-left facing up. Where does it end after F F F R F, and which way does it face?
2. K to 3. Run F R F R F R F from the same start. Where does the toy finish?
3. 4 and 5. What shape does &ldquo;repeat 4 times: F, R&rdquo; trace, and where does the toy end up?
4. 4 and 5. A block game should give a point each time the cat touches a fish, but the score never goes up. Name two things you would test, one at a time.
5. 6 and 7. Encode CODE with a shift of 1.
6. 6 and 7. One pass of bubble sort compares neighbours left to right and swaps any pair in the wrong order. What is the list 4, 2, 3, 1 after one pass?
7. 8. Write 19 in binary.
8. 8. What number is the binary 10110?
9. 8 and 9. A student changed one line of the converter to bits = bits + str(n % 2). What does it now return for 13, and which sub-problem broke?
10. 9. A robot reads distances 50, 35, 22, 18, 30 cm and must stop at the first reading under 20. At which reading does it stop? Write the rule in one line of pseudocode.

## Answers

Short answers with the reason, checked by running the programs where there is code.

1. Top row, second square from the left, facing right. F F F climbs three squares to the top row, R turns it to face right, and one F moves it one square along.
2. Back at the start, facing left. It walks a small square path: up, right, down, left, and three right turns leave it facing left.
3. A square, ending where it started and facing up. Four forward moves and four quarter turns bring it all the way round.
4. For example: does the touch check ever become true (make the cat touch the fish and watch), and does the score block run when it does (add a sound to that block)? Testing one thing at a time tells you which part is broken.
5. DPEF. Each letter moves one place along: C to D, O to P, D to E, E to F.
6. 2, 3, 1, 4. 4 and 2 swap, then 4 and 3, then 4 and 1. The largest number has bubbled to the end; more passes sort the rest.
7. 10011. 16 + 2 + 1 = 19.
8. 22. 16 + 4 + 2 = 22.
9. It returns 1011, the bits in reverse. Sub-problem 2 broke: new bits now go on the end instead of the front. Sub-problem 1 still finds the right remainders.
10. The fourth reading, 18 cm. Pseudocode: for each reading, if reading < 20 then stop. The rule uses selection inside repetition, like the cypher.

## BC ADST and coding: common questions

### What is ADST in BC?

ADST stands for Applied Design, Skills, and Technologies, a subject area in the BC curriculum from Kindergarten to Grade 12. It covers design thinking and making across fields such as computing, robotics, drafting, media, food studies, textiles and woodwork.

### Is coding taught in BC elementary schools?

Not as a required subject. The ADST pages for Kindergarten to Grade 5 list big ideas and competencies to be used with content from other subjects, with no coding content listed. A teacher may still bring in block coding or floor robots, so it varies from class to class.

### What coding is in the Grade 6 and 7 ADST curriculum?

Students take at least three ADST modules a year. One listed module, Computational Thinking, includes simple algorithms for sorting, searching, sequence, selection and repetition, cyphers, and visual programming such as Kodu or Scratch. A Robotics module covers programming and logic for robot components.

### What is in BC ADST Grade 8?

Schools must provide the equivalent of a full-year ADST course in Grade 8. Its Computational Thinking content includes algorithms as precise repeatable instructions, debugging by sub-problems, the binary number system, visual and text-based programming, and programming components such as Arduino or LEGO Mindstorms.

### What coding is in Grade 9 ADST?

The Grade 9 Information and Communications Technologies module lists text-based coding in HTML, CSS and JavaScript, binary representation of data, drag-and-drop mobile development, programmable components and cloud collaboration such as GitHub. Electronics and Robotics lists coding such as G-code, C++ and Sketch.

### Does every BC school teach the Computational Thinking module?

No. Schools choose which modules to offer and may develop their own, which can be offered in addition to or instead of the provincial modules. Ask the school which ADST modules your child's class is taking this year.

### What comes after ADST 9?

Grade 10 is where ADST turns into separate, named courses. On the computing side that means Computer Studies 10 or Web Development 10 first, with programming, information systems and robotics courses for Grades 11 and 12. Mathematics adds its own Computer Science 11 and 12.

### How can my child practise ADST coding at home?

For younger children, give and fix step-by-step directions or build small block games and test them. In Grades 6 and 7, try cyphers and simple sorting by hand. From Grade 8, type short Python programs such as a binary converter, and test each part separately when something breaks.

### When are classes for BC families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. The BC clock has not changed since March 2026, so the class keeps its slot through the winter.
