## Is programming taught in Cajamarca's secundaria schools?

No. Cajamarca follows the national curriculum, which has no programming area at any grade.

### What the week holds

On the regular school day, every secundaria grade has a 35-hour week: four hours each of Matemática and Comunicación, three of Inglés and two of Educación para el Trabajo. None of these is a programming class.

Digital skill is a transversal competency of the CNEB, number 28, used across areas rather than taught in one. A teenager can finish 5° de secundaria without ever writing code.

### Where that leaves a teen

University courses in engineering, economics, sciences and health increasingly assume some programming or data skill. A teenager who arrives having written real programs starts ahead.

Admission itself is by public competition with a compulsory knowledge exam, under the Ley Universitaria, and those exams are in Spanish. Our classes run beside school, the university alone decides who gets in, and we make no promise about it.

## How does a university in Cajamarca turn class hours into credits?

The Universidad Nacional de Cajamarca publishes the list of every undergraduate course section it ran in the 2026-I semester. Our teenagers use it to do what scientists and AI models both do: guess a rule, test it on every row, and keep the exceptions in sight.

### The data

The university released "Dataset Cursos pregrado matrícula semestre académico 2026 I" as open data on datosabiertos.gob.pe, under ODC-By, which lets anyone reuse it with credit. Our copy, unc-cursos-pregrado-2026-I.csv, has 2,882 rows, one per course section, cut on 6 October 2026. Each row gives the course, its credits, its weekly hours of theory and of practice, and how many students enrolled in that section. No student can be identified.

It is a useful look at university for a teenager: 1,653 different courses in 30 professional schools, with a typical section of 16 students. We have no connection with the university.

### The guess

Looking at a few rows, a teen spots a pattern: two hours of theory plus two of practice gives three credits, the commonest row of all with 1,334 sections, and three of theory plus two of practice gives four. Our guess, before testing it, is that one hour of theory is worth one credit and one hour of practice half a credit.

The first program turns that guess into a function and counts how many of the 2,882 sections it fits exactly.

**Version 1: test the guess on every row** (Python)
```python
import csv

def guess(theory, practice):
    return theory + practice / 2

match = 0
rows = 0
with open("unc-cursos-pregrado-2026-I.csv", encoding="latin-1") as listing:
    for course in csv.DictReader(listing):
        rows += 1
        t = int(course["HorasTeoria"])
        p = int(course["HorasPractica"])
        if int(course["Creditos"]) == guess(t, p):
            match += 1
print("Rows:", rows, "Rule fits:", match, "Misses:", rows - match)
```
It prints Rows: 2882 Rule fits: 1921 Misses: 961.

Two thirds right is not bad for a guess, but 961 misses is too many to ignore. Printing a few of them shows the cause: most have an odd number of practice hours, so the guess gives a half, such as 3 theory and 3 practice giving 4.5. The university's credits are whole numbers. So it must round, but which way?

Rather than argue, Version 2 tests three rival rules side by side: the exact guess, the guess rounded up and the guess rounded down. It counts the misses for each, and then lists the rows that even the strongest of the three cannot explain.

**Version 2: compare three rules and show what is left** (Python)
```python
import csv
import math
from collections import Counter

rules = {
    "exact": lambda t, p: t + p / 2,
    "round up": lambda t, p: math.ceil(t + p / 2),
    "round down": lambda t, p: math.floor(t + p / 2),
}
misses = {name: 0 for name in rules}
left_over = Counter()

with open("unc-cursos-pregrado-2026-I.csv", encoding="latin-1") as listing:
    for course in csv.DictReader(listing):
        t = int(course["HorasTeoria"])
        p = int(course["HorasPractica"])
        c = int(course["Creditos"])
        for name, rule in rules.items():
            if c != rule(t, p):
                misses[name] += 1
        if c != rules["round down"](t, p):
            left_over[(c, t, p)] += 1

print(misses)
for (c, t, p), n in left_over.most_common(3):
    print(n, "rows: credits", c, "theory", t, "practice", p)
```
It prints {'exact': 961, 'round up': 837, 'round down': 199}, then 122 rows: credits 4 theory 2 practice 3, 19 rows: credits 4 theory 3 practice 4 and 13 rows: credits 3 theory 2 practice 4.

**Three guessed credit rules tested on 2,882 course sections (our calculation)**

| Rule | Sections it misses | Sections it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Theory + practice / 2, exactly | 961 | 1,921 |
| The same, rounded up | 837 | 2,045 |
| The same, rounded down | 199 | 2,683 |

Course list for 2026-I from the Universidad Nacional de Cajamarca, reused under its Open Data Commons Attribution licence. The rules are our guesses and every count is ours. Hours are weekly hours as the university's data dictionary defines them. Four sections carry 0 credits, among them Deporte and Danzas Típicas Peruanas.

Rounding down wins clearly: it fits 2,683 of 2,882 sections, about 93%. But 199 sections still break it, and they are not random. The largest group, 122 sections with 2 hours of theory and 3 of practice, gets 4 credits where the rule says 3. Perhaps those courses follow an older study plan, or a school sets its own credits; the file does not say, and an honest analyst says "we do not know" rather than inventing a reason.

This is how an AI model should be judged too. A model that is right 93% of the time is useful, and the 7% it gets wrong is where the interesting questions are. Teens who learn to list the exceptions, instead of hiding them, become the people who can be trusted with real data.

1. **Ages 13 to 14, on paper.** For five rows of the table, work out the guess by hand, then round it both ways. Which rule would you have bet on before seeing the counts?
2. **First Python.** Run Version 1, then print the first ten rows it misses, with their hours. Write down the pattern you see before reading on.
3. **Ages 15 and over.** Group the 199 remaining misses by professional school. Are they spread out, or concentrated in a few schools?
4. **Going further.** The file also gives the number of students in each section. Add them all up: you get 52,488. Explain why that is a number of seats, not of students, and why the two are different.

Teens learn how a model is built: examples, a rule, a measure of error, and adjustment. Then any AI answer is treated like the credit rule above, as a guess to be tested on cases where the right answer is known.
