# Bundeswettbewerb KI (BWKI) project help

The Bundeswettbewerb Künstliche Intelligenz asks pupils to pick a real problem and attack it with machine learning, then explain it in writing, in code and in a short video. It is judged as a piece of research, and its rules are strict about one thing above all: the work must be the pupil's own, and every bit of help must be declared. This page explains the contest from its organisers' own rules and shows exactly where a mentor fits, and where a mentor must stop.

## What is the Bundeswettbewerb KI, and who can enter?

A national project competition in artificial intelligence for school pupils, organised by the Tübingen AI Center and carrying a recommendation from the Kultusministerkonferenz.

### What it asks for

The organisers' conditions describe a self-chosen project that tackles a problem of social or scientific relevance with methods of machine learning. No topics are set. A project may train a model on a dataset, made by the pupil, taken from the internet or provided by a partner, or may build in generative AI such as language or image models.

Work can happen at home or at school. The BWKI states that it does not provide rooms or materials, and that participants are responsible for their own computer and internet connection.

### Who may take part

Anyone in Sekundarstufe I or II may enter, solo or with up to three teammates. Entrants must have German citizenship, or live in Germany, or attend a recognised German school abroad. Students may still enter in their first year of a first degree if they left school no more than a year before, and the same applies to young people doing voluntary service.

There are no age categories, the conditions say, but there are special awards for young talent.

Two lines in the conditions matter to families who speak English at home. The organisers accept a project written in English as readily as one in German. And residence in Germany is enough: a pupil at an international school who lives in Germany meets the residence rule on the organisers' own wording. The contest belongs to the AG bundesweiter Jugendwettbewerbe, the association of national youth contests, and the Minister-President of Baden-Württemberg is its patron.

## What must a BWKI project contain, and how is it judged?

Three parts, assessed by an interdisciplinary jury against seven criteria. Every team gets written feedback, finalist or not.

### The three parts

**Documentation:** the idea, the methods, how it was built and what came out, plus possible sources of error and a critical view of the results.

**Code:** runnable and clearly structured, with every dataset, model, framework and outside tool marked.

**Video:** a pitch of two to four minutes that a non-expert can follow and an expert can still respect.

### After submission

A selection jury reviews every project and invites up to ten teams to the final in November, where they present at a stand they design, before the public and the jury. The organisers cover finalists' travel and accommodation, and personal attendance is required for the prizes.

Teams not chosen still receive expert feedback, which the organisers suggest using to come back stronger the next year.

**The seven BWKI judging criteria and the question behind each (English wording ours)**

| Criterion | The jury asks |
|---|---|
| Eigenständigkeit (own work) | How large is your own share of the project? |
| Originalität | How creative and new are the idea and the approach? |
| Technische Umsetzung | How demanding and reliable is the implementation? |
| Wissenschaftliches Arbeiten | Have methods, results and sources of error been examined traceably? |
| Praktische Relevanz | What use or insight does the project offer? |
| Codequalität | Is the code understandable, structured and traceable? |
| Präsentation | Are idea, method and results clear in the documentation and the video? |

## What help is allowed for a BWKI project, and how must it be declared?

Help is allowed. Hidden help is not. The conditions require a clearly visible, documented share of the pupil's own work, and full disclosure of every kind of support.

### What the rules say

The organisers list what must be declared: the use of AI models and frameworks, the involvement of people, institutions or companies, datasets that were provided or downloaded, and any GPU computing power or technical infrastructure made available. On registering, participants also confirm that they solve the tasks and build the project themselves and will name every source and helper in the documentation.

The organisers say they will not use participants' ideas or results for their own purposes without asking.

1. **The pupil proposes.** The pupil brings a question and a possible dataset; the mentor asks what success would look like and how it could be measured.

## Why is accuracy not enough? A lesson on evaluating a model honestly

The jury asks for analysis of error sources and a self-critical view of results. The most common trap in pupil projects is a single impressive accuracy figure. This example is ours, on invented data, to show why.

### The made-up problem (ours)

Imagine 365 days of readings from a school weather station. On a few days there is heavy rain, and we want to warn the caretaker in advance using the morning humidity. The data below is invented by a seeded random generator for teaching. It is not real and not from any project.

The first 240 days are for learning; the last 125 are locked away for testing.

### Two models

**Always no:** predict "no heavy rain" every day. It needs no data at all.

**Threshold:** predict heavy rain when humidity is at or above a number learned from the 240 training days, the one that most fairly balances catching rainy days against false alarms.

**Two models and a confusion matrix** (Python)
```python
import random

random.seed(51)
days = []
for d in range(365):
    heavy = random.random() < 0.07
    humidity = random.gauss(88, 4) if heavy else random.gauss(71, 8)
    days.append((round(humidity, 1), heavy))
train, test = days[:240], days[240:]

def scores(predict, data):
    tp = sum(1 for h, y in data if predict(h) and y)
    fp = sum(1 for h, y in data if predict(h) and not y)
    fn = sum(1 for h, y in data if not predict(h) and y)
    tn = len(data) - tp - fp - fn
    accuracy = (tp + tn) / len(data)
    recall = tp / (tp + fn) if tp + fn else 0
    precision = tp / (tp + fp) if tp + fp else 0
    return tp, fp, fn, tn, round(accuracy, 3), round(precision, 3), round(recall, 3)

def f1(t):
    tp, fp, fn, tn, a, p, r = scores(lambda h: h >= t, train)
    return 2 * p * r / (p + r) if p + r else 0

print("Heavy-rain days in test:", sum(1 for h, y in test if y), "of", len(test))
print("Always no: tp fp fn tn, accuracy, precision, recall =", scores(lambda h: False, test))
best_t = max(range(70, 96), key=f1)
print("Threshold learned on training days:", best_t)
print("Threshold model:", scores(lambda h: h >= best_t, test))
```
It prints Heavy-rain days in test: 10 of 125, then Always no: tp fp fn tn, accuracy, precision, recall = (0, 0, 10, 115, 0.92, 0, 0.0), Threshold learned on training days: 83 and Threshold model: (9, 8, 1, 107, 0.928, 0.529, 0.9).

**The two models on the 125 test days of invented data (our calculation)**

| Model | Rain warned and came | False alarms | Rain missed | Accuracy | Rain days caught |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Always no | 0 | 0 | 10 | 92.0% | 0 of 10 |
| Threshold at 83 | 9 | 8 | 1 | 92.8% | 9 of 10 |

Data: invented with random.seed(51) for this lesson, 365 days, heavy rain on about 7 percent. We chose the split, the threshold search and the seed; every figure was produced by that script under Python 3 on 3 November 2026.

Read the accuracy column alone and the two models look almost equal, 92.0 against 92.8 percent. Read the rest and they are nothing alike. The lazy model is right on 92 percent of days only because 92 percent of days are dry, and it misses every heavy-rain day. The threshold model catches 9 of the 10, at the price of 8 false alarms: its precision is about 0.53, so roughly every second warning is real. Whether that trade is good depends on the caretaker, and saying so in the documentation is exactly the scientific, self-critical work the BWKI criteria name. A pupil who reports only "92.8 percent accuracy" has hidden the most interesting part of their own project.

1. **Run it.** Write down the four numbers of each confusion matrix before reading the explanation.
2. **Move the line.** Fix the threshold at 80, then 86. What happens to false alarms and missed rain? Which would you choose, and for whom?
3. **Break the rule.** Learn the threshold on the test days instead. Why can that result not be trusted in a BWKI documentation?
4. **Your project.** Write one paragraph on which mistake would be worse in your own project idea, a false alarm or a miss, and why.

## From a first dataset to a project you can defend

Milestones we use with BWKI pupils to place them and to show parents where things stand.

**BWKI project milestones (ours)**

| Stage | Roughly when | By then the pupil can |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Data in hand | Month 1 | Load a dataset, describe it, and state its source and licence |
| 2. A baseline | Month 2 | Name the simplest prediction and measure it |
| 3. An honest test | Months 2 to 3 | Split the data, train a model and read its confusion matrix |
| 4. Error sources | Months 3 to 5 | Find where the model fails and explain why |
| 5. A defended project | Months 5 and up | Document, pitch and answer questions on every choice made |

### For pupils

If one number makes your model look perfect, look for the number that does not.
Write down every source and helper as you go. The rules ask for it at the end.

### For parents

Ask to see the project folder, not the result. Your teenager's own commits are the clearest sign of real work.
Help is fine and allowed. Make sure it is all named in the documentation.

## BWKI project help: common questions

### Are the classes in German?

No. Mentoring happens live and only in English, and the mentor adjusts to each pupil's pace and accent. The BWKI accepts projects in German or English, so a pupil can also write the project in English.

### Will you do the project or submit it for my child?

No. The BWKI requires a clearly documented share of the pupil's own work and the disclosure of every kind of help. We teach and ask questions; the pupil chooses the topic, writes the code and the documentation, records the video and submits.

### Do we have to mention you in the project?

Yes. The BWKI conditions require all support by people, institutions or companies to be disclosed in the documentation. A pupil who learned from us names our classes there, as with any other help.

### Who can enter the Bundeswettbewerb KI?

Pupils in Sekundarstufe I and II, alone or in a team of up to four, who have German citizenship, live in Germany or go to a recognised German school outside Germany. Some first-year students and people in voluntary service can enter if they left school within the past year.

### When is the next BWKI round?

The organisers describe a yearly rhythm: registration from early March, the idea by the end of June, the project by the end of September and the final in November. In 2026 the final is on 13 November 2026 in Tübingen. Exact 2027 dates come from the BWKI.

### May a teenager build a chatbot into a BWKI project?

The BWKI allows generative AI inside a project, provided it is declared. Providers set the ages, though: OpenAI asks for parental consent before 18, and Claude is for adults only under Anthropic's terms. We never use Claude with pupils under 18.

### Are you connected with the BWKI?

No. We have no connection with the Bundeswettbewerb Künstliche Intelligenz or the Tübingen AI Center. We are an independent teaching company.

### When are classes in German time?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Most pupils pick a late afternoon or a weekend.

### How are fees paid?

Our euro fee is derived from a European Central Bank reference rate; the payment itself is taken in US dollars and converted by your own bank. The trial class costs nothing, and you enter no card.
