# Maths tutor in Hobart

Anyone who has driven up kunanyi knows the road is steep, but how steep? The LIST, Tasmania's official map data, lets us measure it. Pinnacle Road starts between the 460 and 470 metre contour lines and climbs to a spot height of 1,271 metres, over about 10.6 kilometres of road. That is an average grade of about 7.6%, an angle of only 4.4 degrees. Draw a straight line from the bottom to the top instead and the grade jumps to 26.5%. Two honest answers to one question, and the difference is the whole point of gradient: it depends on what you count as the run. ## How steep is the road up kunanyi?

Gradient is rise divided by run. The LIST gives both, once you decide what the run should be.

We took every segment of Pinnacle Road from the LIST and linked them into one route. Its lower end lies between the 460 m and 470 m contour lines, so we use 465 m and carry the 10 m uncertainty. The highest spot height beside the road's top is **1,271 m**. The rise is therefore 806 m, give or take 5 m. Following the road from its lower end to the point nearest that spot height covers **10,574.5 m**; a straight line between the same two points is 3,043.8 m.

**Two runs, two gradients (our calculation from LIST data)**

| Run used | Run (m) | Gradient | Percent grade | Angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Along the road | 10,574.5 | 806 / 10,574.5 = 0.0762 | 7.62% | 4.36 degrees |
| Straight line, bottom to top | 3,043.8 | 806 / 3,043.8 = 0.2648 | 26.48% | 14.83 degrees |

### Percent and angle are different

A 7.62% grade is not 7.62 degrees. The angle is the inverse tangent of the gradient, which gives 4.36 degrees here. Mixing the two up is one of the most common gradient mistakes.

### The quick method wins on the road

For small slopes, the gradient itself is almost the angle in radians. That shortcut gives 4.37 degrees for the road, out by 0.01 of a degree. For everyday road grades it is all you need.

### And fails on the steep line

For the straight line the same shortcut gives 15.17 degrees against the true 14.83. The steeper the slope, the more the shortcut overstates the angle.

### What students practise

Rise over run, converting a gradient to a percent and to an angle with inverse tangent, and carrying an uncertainty (801 to 811 m) through to the answer: 7.57% to 7.67%.

Younger students compare "steep" and "gentle" with simple ratios; senior students meet the small-angle approximation properly.

### Why the run matters

The road zigzags, so it is more than three times as long as the straight line and its grade is far gentler. Both numbers are correct; they answer different questions.

Real maths starts with choosing which question you are asking.

## The same Pinnacle Road numbers, from Year 5 to Year 12

Good data can carry a whole maths course. Here is how the two measurements above, 806 metres of rise and 10,574.5 metres of road, become tasks at different stages.

**Tasks built on the Pinnacle Road figures (our worked answers)**

| Stage | Question | Worked answer |
|---|---|---|
| Years 5 and 6 | Walking at 4 km/h, how long would the road take? | 10.57 km / 4 km/h, about 2 hours 39 minutes |
| Years 7 and 8 | How many metres higher for each kilometre of road? | 806 / 10.57, about 76 m per km |
| Years 7 and 8 | Write the grade as "1 in something" | 10,574.5 / 806, about 1 in 13 |
| Years 9 and 10 | A cyclist averages 12 km/h. How fast do they climb? | About 53 minutes, so about 15 m of height per minute |
| Years 11 and 12 | Why is the average grade not the steepest grade? | An average spreads the climb evenly; real roads have steeper and gentler stretches |

### Why one data set helps

When a learner meets the same numbers in a new form each year, the earlier work stops being a forgotten topic and becomes a tool. A Year 9 student who once found the walking time already trusts the 10.57 km figure.

It also shows that units and rounding matter: 10,574.5 metres and 10.6 kilometres are the same road described at different precisions.

### Averages and rates of change

The last question leads to a big idea in senior maths. A 7.62% average grade hides the variation along the road, just as an average speed hides stops and starts. Calculus describes how a rate changes from point to point.

Students who have walked or driven a steep road understand this quickly, because they have felt it.
