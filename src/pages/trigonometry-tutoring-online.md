## Which high school course teaches trigonometry?

Usually two of them. The Common Core high school standards put right-triangle trigonometry in the geometry category and the trigonometric functions in the functions category, so most students meet sine twice: once in Geometry and again in Algebra 2 or Precalculus.

**Trigonometry in the Common Core high school standards (thecorestandards.org, read 31 October 2026; summaries ours, quoted words in quotation marks)**

| Code | Strand | What the standard asks | Marked (+)? |
|---|---|---|---|
| G-SRT.C.6 | Geometry | By similarity, "side ratios in right triangles are properties of the angles in the triangle", which defines the trig ratios | No |
| G-SRT.C.7 | Geometry | Explain and use the link between sine and cosine of complementary angles | No |
| G-SRT.C.8 | Geometry | Solve right triangles "in applied problems" with trig ratios and the Pythagorean Theorem | No |
| G-SRT.D.9 to D.11 | Geometry | Area as 1/2 ab sin(C); prove and use the Laws of Sines and Cosines | Yes |
| F-TF.A.1 and A.2 | Functions | Radian measure; the unit circle extends trig functions "to all real numbers" | No |
| F-TF.A.3 and A.4 | Functions | Exact values for pi/3, pi/4 and pi/6; symmetry and periodicity | Yes |

### What the (+) means

The standards document explains the mark on page 57: "Additional mathematics that students should learn in order to take advanced courses such as calculus, advanced statistics, or discrete mathematics is indicated by (+)". Standards without it are meant for every college-ready student.

In practice, a student heading for calculus needs the (+) items too: exact values from special triangles, inverse functions, the Laws of Sines and Cosines and the addition formulas. That is where most of our trigonometry tutoring hours go.

### Why triangles come first

The geometry introduction says "The definitions of sine, cosine, and tangent for acute angles are founded on right triangles and similarity". Two similar right triangles share the same angle and the same ratios, whatever their size, and that is why sin 35&deg; is a single number.

A student who has seen that argument does not need SOH CAH TOA as a spell. They know the ratio is fixed by the angle, and the memory aid becomes a label for something they already understand.

## How do you solve a right triangle with trigonometry?

Name the angle you know, label the sides from that angle (opposite, adjacent, hypotenuse), pick the one ratio that links what you have to what you want, and solve. Here are four of our own examples at Geometry level.

**Right-triangle examples (ours; answers rounded to two decimal places)**

| Problem | Sides from the angle | Ratio chosen | Working and answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| A 20-foot ladder leans on a wall at 70&deg; to the ground. How high does it reach? | Hypotenuse 20, want opposite | Sine | height = 20 sin 70&deg; = 18.79 feet |
| A tree's shadow is 30 feet long when the sun is 35&deg; above the horizon. How tall is the tree? | Adjacent 30, want opposite | Tangent | height = 30 tan 35&deg; = 21.01 feet |
| A right triangle has legs 8 and 15. Find the smaller acute angle. | Opposite 8, adjacent 15 | Inverse tangent | angle = tan-1(8/15) = 28.07&deg;; hypotenuse 17 |
| Show that sin 25&deg; equals cos 65&deg;. | The same triangle, read from each acute angle | Complementary angles (G-SRT.C.7) | Both are 0.4226, because the side opposite 25&deg; is adjacent to 65&deg; |

### The step students skip

Labeling the sides from the chosen angle is the step most errors come from. "Opposite" and "adjacent" are not fixed names for sides; they change when you look from the other acute angle. In the 8-15-17 triangle, the side of length 8 is opposite one angle and adjacent to the other.

We have students mark the angle with a dot and write O, A and H on the sides every time, until they no longer need to. It takes ten seconds and removes most wrong-ratio mistakes.

### Check with a second fact

Every right-triangle answer can be checked another way. For the ladder, the foot of the ladder is 20 cos 70&deg; = 6.84 feet from the wall, and 6.84 squared plus 18.79 squared comes to about 400, which is 20 squared.

For the 8-15-17 triangle, the two acute angles must add to 90&deg;, so the other angle is 61.93&deg;. Small checks like these are what make a student confident on a test without anyone marking the work.

## How do you learn the unit circle without memorizing it?

Learn three triangles and four sign rules, then rebuild any point. Standard F-TF.A.1 defines a radian as "the length of the arc on the unit circle subtended by the angle", so the circle and radians arrive together.

### Radians as a length

On a circle of radius 1, an angle of 1 radian cuts off an arc of length 1. A full turn is the whole circumference, 2&pi;, so 360&deg; equals 2&pi; radians and 180&deg; equals &pi;. Every conversion follows from that one line.

Our example: 225&deg; is 225/180 of &pi;, which is 5&pi;/4. It is &pi;/4 past the half turn, so it sits in quadrant III, with reference angle 45&deg;.

### Rebuilding a point

The 45&deg; triangle on the unit circle has both legs &radic;2/2. In quadrant III both coordinates are negative, so the point at 5&pi;/4 is (&minus;&radic;2/2, &minus;&radic;2/2). Cosine is the x coordinate and sine is the y coordinate, so both equal &minus;&radic;2/2 and the tangent is 1.

The same three steps, reference angle, triangle, signs, give every special value. Students who learn the steps can answer for 7&pi;/6 or 5&pi;/3 just as quickly as for angles they drilled.

**Signs of sine, cosine and tangent by quadrant (ours, from the coordinates of the unit circle)**

| Quadrant | Angles in radians | sin (y) | cos (x) | tan (y/x) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | 0 to &pi;/2 | Positive | Positive | Positive |
| II | &pi;/2 to &pi; | Positive | Negative | Negative |
| III | &pi; to 3&pi;/2 | Negative | Negative | Positive |
| IV | 3&pi;/2 to 2&pi; | Negative | Positive | Negative |

Our identity example for F-TF.C.8: if sin &theta; = &minus;3/5 and &theta; is in quadrant III, the Pythagorean identity sin2&theta; + cos2&theta; = 1 gives cos2&theta; = 1 &minus; 9/25 = 16/25, so cos &theta; = &plusmn;4/5. Quadrant III makes cosine negative, so cos &theta; = &minus;4/5, and tan &theta; = (&minus;3/5)/(&minus;4/5) = 3/4. The identity gives the size; the quadrant gives the sign.

## How are trigonometric functions used to model real situations?

Anything that repeats can be modeled with a sine or cosine. Standard F-TF.B.5 asks students to choose a trig function for a situation with a given amplitude, frequency and midline. Our Ferris wheel example shows how each number in the equation comes from the story.

**A Ferris wheel modeled with cosine (our example): diameter 100 feet, center 60 feet above the ground, one turn every 8 minutes, rider starts at the bottom**

| Feature | From the story | In h(t) = 60 &minus; 50 cos(&pi;t/4) |
|---|---|---|
| Midline | Height of the center | 60 |
| Amplitude | Radius, half the diameter | 50 |
| Period | One turn every 8 minutes | 2&pi; divided by &pi;/4 is 8 |
| Starting point | Rider begins at the lowest point | Minus cosine starts at its minimum: h(0) = 10 |
| Check | Top of the wheel after half a turn | h(4) = 60 + 50 = 110 |

### Solving with the model

When is the rider 85 feet up? Set 60 &minus; 50 cos(&pi;t/4) = 85, so cos(&pi;t/4) = &minus;1/2. On one turn, &pi;t/4 = 2&pi;/3 or 4&pi;/3, giving t = 8/3 and t = 16/3 minutes, about 2.67 and 5.33 minutes.

Two answers in one turn is the point. The rider passes 85 feet on the way up and again on the way down, and a student who stops at the first answer has missed half the question.

### The same idea without a story

Our equation example: solve 2 sin x &minus; 1 = 0 for 0 &le; x < 2&pi;. Then sin x = 1/2. The reference angle is &pi;/6, and sine is positive in quadrants I and II, so x = &pi;/6 or x = 5&pi;/6.

A calculator's inverse sine gives only &pi;/6. Standard F-TF.B.6 is about exactly that: inverse functions exist only on a restricted domain, so the other solutions come from the circle, not the button.

**check_trig.py (our example)** (Python 3)
```python3
import math

# Ferris wheel: h(t) = 60 - 50 cos(pi t / 4)
h = lambda t: 60 - 50 * math.cos(math.pi * t / 4)
print([round(h(t), 2) for t in (0, 2, 4, 8/3, 16/3)])

# Law of Cosines: sides 7 and 9 with a 60 degree angle between them
c = math.sqrt(7**2 + 9**2 - 2 * 7 * 9 * math.cos(math.radians(60)))
area = 0.5 * 7 * 9 * math.sin(math.radians(60))
print(round(c, 2), round(area, 2))
```
Output: [10.0, 60.0, 110.0, 85.0, 85.0], then 8.19 27.28. The third side is &radic;67 because 49 + 81 &minus; 63 = 67, and the area uses 1/2 ab sin(C) from G-SRT.D.9. Checking a hand answer with three lines of code is a habit we teach, never a replacement for the working.

## What is the fastest way to learn trigonometry for beginners?

Learn it in the order the ideas depend on each other, and do not move on until the previous step is automatic. This is the order our tutors use for a student starting from scratch.

1. Similar triangles and the three ratios. Why a ratio depends only on the angle; labeling sides from an angle.
2. Solving right triangles. Missing sides, then missing angles with inverse functions; angles of elevation and depression.
3. Special triangles. The 30-60-90 and 45-45-90 triangles, built from a square and an equilateral triangle, not memorized.
4. Radians and the unit circle. Arc length, conversions, reference angles and signs by quadrant.
5. Graphs. Sine and cosine as the unit circle unrolled; amplitude, period, midline and phase shift.
6. Equations and identities. The Pythagorean identity first, then solving on an interval, then addition formulas.
7. Non-right triangles. The Laws of Sines and Cosines, including the ambiguous case.

**The trigonometry mistakes we correct most often (ours)**

| Mistake | What it looks like | The fix we teach |
|---|---|---|
| Calculator in the wrong mode | sin 30 gives &minus;0.99 instead of 0.5 | Check the mode before every test; know sin 30&deg; = 1/2 as a sense check |
| Wrong ratio | Uses cosine for the opposite side | Mark the angle, label O, A, H, then choose |
| Sign lost | cos 5&pi;/4 written as positive | Find the quadrant before the value |
| One solution only | Stops at x = &pi;/6 | Ask which other quadrant shares the sign |
| Ambiguous case missed | One triangle found where two exist | Sketch both possible triangles for side-side-angle |

The last row is in the standards too. The geometry introduction notes that the Laws of Sines and Cosines "yield two possible solutions in the ambiguous case, illustrating that Side-Side-Angle is not a congruence criterion." A quick sketch of both triangles is the habit that catches it.

## Trigonometry tutoring: common questions

### Is there trigonometry tutoring near me?

Our trigonometry tutoring is live online, so location does not matter. Students join by video from home and work with the same teacher each week, in a group of 15 to 20 or one to one.

### What grade is trigonometry taught in?

Usually grade 10 for right-triangle trigonometry in Geometry, then grades 11 or 12 for trigonometric functions in Algebra 2 or Precalculus. Course order varies by district, and some states use their own standards.

### Is trigonometry hard?

Right-triangle trigonometry is usually manageable. The unit circle, radians and graphs feel harder because the same idea appears in new forms, which is why we teach the triangle, the circle and the graph together.

### How do I memorize the unit circle?

Learn the 30-60-90 and 45-45-90 triangles and the signs in each quadrant instead. With a reference angle, a triangle and a sign, any special value can be rebuilt in seconds, and nothing has to be memorized point by point.

### Is trigonometry on the AP Precalculus exam?

Yes. Unit 3, Trigonometric and Polar Functions, makes up 30 to 35 percent of the multiple-choice section according to the College Board's Course and Exam Description, and one free-response question models a periodic context. AP Central lists the 2027 exam for May 11, 2027.

### Is trigonometry on the SAT?

Yes, a small part. The College Board lists right triangles and trigonometry as one of four topics in the Geometry and Trigonometry domain of the SAT math section.

### Should my calculator be in degrees or radians?

Whichever the question uses. If angles are written with a degree symbol use degrees; if they are written with pi or as plain real numbers use radians. Check the mode before every test.
