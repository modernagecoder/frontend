In short

For grades K to 5, AI literacy is not about using chatbots. It is understanding that AI is a non-human tool that produces outputs from patterns in data, spotting where AI appears in daily life, telling rule-following machines from ones that learn, and checking AI answers against trusted sources. Coding and data activities build all of it.

What it means

What AI literacy means for a child in kindergarten to fifth grade

AI literacy is a newer idea than computer literacy, and the two are easy to confuse. The difference matters most for young children, who are surrounded by AI long before they could use a chatbot.

A working definition

TeachAI, an initiative whose steering committee is made up of non-profit organizations working across education, workforce and technology, puts it this way: "AI literacy represents the technical knowledge, durable skills, and future-ready attitudes required to thrive in a world influenced by AI."

For a six-year-old, the technical knowledge is small but real: a smart speaker does not understand them the way a friend does; it matches sounds to patterns it was trained on.

Not the same as computer literacy

The K-12 Computer Science Framework separates computer science from computer literacy, which it says "focuses on the use of existing technologies and computer programs like word processing and spreadsheets".

AI literacy sits closer to computer science. A child who can only use an AI tool is not AI literate; a child who can explain, in their own simple words, how it reaches an answer and why it might be wrong is on the way.

The basic level

What the AILit framework expects at its basic level

The AILit Framework is a joint initiative of the European Commission and the OECD, developed with support from CodeAI, the organisation formerly called Code.org. It sets out learner expectations at three levels, from "Basic to Intermediate and Advanced", and says these "do not correspond to specific age ranges or grade levels". The basic level is the natural starting point for elementary students.

Selected basic-level learner expectations, quoted from Empowering Learners for the Age of AI (OECD / European Union, 2026)
CompetenceBasic-level expectationClassroom idea in the framework
Engage with AI 1: recognise AI’s role"Learners can identify where different types of AI are present in their everyday lives."An "AI or Not" game sorting smart home devices, social media and apps
Engage with AI 2: describe how AI works"Learners know AI is a non-human tool that produces outputs based on patterns of data and lacks authentic context or understanding."Spotting misleading phrases such as "AI thinks" and "AI understands"
Engage with AI 3: evaluate outputs"Learners understand why AI-generated outputs should be verified" and how to check themComparing an AI tool’s solution to a math problem with the method the teacher taught
Manage AI 2: choose an approach"some types of AI systems can be programmed with specific rules to accomplish tasks, while others can learn patterns from data."Directing a classmate playing a robot to sort pictures of trash
Shape AI 3: data and design"Learners identify examples of data that might be used to train AI systems."Deciding what data would train a tool that sorts recycling

The idea under all of them

The framework’s first knowledge statement is four words long: "AI is not human." Almost everything an elementary child needs follows from that one sentence, said often and shown in many ways.

Language is part of it. When a child says the computer "knows" or "wants" something, a gentle correction helps: it was trained to match patterns, and it can be wrong without knowing it.

Built for families too

The framework lists parents among its audiences, alongside teachers and policymakers, and its draft, published in May 2025, drew on "Insights from more than 2,000 individuals" before the final version.

Because it is international, it asks that classroom activities be adapted to "local contexts, guidance and legislation". In the US, that means following your district’s rules on which AI tools, if any, children may use.

Source: Empowering Learners for the Age of AI: An AI Literacy Framework for Primary and Secondary Education (OECD / European Union, 2026), read 17 October 2026. The framework also notes that it contributes to the Innovative Domain of the PISA 2029 Assessment.

US standards

Where AI ideas already sit in US elementary computer science standards

The K-12 Computer Science Framework, which informs state and district computer science decisions, has no separate AI concept among its five core concepts, but its data strand already covers the foundations for grades K to 5: collecting data, and using it to make predictions.

Data and prediction statements for the elementary grades, in the framework’s wording (k12cs.org, read 17 October 2026)
SubconceptBy the end of grade 2By the end of grade 5
Collection"Everyday digital devices collect and display data over time.""People select digital tools for the collection of data based on what is being observed and how the data will be used."
Inference and models"Data can be used to make inferences or predictions about the world.""The accuracy of inferences and predictions is related to how realistically data is represented."

Why this is AI literacy

An AI system is, at heart, a machine that makes predictions from data. A second grader who predicts tomorrow’s lunch choice from last week’s tally chart is doing, by hand, what a recommendation system does at scale.

The grade 5 statement adds the crucial warning: predictions are only as good as the data, with accuracy affected by factors "such as the amount and relevance of data collected". That is the root of every conversation about AI bias.

What comes next, in middle school

Code.org’s AI Discoveries, the course it now recommends for middle school, is listed for grades 6, 7 and 8. Elementary families who want AI ideas earlier can build them through the data activities on this page.

That makes the elementary years a quiet opportunity. Children who already understand data and patterns arrive in middle school ready to reason about AI rather than just use it.

Sources: K-12 CS Framework, statements by grade band and Code.org AI Discoveries, read 17 October 2026. We are not connected with either.

Try it at home

Rules or learning? A twelve-card sorting activity

This activity takes ten minutes and works from about first grade. Write each item on a card. Your child sorts them into three piles: follows set rules, learns from examples, or not a machine decision at all. The AILit framework uses calculators, traffic lights and thermostats as its examples of rule-following technology.

Twelve cards with suggested answers (ours; how a specific product works can vary)

The conversation that matters

Disagreement is the point. If your child puts the video app under rules, ask how it could know what they like. If they put the calculator under learning, ask whether it ever surprises them.

End with the framework’s key sentence in kid language: a machine that learns is copying patterns from examples, and if the examples are odd, its answers will be odd too.

A free classroom game to follow

The AILit site lists AI for Oceans as an exemplar: "a web app learning game that introduces young learners to the concepts of how AI systems are trained, and how training can introduce bias, and potentially produce errors." It is listed for primary education, "Age 7 and up", in an activity of 60 minutes.

Children train a sorter on pictures, then see it make mistakes. It is a natural next step after the card sort, ideally with an adult nearby.

Sources: the Manage AI 2 learner expectations in the AILit Framework and its AI for Oceans exemplar page, read 17 October 2026. The twelve cards are ours.

A learning program

A tiny program that learns from examples, and gets things wrong

For fourth and fifth graders who have started Python, and for parents who want to see the idea in action, this is a program we use in class. It learns which words go with happy and sad sentences from four examples, then guesses. Its third guess is the lesson.

Training

The loop counts words in four labelled examples. That is all the program ever knows.

Predicting

For a new sentence, it adds up word counts for each label and picks the bigger total.

Being wrong

With too few examples it answers confidently anyway, and it can never say "I do not know".

Questions we ask children

Why did it say "my kite is broken" was happy? Children who answer "because of the word is" have understood that AI follows patterns in its data, not meaning.

How could we fix it? Add more examples, especially sad ones that use everyday words. Run it again. That is the framework’s point about the amount and relevance of data, discovered by a ten-year-old.

Why a program and not a chatbot

A child who builds a tiny guesser sees every step. Nothing is hidden, so nothing seems magical, and the idea that big AI systems work on the same principle, at enormous scale, becomes believable.

It also keeps the lesson inside our own code, so no child needs an account on any AI service to learn how AI works.

Safety and accounts

AI tools, accounts and young children: our rules, and questions to ask a school

Many AI services set a minimum age in their terms of service, and some are for adults only. Those rules change, so we do not list them here; check the terms of any tool before your child uses it.

How we handle it

We never ask a child to create an account on a service whose terms do not allow their age. Elementary students in our classes learn with programs they build themselves, child-appropriate activities and demonstrations run by the teacher.

When a teacher demonstrates an AI tool, the lesson is about checking it: find one thing it got right, one thing it got wrong, and how we know.

Five questions for your child’s school

Which AI tools, if any, do students in this grade use, and are they school-managed accounts? What does the district policy say about AI for elementary students? Is any student work or data shared with an AI service?

How is AI literacy taught, rather than just AI use? And how should my child say when they have used AI for schoolwork? Schools that have thought these through will have ready answers.

Grade by grade

Four stages of AI understanding in elementary school

An outline, not a schedule. Many children reach stage 2 at home long before school mentions AI.

Stages of AI understanding, grades K to 5 (ours)
StageA child at this stage says
1. Noticing"The speaker heard me and played my song."
2. Not a person"It does not really understand; it matches what I say to things it learned."
3. Learns from examples"If you show it lots of cat pictures, it gets better at finding cats."
4. Can be wrong"It might be wrong if its examples were wrong, so I should check."

For parents

Stage 2 is the big one. Saying "it was trained" instead of "it knows" at home helps more than any app.

Ask your child to catch an AI mistake this week, perhaps a wrong song or a silly suggestion. Talk about why it happened.

For kids

AI is a tool, like a very fast pattern-matcher. It is not your friend and it is not always right.

If an answer matters, check it with a book, a teacher or a grown-up.

Questions from parents and teachers

AI literacy for elementary students: common questions

What is AI literacy for elementary students?

Knowing that AI is a tool and not a person, that it produces answers from patterns in data it was trained on, where it shows up in daily life, and that its answers need checking. For grades K to 5 it is mostly about understanding, not using chatbots.

What age should children learn about AI?

The ideas can start in kindergarten with games about patterns and sorting. Our live AI literacy course starts at 8, which is usually third grade, and runs to 14.

Should elementary students use ChatGPT or other chatbots?

Many AI services set minimum ages in their terms, and some are for adults only. Check the terms and your school district policy first. Our classes never ask a child to create an account on a service that does not allow their age.

What is the AILit Framework?

An AI literacy framework for primary and secondary education from the European Commission and the OECD, developed with support from CodeAI, formerly Code.org. It has four domains: Engage with AI, Create with AI, Manage AI and Shape AI.

Do US elementary standards include AI?

Not usually by name, but the K-12 Computer Science Framework covers its foundations: by the end of grade 2, using data to make predictions, and by grade 5, understanding that prediction accuracy depends on the data.

How can I teach my child about AI at home?

Try the rules or learning card sort on this page: calculators and thermostats follow rules, while voice assistants and video suggestions learn from examples. Then talk about why a machine that learns can be wrong.

Is AI for Oceans suitable for young children?

The AILit Framework lists it as an exemplar for primary education, age 7 and up, in a 60-minute activity that shows how AI is trained and how training can introduce bias and errors.

Does my child need to code to learn AI literacy?

No coding is needed to begin. Coding helps from about grade 4, because building a tiny program that learns from examples makes the ideas concrete.

What times are classes for US families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible. Choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

How much do AI literacy classes cost?

The first lesson costs nothing. Classes after that are paid monthly, and the fees section on this page shows the amount in US dollars.
