Cambridge IGCSE Maths 0580 help

Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics, syllabus code 0580, is sat by pupils in UK independent schools, by home-educated teenagers entering as private candidates and by international schools across six administrative zones. Every candidate sits two papers, and one of the two is taken with no calculator at all. Which pair a pupil sits, Core or Extended, sets the grades on offer before anything is written. This page sets out the four papers from the syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027, the accuracy rules that quietly cost marks, and how we teach 0580 live online.

The short answer

Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 is a two-paper qualification from Cambridge International. Core candidates sit Paper 1 (no calculator) and Paper 3 (calculator), each 1 hour 30 minutes and 80 marks, and can be awarded grades C to G. Extended candidates sit Paper 2 (no calculator) and Paper 4 (calculator), each 2 hours and 100 marks, and can be awarded A* to E. Each paper is half the qualification. The four papers

How is Cambridge IGCSE Maths 0580 structured?

Four papers exist, and every candidate sits exactly two of them: a non-calculator paper and a calculator paper at the same tier.

The 0580 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027 says it plainly: "All candidates take two components." A Core candidate sits Paper 1 and Paper 3; an Extended candidate sits Paper 2 and Paper 4. The two papers at each tier are equal in length and in weight, so the non-calculator paper is worth exactly as much as the calculator one. Every paper mixes structured questions, split into parts such as (a) and (b)(i), with unstructured questions that leave the method to the candidate.

Nine topics, one content list

The syllabus lists nine topics for every candidate: number; algebra and graphs; coordinate geometry; geometry; mensuration; trigonometry; transformations and vectors; probability; and statistics. Extended content contains all of Core plus further material, so an Extended candidate is examined on the whole of the Core list as well.

Cambridge states the content "is not presented in a teaching order", which leaves a school or a tutor to sequence it. Questions may also draw on more than one topic at once.

What goes into the exam room

For both tiers the syllabus asks for a pair of compasses, a protractor and a ruler. Tracing paper is allowed on all four papers, but candidates "cannot bring their own tracing paper but may request it during the examination."

For Papers 3 and 4 a scientific calculator is needed, and one with trigonometric functions is strongly recommended. Algebraic or graphical calculators are not permitted on any paper.

Core or Extended

Should my child sit Core or Extended 0580?

Cambridge ties the choice to the grade a pupil is expected to reach, and each tier has a ceiling or a floor.

The entry rule is written into the syllabus. Candidates "who are expected to achieve a grade D or below" should sit Core, Papers 1 and 3, and "will be eligible for grades C to G." Candidates expected to achieve a grade C or above should sit Extended, Papers 2 and 4, and "will be eligible for grades A* to E." The two ranges overlap only at C, D and E, which is where most entry arguments happen.

The last two rows are easy to skip and they matter. On Core papers most marks reward recalling and carrying out a technique. On Extended papers more than half of the marks reward analysing a problem, choosing a strategy and communicating it, so an Extended pupil who can do every method in isolation can still struggle when the question does not say which method to use.

When Core is the honest entry

A pupil who is not yet secure on algebraic manipulation, and whose mock marks sit well below the Extended range, can do better on Core: a secure C is worth more than an Extended paper that ends below E and leaves the pupil ungraded.

A Core entry closes the door to A* to B in that series. Cambridge allows retakes of the whole qualification "as many times as they want to", so a later Extended entry remains possible.

When Extended is worth the stretch

Any pupil thinking about A level maths, sciences or a numerate sixth-form course needs grades that only Extended can give. Cambridge itself says candidates aiming for grades A* to C should study the Extended content.

The real question is whether the pupil can handle unstructured problems without a calculator. Our first class tests exactly that, with problems we wrote, before any family conversation with a school.

The accuracy rules

How many significant figures does IGCSE 0580 want?

Three, unless the question says otherwise, and the rounding has to wait until the very end.

For Papers 3 and 4 the syllabus gives one standing instruction: "Candidates should give non-exact numerical answers correct to 3 significant figures, or 1 decimal place for angles in degrees, unless a different level of accuracy is specified in the question." It then adds the sentence that decides accuracy marks: "To earn accuracy marks, candidates should avoid rounding figures until they have their final answer." Where a rounded answer is used again later in the question, the syllabus asks candidates to use the value before it was rounded.

Worked example, ours

What does an early rounding error actually cost?

A two-part problem we wrote at Extended calculator level, solved twice: once with the rounding rule, once without it.

Our problem. Triangle ABC has a right angle at B, with AB = 7.4 cm and BC = 5.1 cm. (a) Calculate AC. (b) A semicircle is drawn with AC as its diameter. Calculate the area of the semicircle.

  1. Part (a), by Pythagoras: AC2 = 7.42 + 5.12 = 54.76 + 26.01 = 80.77, so AC = 8.987213... cm. The answer line takes 8.99 cm, correct to 3 significant figures.
  2. Part (b), the careful way: the radius is AC ÷ 2, so the semicircle's area is π × (AC ÷ 2)2 ÷ 2. Since AC2 is exactly 80.77, this equals π × 80.77 ÷ 8 = 31.718... cm2, which is 31.7 cm2.
  3. Part (b), the careless way: starting from the rounded 8.99 gives π × 4.4952 ÷ 2 = 31.737... cm2, which also rounds to 31.7. Here the slip happens to survive.
  4. Now change one instruction: ask for the area of the full circle on diameter AC. Unrounded, π × 80.77 ÷ 4 = 63.4366..., so 63.4 cm2. From the rounded 8.99, π × 4.4952 = 63.4759..., so 63.5 cm2. The third figure has moved, and the accuracy mark goes with it.
  5. The habit that prevents it: keep AC2 = 80.77 or the full calculator value in memory, write the rounded 8.99 on the answer line for part (a), and never type 8.99 back in.

The lesson is not that rounding always loses marks. It is that the loss is unpredictable, which makes it impossible to check for in the exam room. A pupil who always carries the stored value never has to wonder. We drill it until reaching for the memory key is automatic, because the syllabus rule is the same on every calculator paper.

Without a calculator

How do you prepare for the 0580 non-calculator paper?

By treating it as its own subject: exact values, index laws and surds done by hand, quickly, with a check at the end.

Our second example, Extended non-calculator level. (a) Work out 82/3 × 16−1/4. (b) Simplify (√45 + √20) ÷ √5.

Part (a), index laws

A fractional power is a root followed by a power. 81/3 = 2, so 82/3 = 22 = 4. A negative power is a reciprocal: 161/4 = 2, so 16−1/4 = 1/2.

The product is 4 × 1/2 = 2. The check: both numbers are powers of 2, so the whole expression is 22 × 2−1 = 21.

Part (b), surds

Split each root around a square factor: √45 = √9 × √5 = 3√5, and √20 = √4 × √5 = 2√5. Together they make 5√5.

Dividing by √5 leaves 5. An integer answer from a surd expression is a common sign that the working is right.

Entries and series

When can 0580 be sat, and what has changed?

Two series a year in the UK, a syllabus that runs to 2027, and a successor for 2028 to 2030 that changes nothing teachers need to reteach.

The 0980 exclusion catches some UK families out. 0980 is the version of the same content graded 9 to 1, used by schools that want numbered grades; it cannot be sat alongside 0580 in the same series. A pupil switching schools in Year 10 should check which code the new school enters before relying on any past papers.

Version changes inside 2025 to 2027

Version 2, published February 2024, clarified the term prism, the expectations for reciprocal and exponential graphs in E2.11, and the word random in probability. Version 3 adjusted text alignment only.

None of these changes the papers. The syllabus still tells families to "Check you are using the syllabus for the year the candidate is taking the exam."

What the 2025 revision removed from Core

Adding and subtracting vectors, multiplying a vector by a scalar, and data collection all left the Core content when the syllabus was revised. Cambridge expects data collection to stay part of a course, but it is no longer examined.

A Core pupil can therefore set aside some older Core questions on vector arithmetic. An Extended pupil cannot: vectors remain in Extended content.

Questions about 0580

What families ask about IGCSE Maths 0580

How many papers are there in IGCSE Maths 0580?

Four papers exist and each candidate sits two. Core candidates take Paper 1 and Paper 3, and Extended candidates take Paper 2 and Paper 4. At each tier one paper bans calculators and the other requires one, and each is half the qualification.

Is there a non-calculator paper in IGCSE Maths 0580?

Yes. The syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027 states that calculators are not allowed for Paper 1 and Paper 2. So every candidate, Core or Extended, sits one paper without a calculator.

What is the highest grade on Core 0580?

Grade C. Core candidates are eligible for grades C to G, while Extended candidates are eligible for A* to E. A pupil who needs an A or A* must be entered for Extended.

How long are the 0580 papers?

Paper 1 and Paper 3, the Core papers, are each 1 hour 30 minutes and 80 marks. Paper 2 and Paper 4, the Extended papers, are each 2 hours and 100 marks.

How many significant figures should I give in 0580?

Three significant figures for non-exact answers, or one decimal place for angles in degrees, unless the question asks for something else. Cambridge also says not to round until the final answer, and to use pi from the calculator or 3.142.

What is the difference between 0580 and 0980?

0980 is Cambridge IGCSE (9 to 1) Mathematics, graded with numbers instead of letters. The two cannot be taken in the same exam series, so check which code a school enters before choosing past papers.

Can a home-educated child sit 0580?

Yes. The syllabus says private candidates can enter. The family finds an exam centre that accepts private candidates and makes the entry through that centre, which sets its own arrangements.

Is IGCSE Maths 0580 hard?

It depends on the tier and on hand arithmetic. Extended papers put more than half their marks on analysing and communicating, which is harder than recalling methods, and the non-calculator paper exposes weak fractions and indices quickly.

When do your 0580 classes run?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Every time is shown in UK time.

Are you part of Cambridge International?

No. Modern Age Coders is an independent teaching company with no connection to Cambridge University Press and Assessment. We teach to the published syllabus and do not reproduce Cambridge papers.

Can you guarantee an A* in 0580?

No, and nobody honestly can. We can teach the full Extended content, train accuracy and non-calculator speed, and tell you plainly how far a pupil is from the grade they want.
