How can a Winnipeg student get a live math tutor?

Modern Age Coders teaches math live online to Winnipeg students from Grade 1 to Grade 12 Pre-Calculus Mathematics, one to one or in a group of fifteen to twenty at a single level. Teens usually take High School Mathematics: Algebra to Calculus, and contest fans add Waterloo Math Contests: Gauss to Euclid. We charge nothing for the opening lesson.

Manitoba checks a child’s math at a few fixed points: a classroom-based numeracy assessment in Grade 3, a Grade 7 mathematics assessment of number skills and number sense, and in Grade 12 a provincial test in Essential, Applied or Pre-Calculus Mathematics that counts for 20 per cent of the final grade. In between, the province is piloting two new Grade 9 math courses with more financial literacy. This page explains those steps from Manitoba’s own pages, then teaches a lesson in counting and probability on the City of Winnipeg’s public tree records, works a problem of the kind Pre-Calculus students meet, and covers contests, class times after Manitoba’s clock change, and price.

The early assessments are formative: Manitoba describes them as classroom-based assessment for and as learning, reported to parents to show a child’s strengths and needs. The Grade 12 tests are different. Manitoba says they are administered at the end of each semester, marked locally by teachers according to marking guidelines and training offered by the department, and that they count for 20 per cent of students’ final grades in Applied, Pre-Calculus and Essential Mathematics. Which test a student writes depends on which Grade 12 course they take, so the course choice in Grade 10 and 11 matters.

Grade 9 is changing

Manitoba says two new Grade 9 Mathematics courses, incorporating an enhanced focus on financial literacy, are posted for pilot and voluntary implementation in 2026/27, with full implementation planned for 2027/28. Its Grade 9 list shows them as Course I and Course II, both marked draft.

Five Grade 12 routes

The Grade 12 list includes Essential Mathematics, Applied Mathematics, Pre-Calculus Mathematics, Introduction to Calculus and Advanced Mathematics. Pre-Calculus carries course code 3939 and one credit; Applied is 3903.

What Pre-Calculus covers

Manitoba’s overview says Grade 12 Pre-Calculus learners develop trigonometric reasoning, study logarithms and inverses, and reason about combinatorics by applying the counting principles. The tree lesson below is that last idea, on real data.

Winnipeg data lesson

Two Winnipeg trees at random: what is the chance they share a name?

The City of Winnipeg publishes every public tree it manages as open data. Pre-Calculus students use combinations to turn the species counts into a probability, then compare neighbourhoods. Every count and probability below is ours.

The dataset is the Tree Inventory, which the City describes as a detailed list and map of all public trees located in the City of Winnipeg, including botanical name, common name and precise location. It is maintained by Public Works and updated weekly. Rather than download all of it, we asked the City’s data service for counts grouped by common name and by neighbourhood on 23 October 2026. It reported 305,969 trees under 197 common-name labels.

Now the counting. The number of ways to choose two trees from 305,969 is C(305,969, 2). The number of ways to choose two that share a label is the sum of C(n, 2) over every label, where n is that label’s count. Dividing gives our answer: 0.1102, so two public trees picked at random share a common name about 11 times in 100. The chance that both are green ash alone is 0.0716. The Python takes only a few lines:import json from math import comb with open("by_species.json", encoding="utf-8") as f: counts = [int(row["n"]) for row in json.load(f)] total = sum(counts) pairs = comb(total, 2) same_name = sum(comb(n, 2) for n in counts) print("trees:", total) print("ways to choose two trees:", pairs) print("chance both share a name:", round(same_name / pairs, 4))

What the number means

A low value means a mixed neighbourhood, where two trees are rarely the same kind; a high one means a street planted with mostly one kind. In Southland Park, 452 of 696 trees are green ash, which drives its value above 0.43.

Why combinations, not squares

Squaring each share gives almost the same city-wide answer, because 305,969 is large. On a street of ten trees the difference is big, and only combinations count it correctly, because the second tree cannot be the same tree as the first.

Labels are not species

At least eight labels in the file contain the word elm, from American Elm to Brandon American elm, with different capital letters. Our probability is about matching labels, and a careful student says so rather than claiming a species count.

What we left out on purpose

690 trees carry no neighbourhood, so the neighbourhood table leaves them out, and neighbourhoods with fewer than 500 trees are skipped because a handful of trees can make the value swing wildly. Both choices are ours, and both are written down, which is the habit Grade 12 statistics and probability asks for.

A worked problem, ours

Worked problem: counting the trees on one street

An invented street, written by us at Grade 12 Pre-Calculus level, small enough to count by hand. It uses the three counting ideas Manitoba’s course overview names.

Question. A Winnipeg street has ten boulevard trees: 5 green ash, 3 elm and 2 linden. (a) Two trees are chosen at random. What is the probability they are the same kind? (b) In how many distinct orders could the ten trees stand along the street, if trees of one kind look alike? (c) If three trees are chosen at random, what is the probability that at least one is an ash?

Step 1, count pairs. There are C(10, 2) = 45 ways to choose two trees. Same-kind pairs: C(5, 2) = 10 ash pairs, C(3, 2) = 3 elm pairs and C(2, 2) = 1 linden pair, 14 in all. The probability is 14/45, about 0.311, much higher than the city-wide 0.110 because this street is mostly ash.

Step 2, arrange with repeats. If all ten trees were different there would be 10! orders. Swapping two ash trees changes nothing you can see, so divide by 5! for the ash, 3! for the elm and 2! for the linden: 10! / (5! 3! 2!) = 2,520 distinct orders. This is the permutations with repeated elements idea that trips students who reach for 10! straight away.

Step 3, use the complement. “At least one ash” is the opposite of “no ash”. Choosing three non-ash trees from the five others can be done in C(5, 3) = 10 ways, out of C(10, 3) = 120. So the probability of no ash is 10/120, and of at least one ash, 1 minus 10/120, about 0.917.

Step 4, connect to the data. The city-wide figure of 0.0716 for two green ash trees comes from exactly the step 1 method, with 81,895 in place of 5 and 305,969 in place of 10. The arithmetic is bigger; the idea is identical.

Try it yourself. (1) Add one bur oak to the street and redo part (a). (2) How many distinct orders are there now? (3) Using the city table, estimate the chance that three random Winnipeg trees include no green ash. Bring your answers to the free lesson and we will check them together.

Math and AI

Can a Winnipeg student let an AI do the counting?

It can do the arithmetic. It cannot decide what is being counted.

Ask an AI tool for the chance that two Winnipeg trees match and it may square the shares and call the result exact, or treat “American Elm” and “Brandon American elm” as unrelated without saying so. A student who has counted pairs by hand knows why combinations matter and what a label really measures.

Our teachers ask students to estimate the answer first, then count, then explain the result in a sentence. Older students may use AI tools within the age rules we keep to, and only to check finished work. Work handed in at school always belongs to the student.

Manitoba’s Pre-Calculus course asks students to apply the counting principles; choosing what deserves counting stays a human job. The longer argument is in why careful thinking outlasts any AI shortcut.
