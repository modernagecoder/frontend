## What does it mean to learn AI, at any age?

We split it into three layers. Most courses online teach only the first. Our classes reach the second and third, because that is where understanding starts.

### Use

Knowing what a tool can and cannot do, asking it well, and checking what comes back. For adults this includes the chat assistants used at work. For children it means age-appropriate tools only, and never an adult account.

### Build

Writing a small model yourself in Python: a program that learns from examples, a rule that flags strange values, a guess that improves with data. This is where coding and AI meet, and it is the heart of every course.

### Judge

Deciding whether a result is right, and saying why. A model that flags a year as unusual has not explained anything yet. The judging layer is the one that makes a learner hard to fool, by a machine or a headline.

## Which years were strange at the Oldenburg lock? An anomaly detector, then a human

Stadt Oldenburg publishes how many ships passed its lock each year since 1987. Our teenagers and adults use it to build the simplest kind of anomaly detector, and to learn the most important rule about such systems: a flag is a question, not an answer.

### The data

The dataset Schleuse Oldenburg seit 1987 lists, for each year, ships travelling up (Bergfahrt, coming from the Weser) and down (Talfahrt, heading to the Weser), how many were loaded, the cargo in tonnes, and a total. It is published by Stadt Oldenburg under the Datenlizenz Deutschland Namensnennung 2.0. We downloaded the file on 3 November 2026.

The city notes that there are no figures for 2019, so that row is empty. We keep the file as published and call it schleuse_oldenburg.csv.

### The idea

An anomaly detector learns what normal looks like and flags whatever is far from it. Here, normal is the usual change in the number of ships from one year to the next. For each year we measure how many spreads it sits from the usual change: its z-score.

Anything more than 1.5 spreads away gets flagged. The same idea, with far more data, sits behind systems that flag unusual card payments or a sensor that suddenly reads too high.

**Step 1: learn normal, flag the unusual** (Python)
```python
import csv
from statistics import mean, pstdev

ships = {}
with open("schleuse_oldenburg.csv", encoding="utf-8") as table:
    for row in csv.reader(table, delimiter=";"):
        if row[0].isdigit() and row[7] != "":
            ships[int(row[0])] = int(row[7])        # column 8: ships in total

print("Years with data:", len(ships), "from", min(ships), "to", max(ships))

change = {}
for year in ships:
    if year - 1 in ships:
        change[year] = 100 * (ships[year] - ships[year - 1]) / ships[year - 1]

centre = mean(change.values())
spread = pstdev(change.values())
print("Typical yearly change:", round(centre, 1), "%  spread:", round(spread, 1))

for year in sorted(change):
    z = (change[year] - centre) / spread
    if abs(z) > 1.5:
        print("Unusual:", year, round(change[year], 1), "%  z =", round(z, 1))
```
It prints Years with data: 38 from 1987 to 2025, Typical yearly change: -2.0 % spread: 8.7, then six flags, among them Unusual: 1988 11.5 % z = 1.5, Unusual: 1994 -17.1 % z = -1.7 and Unusual: 2025 13.3 % z = 1.8.

Ship numbers fell by about 2 percent a year on average, from 6,344 in 1987 to 2,671 in 2025. The detector flags 1988, 1994, 2003, 2010, 2024 and 2025. The program cannot say why any of them is unusual. That is the judging layer, and it belongs to the learner.

**Step 2: look closely at one flag** (Python)
```python
with open("schleuse_oldenburg.csv", encoding="utf-8") as table:
    for row in csv.reader(table, delimiter=";"):
        if row[0] in ("1993", "1994", "1995"):
            up, down, total = int(row[1]), int(row[4]), int(row[7])
            print(row[0], "up", up, "+ down", down, "=", up + down, " total column:", total)
```
Run after Step 1. It prints 1993 up 3499 + down 3776 = 7275 total column: 7275, 1994 up 3757 + down 4039 = 7796 total column: 6033 and the same check for 1995, which agrees.

In 1993 and 1995 the ships going up and the ships going down add up exactly to the total. In 1994 they add up to 7,796, but the total column says 6,033. The file does not say which figure is right, so we do not either. What we can say is that the 1994 flag may come from the bookkeeping rather than the canal: measured with the two directions, 1994 would be a rise of about 7 percent, an ordinary year.

**The six flagged years and what a person can find out (our calculation and reading)**

| Year | Change in ships | z-score | What we found |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1988 | +11.5 % | 1.5 | A rise after 1987; nothing odd in the file |
| 1994 | -17.1 % | -1.7 | Up plus down does not match the total |
| 2003 | +14.0 % | 1.8 | A rise; the parts match |
| 2010 | -16.3 % | -1.6 | A real drop: up and down both fell |
| 2024 | -15.8 % | -1.6 | A real drop: up and down both fell |
| 2025 | +13.3 % | 1.8 | A recovery after 2024 |

Data: Stadt Oldenburg (Oldb), Schleuse Oldenburg seit 1987, licence Datenlizenz Deutschland Namensnennung 2.0, downloaded 3 November 2026. The z-scores, the 1.5 threshold and the checks are our own. A second, smaller mismatch exists in 2005, where the two directions add up to 18 ships more than the total. The cargo per ship rose from about 472 tonnes in 1987 to about 510 tonnes in 2025, also our calculation. We make no claim about the causes of any change.

1. **Children, 9 to 12.** Write the ships for six years on cards. Which card looks strange? Now explain how you decided. That explanation is your detector.
2. **Teenagers.** Change the threshold to 2 and then to 1. How many years are flagged each time, and which threshold would you choose for a real alarm?
3. **Adults.** Rebuild the detector on cargo in tonnes instead of ships. Do the same years stand out?
4. **Everyone.** Write one sentence on the difference between "unusual" and "wrong", using 1994 and 2010 as your two examples.
