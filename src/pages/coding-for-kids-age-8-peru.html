Who this page is for

Who are these coding classes for eight year olds for?

Families in Peru with a child of eight who can follow simple spoken English, or who would like them to learn it while building something.

Children who have done some Scratch

Perhaps at school, perhaps alone on a tablet. They can move a sprite but their games fall apart when there are rules to follow. That is exactly where an eight year old needs a teacher.

Complete beginners

Eight is a fine age to start from nothing. Reading is easier than at six, so blocks go faster, and many eight year olds catch up with older beginners within a term.

Families who want English with a purpose

An eight year old who hears "if it touches the edge, bounce" every week learns English the way children pick up a language most easily: because they need it to make their game work.

What changes at eight

What can an eight year old build with code?

Games with rules, quizzes that check answers and stories that branch. The new idea at this age is the decision: a program that does one thing or another depending on what happens.

Projects that work at eight

A catch game. Fruit falls, a basket moves left and right, and each catch adds a point. The child decides what happens when a piece is missed.

A quiz. The program asks five questions about the child's favourite animal and says "Correct!" or "Not quite" for each. Then it gives a badge at the end, which is where today's lesson comes from.

A branching story. The reader chooses a door, and the story goes one of two ways. Children love writing these for younger siblings.

The thinking behind them

Each of these needs a condition: a question the computer can answer with yes or no. Is the fruit touching the basket? Is the answer equal to 12? Did the reader click the red door?

Eight year olds are good at conditions because school is full of them already. If you finish your work, you may read. If it rains, we stay inside. Coding asks them to write those rules down so exactly that a machine can follow them, and that exactness is the real lesson.

A child of eight also starts to keep things: a score, a number of lives, a time left. These are variables, and they make games feel real. By the end of a year most eight year olds in our classes can explain, in short English sentences, what each variable in their game is for.

Third grade in Peru

What changes in 3° de primaria, and where is computing?

Third grade brings more English and slightly less mathematics on the national plan, and still no computing lesson of its own. Here is what MINEDU sets and what it leaves to schools.

The national plan at eight

MINEDU decides a child's grade by age on 31 March of each school year. On its table of normative ages, 3° de primaria lines up with eight, so most eight year olds in Peru are in third grade.

In the CNEB plan of studies, Inglés goes up by one period at third grade, from two weekly periods to three, while Matemática drops from 5 to 4. With 45-minute periods, that is 135 minutes of English a week from third grade instead of 90, by our sum.

Computing, technology and making

Computing is not on that list of areas. The CNEB treats working in virtual environments as a competency that crosses every area, and describes one of its parts as building digital materials "siguiendo un proceso de mejoras sucesivas y retroalimentación": making something, getting feedback, improving it.

Science and technology has its own competency, "Diseña y construye soluciones tecnológicas para resolver problemas". Coding fits both ideas well, which is why many schools add it in their own way. Ask your child's school how much they do; it varies a great deal.

MINEDU's norm for 2026 set a school year of thirty-six teaching weeks. Our classes sit beside that year, not inside it: they continue through the breaks and the summer, and nothing we do counts towards a school grade. We are not connected with MINEDU or with any school, and the only certificate we give is our own certificate of completion.

The right device

Which device does an eight year old need for online coding?

A laptop or desktop computer. Peru goes online mostly by phone, which is fine for chat and video but hard for building a game. INEI's figures show how people actually connect.

What the numbers say

Nine in ten Peruvians who used the internet between April and June 2026 did it on a phone, and fewer than one in five used a laptop. Desktop use has slipped from 16.6% to 14.0% since 2023, while smart televisions and other Wi-Fi devices have climbed to 15.7%.

Children are different. In INEI's age table, only 48.5% of internet users aged 6 to 11 went online by phone in 2026. So for many families the shared laptop, the desktop in the corner or the television is still the child's way onto the internet, far more than for grown-ups.

What it means for your child

For coding, the phone is the wrong tool: blocks are small, there is no room for the mentor's screen, and typing is slow. An eight year old needs a laptop or desktop, a mouse, and a headset with a microphone.

Only 36.4% of Peruvian homes owned a computer of any kind in that 2026 survey, and that 84.7% of households in Lima Metropolitana had internet against 27.1% in rural areas. If your family shares one laptop, flexible timing lets you book the class when it is free.

An original lesson

Why does every player get bronze? A lesson on decisions

This is our lesson for eight and nine year olds who have built a quiz and want it to hand out badges. It shows, in one surprising run, that the order of your questions matters.

The plan

The child's quiz counts stars. The rules they choose are: 5 stars or more wins bronze, 10 or more wins silver, 15 or more wins gold, and fewer than 5 means "keep going".

In Scratch they build it with "if, else" blocks, writing the rules in the order they said them: bronze first, then silver, then gold. They test with 7 stars, get bronze, and are delighted.

The surprise

Then a friend scores 18 stars and also gets bronze. So does someone with 12. The mentor does not explain; we ask the child to read the first rule aloud with the number 18 in it. "Is 18 more than or equal to 5?" Yes. So the program stops right there and says bronze.

The fix is to ask the hardest question first: gold, then silver, then bronze. The computer checks the rules from the top and stops at the first one that is true, so the strictest rule must come first.

Children of eight do this in blocks. We show the same two versions in Python so parents can see it, and so the child sees, perhaps for the first time, that Scratch and typed code are the same idea in two forms.

The real lesson is not about badges. It is that a computer is perfectly obedient and completely literal: it did exactly what the child wrote, not what the child meant. Learning to spot that gap is the most useful habit a young coder can build, and it starts with testing more than one number. Seven stars looked right; eighteen stars told the truth.

  1. Our practice, talk it through. Read the wrong version aloud with 12 stars. Which line does the computer stop at, and why?
  2. Our practice, a new rule. Add a "platinum" badge for 20 stars or more. Where must it go in the list?
  3. Our practice, test the edges. Try exactly 5, 10 and 15 stars. Do they get the badge you expected? What would change if the rules said "more than" instead of "more than or equal to"?
  4. Our practice, in Scratch. Rebuild the right version with nested if, else blocks and test it with four different scores.

Between classes

How should an eight year old practise coding at home?

A little, often, and on their own project. Here is the routine we suggest to families; it is ours, not a school rule, and you should bend it around your week.

  1. Two short sessions a week. Twenty minutes of tinkering on the game from class is worth more than an hour of new tutorials.
  2. One change at a time. Change a single block, run the game, and see what happened. Eight year olds who change five things at once cannot tell which one broke it.
  3. Keep a bug list. On paper, write down anything that went wrong and what fixed it. Bring it to the next class; mentors love these lists.
  4. Show someone. A grandparent, a cousin, a friend from school. Explaining a game out loud is where the English and the logic both settle.
  5. Stop while it is fun. Finish on something that works, so the child wants to come back.

If your child wants more, the next step is usually a second project in the same tool rather than a new tool. A child who has built three Scratch games with scores and badges is far better prepared for Python at nine or ten than one who has tried five different apps for a week each. When that time comes, our page on clases de programación para niños en inglés explains, in Spanish, how the English of code becomes an advantage.

AI at eight

Can an eight year old learn about AI?

Yes, as a thing to understand rather than a thing to ask for answers. The big chatbots are not for this age, and we keep it that way.

The rules are clear

OpenAI's terms for ChatGPT open with "You must be at least 13 years old". An eight year old is five years short, so our classes never use it with children of this age.

What we use instead

Teachable Machine, a Google tool that trains in the browser without signing in. A child shows it ten pictures of a thumbs-up and ten of a thumbs-down, and watches it learn, and fail, from what they gave it.

The habit that lasts

When the model gets a picture wrong, the child asks why. Too few examples? All taken in the same light? That question is the same one they asked about the bronze badge: what did I actually tell it?

Each tool and the age its maker allows is listed, with the maker's own words, in our AI tools age guide.

Progress at eight

How will I know my eight year old is progressing?

By what they can build and explain. These five milestones are ours; mentors use them to decide what comes next.

MilestoneUsually byBuildsSays
1. Moves with purposeMonth 1A sprite that goes where the child intends"It walks to the tree, then stops."
2. Keeps scoreMonth 2 to 3A game with a score variable that starts at zero"Every catch adds one."
3. DecidesMonth 3 to 5A game with if, else rules"If it touches the dog, you lose a life."
4. TestsMonth 5 to 7Tries edge cases like exactly 10"I tried 10 and 11 to check."
5. PlansMonth 7 to 12A whole game planned on paper first"First the menu, then the levels, then the badges."

For parents

Milestone 4 is the big one. A child who tests more than one number without being asked has started to think like a programmer.

Ask to see the bug list. A growing list is good news: it means your child is noticing.

For your eight year old

When something works, try to break it. Try a big number, a small number, and the exact edge.

If the computer does something strange, it is following your rules exactly. Read them again slowly.

Coding at age 8: questions from parents in Peru

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. Every class is live, online and taught only in English. Mentors keep their English clear and unhurried, adapt to how fast your child works and how they speak, and put each instruction on the shared screen as they say it. Eight year olds who are still building their English follow well, because the game itself shows whether they understood.

Is eight a good age to start coding?

Yes, and a very common one. An eight year old reads well enough to use blocks quickly, can hold a rule in mind, and is old enough to enjoy testing it. Children who start at eight often move to typed Python at nine or ten.

Should an eight year old learn Scratch or Python?

Scratch first for nearly every eight year old. It teaches decisions, loops and variables without spelling mistakes getting in the way. A strong reader who has already built several Scratch games can start Python; the free class shows us which.

What does 3° de primaria cover in computing?

There is no computing area in the national plan for third grade. MINEDU's curriculum treats digital skills as a competency shared across subjects, and Ciencia y Tecnología includes designing technological solutions. Some schools teach coding in their own time; many do not.

Can my child use a tablet or phone?

Only to watch. Building games needs a laptop or desktop with a mouse and a headset. INEI's 2026 data show that most people in Peru go online by phone, but coding is one of the jobs a phone does badly.

At what hour would my eight year old have class?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India is always ten hours thirty minutes ahead of Peru, so a 16:00 class for your child is 02:30 the next day for the mentor.

Will my eight year old use ChatGPT?

No. OpenAI sets a minimum age of 13 for ChatGPT. In our classes eight year olds learn how AI learns with tools like Teachable Machine, which runs in the browser without an account.

How much practice is needed between classes?

Not much: two short sessions a week on the child's own game is plenty. The aim is a habit of small changes and testing, not hours at the screen.

Do you teach robotics?

We teach coding, not hardware, so there is no kit to buy. Children program characters, games and simple AI models on screen, which builds the same logic that robot programming uses.

Is Modern Age Coders part of MINEDU or any Peruvian school?

No. We are a private online school with mentors in India, unconnected to MINEDU, any UGEL or any school in Peru. We give no Peruvian grades; finishing a course earns our own certificate of completion.
