## Repeating patterns: the Ontario Grade 3 coding an Etobicoke child meets

Etobicoke public schools teach the Ontario curriculum. The quotes below come from the Grade 3 math expectations and notes, read from the ministry&rsquo;s curriculum content service on 22 October 2026; the second column is our approach.

- **Grade 3, overview**: The ministry&rsquo;s summary of the year: &ldquo;Students will write code to perform a repeating operation, such as a repeating number pattern.&rdquo; Our answer: We start where Grade 3 already is: counting by 2s, 5s and 10s. A child says the pattern out loud, then builds the loop that says it for them.
- **Grade 3, C3.1**: Code that involves &ldquo;sequential, concurrent, and repeating events&rdquo;, now with repeating added to the list. Our answer: Repeat is the new block this year. We set one challenge in every lesson: find the part of your program that happens more than once and put a loop around it.
- **Grade 3, example**: A program in the notes: &ldquo;Set value to 0. Repeat 10 times: Add 2 to Value, Output value.&rdquo; It prints 2, 4, 6 and so on up to 20. Our answer: Children run it, then change the 2 to a 5 and the 10 to a 4 and predict the last number before pressing go. Guessing first, checking second, is the habit we want.
- **Grade 3, teacher notes**: &ldquo;Loops can also help to emphasize the repetitive properties of some mathematical tasks and concepts.&rdquo; Our answer: We use loops to show why multiplication is repeated addition: four loops of 3 steps land the sprite on 12, every time.

The ministry also suggests unplugged coding, away from the screen, such as &ldquo;two characters mirroring one another&rsquo;s patterns of movement&rdquo;. We use the same idea at the start of class: one child calls out turns and claps, the other follows, and both find out quickly how exact an instruction has to be. The Grade 3 number strand has a line that ties straight into the project below: &ldquo;Each decade repeats the 0 to 9 counting sequence.&rdquo; Grouping years into decades is exactly that pattern, used on real dates. For the years before and after Grade 3, our [Ontario coding guide for Grades 1 to 8](/ontario-coding-curriculum-grades-1-to-8) walks through each grade.

## When did Etobicoke get its public art? A decade chart from the City&rsquo;s own list

The City of Toronto publishes its public art inventory as open data: artworks owned by the City and works delivered through its percent-for-art program with private developers. Our older kids keep the rows in wards 1, 2 and 3, the three wards whose names begin with Etobicoke, and count the works by decade. That ward choice is ours, and every number below is our own count from the file downloaded on 22 October 2026.

**Public artworks in Toronto wards 1, 2 and 3 (Etobicoke North, Etobicoke Centre, Etobicoke-Lakeshore) by the decade in the YEAR column, our count from the City of Toronto Public Art file**

| Decade | Works in the file | As a text bar |
|---|---|---|
| 1930s | 1 | # |
| 1950s | 1 | # |
| 1960s | 3 | ### |
| 1990s | 6 | ###### |
| 2000s | 3 | ### |
| 2010s | 2 | ## |
| 2020s | 8 | ######## |
| Year unknown | 1 | # |

The file holds 459 rows for the whole city; 25 of them sit in the three Etobicoke wards, 15 in ward 3, 7 in ward 1 and 3 in ward 2. The newest work is dated 2024 and the oldest 1930, and the 2020s already have more works than any earlier decade. Here is the program an 11-year-old writes, using only the Python standard library:

```
import csv

decades = {}
with open("public-art.csv", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    for art in csv.DictReader(f):
        if art["WARD"] in ("1", "2", "3"):
            year = art["YEAR"]
            if year.isdigit():
                label = str(int(year) // 10 * 10) + "s"
            else:
                label = "unknown"
            decades[label] = decades.get(label, 0) + 1

for label in sorted(decades):
    print(label, "#" * decades[label])
```

### Decades by division

Integer division throws away the remainder: 1997 // 10 is 199, and times 10 is 1990. Adding an s gives the label. It is the Grade 3 decade pattern, done by a computer.

### When the year is missing

One row says unknown instead of a year. Calling int on it would crash the program, so isdigit checks first, and the row lands in its own group rather than vanishing.

### A chart from symbols

Multiplying a string repeats it, so "#" * 8 prints eight marks. A child sees the 2020s bar is longest without any charting library at all.

### Why we match the number, not the name

Our first try matched the ward names exactly as the City&rsquo;s ward file spells them and found 21 works. Four were missing. The art file writes ward 2 as Etobicoke-Centre, with a hyphen, and one ward 3 row adds (3) to the name. Matching the WARD number instead finds all 25. Across the city, 36 rows put a number in brackets after the ward name. Real files are typed by people, and a good program trusts the code column over the label.

There is more for a curious class. Sculptures make up 20 of the 25, with two murals or paintings, two pieces of architecture or landscape and one multidisciplinary work. 13 belong to the City&rsquo;s own collection and 11 came through the developer program. Searching the materials column, after making everything lower case, finds steel in 9 rows and bronze in 5; a single row can name more than one material, so those numbers overlap and must not be added together. The package notes say the inventory &ldquo;shows over 400 works of public art throughout the City of Toronto&rdquo;, which matches the 459 rows we downloaded. We print counts, decades and materials only, never artists, titles or descriptions. ## The Beaver Computing Challenge in November 2026: what Etobicoke parents should know this week

The Beaver Computing Challenge (BCC) from CEMC at the University of Waterloo is a logic contest with a level for Grade 6 and younger. It runs in schools, so timing depends on a school ordering it. Details are CEMC&rsquo;s, read on 22 October 2026.

### The deadline is close

CEMC gives Monday 26 October 2026 as the ordering deadline for this year&rsquo;s BCC, which runs from 9 to 20 November 2026. A school that has not ordered by then may miss this year, so a quick question to the teacher now is worth it.

### Last year&rsquo;s numbers

CEMC reports 14 260 contestants at the Grade 5/6 level in 2025, with a mean score of 37.22 out of 60. It is a contest many children write, and an average score sits well below full marks.

### Not a test

CEMC puts it plainly: &ldquo;Our contests are not tests or exams. There is no passing or failing score.&rdquo; We tell children the same: the point is to enjoy hard puzzles, not to be perfect.

Entry goes through school, since CEMC says all students must participate through their school. Most Etobicoke children in our Grade 3 and 4 classes are still a year or two from the Grade 5/6 level, and that is fine: a child who has spent those years predicting what a loop will print is already practising the reasoning the BCC rewards. We teach with puzzles we write ourselves, labelled as ours, and send families to CEMC for past contests. One of ours for an eight-year-old: a sprite starts at 0 and repeats &ldquo;add 3&rdquo; seven times; where does it stop, and how many times would it need to repeat to pass 30? The [Beaver Computing Challenge guide](/beaver-computing-challenge-guide) covers every level, and the [Canada contest calendar](/canada-math-and-coding-contest-calendar) lists the rest of the year. ## Does an Etobicoke child still need to code if AI can do it?

Yes, because a child who can code can tell when the computer is wrong.

The public art project shows it. A program that matched ward names would have reported 21 works and looked perfectly confident. Only someone who checked the result against the ward numbers noticed four were missing. AI tools make that kind of quiet mistake too, and the defence is a person who knows what the right answer should look like.

We do not put adult chatbots in front of children under 13. Instead they learn, through teacher-led activities, how AI systems learn from examples and why they get things wrong. Most of each lesson is the child thinking: breaking a problem into steps, trying one, and checking whether it worked.

Grade 3 asks children to spot what repeats in a pattern, and seeing structure for yourself is the skill that keeps a person in charge of the tools. The longer argument is in [the case for coding in an AI world](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
