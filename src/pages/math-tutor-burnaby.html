Who teaches math live to Burnaby students?

Modern Age Coders gives Burnaby students live online math tutoring, Grade 1 to Pre-calculus 12, with a teacher working one to one or with fifteen to twenty students at the same level. Trigonometry is where many Burnaby teenagers first feel that math has turned against them. It arrives gently in Grade 10 with right triangles, speeds up in Pre-calculus 11 with the sine and cosine laws and their ambiguous cases, and in Pre-calculus 12 switches to radians, the unit circle and identities. Each step leans on the last. This page follows that thread through the BC curriculum, using the province’s own wording, and then teaches it on something every Burnaby student knows: the SkyTrain stations, as the City of Burnaby maps them in its open data. A worked problem, the CEMC contests that reward written solutions, and an honest note on prices follow.

Trigonometry through BC math, from Grade 10 to Pre-calculus 12

Public schools in Burnaby follow BC’s provincial curriculum. The phrases in the table are copied from the province’s curriculum site on 23 October 2026; the right-hand column is ours.

Where trigonometry appears in BC secondary math. Content wording from curriculum.gov.bc.ca; the summaries are ours
CourseThe province’s wordingWhat the student actually does
Mathematics 9“spatial proportional reasoning scale diagrams, similar triangles and polygons”Similar triangles, the idea every trigonometric ratio rests on
Foundations of Mathematics and Pre-calculus 10“right-triangle problems: determining missing sides and/or angles using trigonometric ratios and the Pythagorean theorem”Sine, cosine and tangent in right triangles

Read top to bottom, the table explains why trigonometry trouble is rarely about trigonometry. A Pre-calculus 11 student who cannot apply the cosine law usually has a Grade 9 gap in similar triangles or a Grade 10 gap in rearranging an equation. A Pre-calculus 12 student lost in radians often never felt comfortable with angles in standard position the year before. Our first lesson with a Burnaby teenager therefore starts one course back, briefly, before it moves forward.

Special angles

Pre-calculus 11 lists “special angles, as connected with the 30-60-90 and 45-45-90 triangles”. Students who can sketch those two triangles from memory rarely need a calculator for exact values, and Pre-calculus 12 assumes they can.

Identities in Grade 12

Pre-calculus 12 asks students to use “identities to reduce complexity in expressions and solve equations”: Pythagorean, quotient, double angle, reciprocal, sum and difference. Proving an identity and solving an equation are different tasks, and we teach them as such.

Beyond trigonometry

The same senior courses also carry logarithms, polynomials and series. For calculus after Pre-calculus 12, our Calculus 12 tutor page sets out the course in full.

Our role beside school

Burnaby data lesson

Solving a triangle drawn between three Burnaby SkyTrain stations

The City of Burnaby publishes the positions of the SkyTrain stations in the city as open data, in metres. Pre-calculus 11 students turn three of them into a triangle and solve it completely. The distances and angles below are our own calculations.

The layer is called SkyTrain Station, and the City describes it as points of ingress and egress for the Skytrain network. Our query on 23 October 2026 returned 11 stations, from Gilmore and Brentwood Town Centre in the north to Royal Oak and Edmonds in the south. Each comes with an easting and a northing in metres, so the distance between two stations is simply the Pythagorean theorem applied to the differences.

Two checks are built into the table. The three angles add to 180 degrees, and the sine law ratio comes out at 8,385.6 m for all three sides, exactly as it must. The area agrees too: one half of b times c times the sine of A gives 17.37 square kilometres, the same as the shoelace formula on the three coordinates. Here is the Python that computes the sides and angles:import json, math with open("skytrainstn.json", encoding="utf-8") as f: points = {feat["attributes"]["NAME"]: (feat["geometry"]["x"], feat["geometry"]["y"]) for feat in json.load(f)["features"]} A = points["METROTOWN STATION"] B = points["BRENTWOOD TOWN CENTRE STATION"] C = points["LOUGHEED TOWN CENTRE STATION"] a, b, c = math.dist(B, C), math.dist(A, C), math.dist(A, B) angle_A = math.degrees(math.acos((b*b + c*c - a*a) / (2*b*c))) angle_B = math.degrees(math.acos((a*a + c*c - b*b) / (2*a*c))) angle_C = 180 - angle_A - angle_B print("sides (m):", round(a, 1), round(b, 1), round(c, 1)) print("angles:", round(angle_A, 2), round(angle_B, 2), round(angle_C, 2)) print("sine law:", round(a / math.sin(math.radians(angle_A)), 1))

Closest and farthest

With 11 stations there are 55 pairs. The closest pair in our calculation is Metrotown and Patterson, 764.3 m apart; the farthest is Gilmore and Lougheed Town Centre at 8,679.9 m. Students find both with a loop before they guess.

Angles in standard position

Measured from east, anticlockwise, Brentwood Town Centre sits at 88.0 degrees from Metrotown, almost due north, and Lougheed Town Centre at 18.1 degrees. In Pre-calculus 12 terms those are 1.536 and 0.315 radians.

Negative angles

Edmonds lies south-east of Metrotown, so its angle in standard position comes out as minus 25.1 degrees. Students then find the positive coterminal angle, 334.9 degrees, which the Pre-calculus 11 content asks for by name.

What these numbers are not

Every distance here is a straight line between two points on the City’s map, not the length of the track and not a travel time. The trains follow a curving guideway, so a rider covers more than 4,517.9 m between Metrotown and Brentwood Town Centre. A good answer says which distance it means.

A worked problem, ours

Worked problem: the ambiguous case, settled by a map

Our own Pre-calculus 11 problem. It uses two sides and an angle from the station triangle, chosen so that the sine law offers two answers.

Question. In a triangle, side a is 7,877.8 m, side c is 4,517.9 m, and the angle C opposite side c is 32.6 degrees. Find angle A and side b. How many triangles fit these measurements, and which one matches the three Burnaby stations?

Step 1, use the sine law for A. sin A divided by a equals sin C divided by c, so sin A equals 7,877.8 times sin 32.6 degrees, divided by 4,517.9, which is about 0.9394. The calculator gives A = 69.96 degrees.

Step 2, look for the second angle. Sine is positive in the first and second quadrants, so 180 minus 69.96, or 110.04 degrees, has the same sine. Both are possible here, because 110.04 plus 32.6 is still less than 180. This is the ambiguous case the Pre-calculus 11 content names: two sides and an angle not between them can fit two different triangles.

Step 3, finish both triangles. If A is 69.96 degrees, B is 77.44 degrees and the sine law gives b = 8,184.9 m. If A is 110.04 degrees, B is 37.36 degrees and b = 5,088.4 m. Both are correct triangles.

Step 4, let the data decide. The real distance from Metrotown to Lougheed Town Centre, from the coordinates, is 8,184.9 m, so the stations form the first triangle. The algebra alone could not choose; extra information had to. Students who learn to ask what information would settle an ambiguity have learned something larger than a trigonometry rule.

Try it yourself. (1) Keep a and C the same and shrink c until only one triangle is possible; at what length does that happen? (2) Use the cosine law to find the distance from Patterson to Holdom from their coordinates, then check with the distance formula. (3) Convert the Gilmore angle from Metrotown, 99.2 degrees, to radians. We go through the answers in the free lesson.

Math and AI

Will an AI solve a Burnaby student’s triangle for them?

It will give an answer. Whether it gives both answers, or the right one, is another matter.

Type the ambiguous-case question into a chatbot and it may return 69.96 degrees and stop, never mentioning the second triangle. Ask it for the distance between two stations and it may give a straight line when the question meant track length. A student who understands the sine law and the map catches both slips; one who copies the output does not.

So every lesson asks the student to sketch, estimate and justify before computing. AI tools appear only for students old enough under our age policy, and then only as a second opinion on finished work. Work handed in at school stays the student’s own.

The Pre-calculus 11 phrase “including ambiguous cases” is a small reminder that mathematics is about knowing when an answer is incomplete. The longer argument is in why problem solving beats copying in the age of AI.
