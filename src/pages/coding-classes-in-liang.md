## Twenty settlements, three of them called Lumut

The shape of this mukim explains both why families here have few local options and why its data catches people out.

Mukim Liang sits in the northern part of Belait district, on the South China Sea. It recorded **14,301** residents at the 2016 census and **16,813** at the 2021 one, an increase of 2,512 people or **17.6 per cent** in five years. For comparison, the six water village mukims in the capital fell from 10,250 to 8,902 across the same interval, a drop of about thirteen per cent. Liang is where Brunei added people.

In 2016 the mukim was recorded as 32.3 per cent urban and 67.7 per cent rural, and 51.4 per cent male. It contains twenty listed settlements. The industrial anchor is the Sungai Liang Industrial Park, with Brunei Fertilizer Industries on the same site.

The detail worth pausing on is how the population is distributed among those settlements, because it is not distributed the way the names suggest.

The village called Lumut is the smallest of the three places called Lumut

Kampong Lumut recorded 171 residents. Beside it, the two national housing scheme areas that carry its name recorded 2,035 and 5,605. So the settlements sharing the name hold 7,811 people between them, and the village itself is about one forty-fifth of that. Nothing is wrong with any of those figures. They are simply three separate places with overlapping names, which is an ordinary thing for a country that builds housing schemes next to existing villages, and a trap for anybody who reads the names as identifiers.

## Join two tables on a name and you will merge things that are not the same

Every page in this cluster works through one analytical mistake. Liang supplies the cleanest example of this one anywhere in the country, and it is the single most common cause of quietly wrong numbers in real work.

The learner gets two files. One lists settlements and populations. The other lists, say, schools by settlement. The task is ordinary: how many people live in each place that has a school? They join the two files on the settlement name, because that is the column both files share.

The trap

The join turns 171 people into 7,811, and reports it without complaint

Three settlements in this mukim contain the word Lumut: Kampong Lumut with 171 residents, RPN Kampong Lumut Area 1 with 2,035, and RPN Kampong Lumut Area 2 with 5,605. Depending on how the join is written, one of two things happens, and both are silent.

If the match is loose, by prefix or by contains or by anything fuzzy, the three collapse into a single row of **7,811**. The village of Lumut is now reported as forty-five times its actual size, and every per-head figure computed from it is wrong by that factor. If the match is strict, the two housing scheme rows fail to match anything, get dropped, and **7,640 people vanish from the analysis** without an error being raised. One join, two opposite failures, and no warning either way.

The fix is not a cleverer string comparison. It is to stop joining on names at all. Names are for humans: they get abbreviated, transliterated, prefixed with RPN, suffixed with Area 2, spelt with and without Kampong, and changed when a scheme is renamed. **An identifier is a value that exists in order to be matched, and a name is not one.** Where a real code exists, join on the code. Where none exists, the honest step is to build a lookup by hand once and keep it under version control, so the mapping is a documented decision instead of a lucky string comparison.

And there is a check that catches it every time, which is the actual habit being taught. **Count the rows before the join and count them after.** If the number went up, something matched more than once. If it went down, something matched nothing. If either happened and you did not expect it, stop, because from that point every figure downstream is confident and wrong.

All the settlement figures are public and linked above. The learner writes both versions of the join, sees both failures, and only then is shown the row count check, which is deliberately the last thing rather than the first.

## Does coding still matter when AI can write it?

Give any assistant the seven settlements above and ask it to total the population of Lumut. It will produce a number, in a clean sentence, without hesitating.

Which number it produces depends entirely on an assumption it made and did not mention: whether the three Lumut rows are one place or three. Both readings are defensible. That is precisely why the answer should have arrived with the assumption attached, and it usually will not, because the question did not ask for one.

So the person is still needed for the same thing they were needed for before: knowing that a name is not an identifier, and asking which of the two questions was actually answered. That habit comes from having been burned by a join, on data you could check, at an age where being wrong costs nothing.

Probably, eventually, and that is not much use to a twelve year old now. Provision follows population with a lag of years, and this mukim added 2,512 people between the 2016 and 2021 censuses. Not in Belait and not anywhere else in Brunei. What happens is a video call originating in India, and the telephone number above rings there too. It never moves. Brunei holds UTC plus 8 all year and India holds UTC plus 5:30 all year, so the separation is permanently two and a half hours. Seven in the evening in Lumut is half past four in the afternoon for the teacher, in January and in July alike. A slot agreed once needs revisiting never.

The days your household names.
