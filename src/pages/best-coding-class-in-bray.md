The census counts 2,245 residents aged ten to fourteen and only 1,704 aged four or under, a quarter fewer, and the obvious headline writes itself: fewer children in Bray. The headline is not supported. One census is one day, and those two groups are two different sets of people, not the same children photographed twice. ## Age is not a time series

Bray residents by five-year age band, from the single-year Census 2022 table, beside the row for all towns combined. The bands are our sums of the published single years.

**Younger age bands, Bray and all towns combined, April 2022**

| Age band | Bray | All towns combined |
|---|---|---|
| Under 5 | 1,704 | 211,241 |
| 5 to 9 | 2,056 | 240,435 |
| 10 to 14 | 2,245 | 257,812 |
| 15 to 19 | 2,131 | 230,877 |

### What the table is

A count of who lived in Bray on one day in April 2022, sorted by how old each person was. Age runs left to right like time, which is exactly what makes it so easy to misread as a history of the town.

### Why the slope is not a trend

The 2,245 residents aged ten to fourteen were never the same people as the 1,704 aged under five. Nobody moved between those rows. A smaller younger band can come from fewer births, from families moving out, from families with older children moving in, or from all three at once, and this table separates none of them.

### The comparison that settles it

All towns combined show the same shape, 211,241 under five against 257,812 aged ten to fourteen. That is a youngest band at 81.9% of the ten to fourteen band, against 75.9% in Bray by our arithmetic, so the slope runs through the whole country and is somewhat steeper here.

### Cross-section, not history

A single snapshot sorted by any quantity that grows with time will look like a trend. Customers by tenure. Employees by years of service. Files by age. Accounts by signup month. In each case the oldest group has survived something the newest group has not yet faced, and the ones who left are simply absent from the picture. The fix is to name the question first: if the question is about change, one snapshot cannot answer it, and no amount of clever plotting inside that snapshot will help.

The project has two halves. First the learner reproduces the table honestly from the published single years, writing a function that sums a band and checking the four bands against the table row by row. Then they write the plan for the real question: pull the same bands from an earlier census, confirm first whether the published area for Bray covered the same ground both times, and only then compare. We have not run that comparison here, and the page says so rather than implying a trend we have not measured.

## Ask a chatbot whether Bray has fewer children now

The answer will arrive with a trend in it.

Give a model this age table and ask what is happening to the town and it will usually describe a decline, because the numbers slope and a slope invites a story. It may even name causes. Nothing in the table supports either move, and nothing in the reply flags that the table is a single day rather than a sequence of years.

A learner who has worked through this page answers in a different order: the question is about change, the data is a snapshot, so here is what a snapshot can show and here is the second dataset that would be needed. It is a shorter answer with nothing invented in it.

Producing a plausible trend is now free. Knowing when there is no trend to produce is the part worth learning. The longer argument is in [the long case for learning to code](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
