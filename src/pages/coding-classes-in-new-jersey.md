## Why New Jersey is different

New Jersey asks more of its students in computer science than most states. Since the 2018 to 2019 school year, every public high school in the state has been required to offer a computer science course covering computational thinking, computer programming, appropriate use of the internet and development of web pages, data security and the prevention of data breaches, ethical matters in computer science, and the global impact of advances in computer science.

The standards behind that requirement are the New Jersey Student Learning Standards for Computer Science and Design Thinking, adopted in 2020. They run from Kindergarten through grade 12 and are split into two halves:

**Computer science concepts:** Computing Systems; Networks and the Internet; Impacts of Computing; Data and Analysis; Algorithms and Programming.

**Design thinking concepts:** Engineering Design; Interaction of Technology and Humans; Nature of Technology; Effects of Technology on the Natural World; Ethics and Culture.

Every course a New Jersey student is placed into maps onto named concepts from that document.

## The graduation credit rule most families miss

New Jersey requires that local graduation requirements adopted by a board of education permit an Advanced Placement computer science course to satisfy part of the total credit requirement in **mathematics**. The condition is that the student is concurrently enrolled in, or has already completed, Algebra 1 and Geometry or the content equivalent. It applies beginning with the grade nine class of 2016 to 2017.

That makes AP Computer Science A unusually valuable in New Jersey: it can cover part of a mathematics requirement while also being an exam colleges recognise. Districts set their own local requirements within the state framework, so confirm the specifics with your school counsellor before planning a schedule around it.

## The New Jersey student data project

Each market we teach has its own data project built on local data with a genuine analytical trap inside it. New Jersey students work on NJ Transit punctuality.

NJ Transit publishes three performance statistics: On-Time Performance, Mean Distance Between Failure, and Total Cancellations. A vehicle running within six minutes of the published schedule counts as on time, so a train five minutes and fifty seconds late is statistically punctual.

The trap is that cancellations are reported separately and are not part of On-Time Performance. A train that never runs is not a late train. So a line that cancels services can post better punctuality than a line that runs everything late, and a student ranking lines by On-Time Performance alone will produce a ranking that is the opposite of the passenger experience.

A third layer: NJ Transit publishes two rail dashboards for the same trains, one for all causes and one adjusted to remove delays attributed to Amtrak, which owns the Northeast Corridor track. Comparing the adjusted series against the all-causes series manufactures a trend that does not exist.

Students pull the current published figures themselves rather than using numbers reproduced here, which would go stale within a month.
