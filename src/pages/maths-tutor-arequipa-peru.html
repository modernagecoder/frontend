Arequipa and maths

How does Arequipa do in national maths results?

Well. MINEDU has named Arequipa among the regions with the highest learning results in two consecutive national assessments, and that changes what families ask a tutor for.

What MINEDU said, in its own words

Reporting ENLA 2024, the test of 4° de primaria, MINEDU wrote that "Tacna, Moquegua y Arequipa se mantienen como las regiones con mayores logros de aprendizaje". Reporting ENLA 2025, which tested the fourth year of primaria and the second and fifth years of secundaria, it wrote again that the three regions "continúan liderando".

Those statements cover reading and maths together, and we could not reach MINEDU's regional result tables, so we print no Arequipa percentage.

What that means for tutoring

A region doing well still has students who fall behind, and we help them. But in Arequipa we also meet many students who are fine in class and bored by it.

For them a tutor is a source of harder questions: why a rule works, what happens at the edge of a pattern, how to prove something rather than check it on three examples.

The curriculum

What maths does the national curriculum ask of an Arequipa student?

Arequipa follows the CNEB, which organises maths into four competencies and sets weekly hours for each stage.

Four competencies

Competency 23, "Resuelve problemas de cantidad", deals with numbers and operations. The other three cover regularity, equivalence and change (patterns and algebra), form, movement and location (geometry), and data and uncertainty (statistics and probability).

Hours in primaria

Five hours of Matemática a week in 1° and 2° de primaria, then four a week from 3° to 6°. Each school hour, the hora pedagógica, lasts 45 minutes.

Hours in secundaria

Four hours a week in every grade on the regular school day, six on the full school day (Jornada Escolar Completa). Maths keeps the same weight from 1° to 5°.

No. Our maths classes are live, online and in English only. For Arequipa families that is usually a reason to choose us, not a barrier.

How tutors teach in English

Tutors are based in India. They write as they speak, so every word is also on the whiteboard, keep sentences short, and stop to check. A student may answer in Spanish; the tutor accepts it and gives back the English.

Maths has fewer words than most subjects and they repeat constantly: numerator, equation, gradient, probability. After a few weeks they stop being English words and become maths words.

Olympiad problems reward a few habits more than any formula: trying small cases, looking for what stays the same, counting carefully, and writing a short argument. Our ONEM preparation in English page explains our approach in detail, and maths olympiad coaching for Peru covers the international route.

A problem of ours, in the olympiad spirit. How many whole numbers from 1 to 999 start with the digit 1? And how many start with 9? (Ours. Answer: 111 each, since 1, 10 to 19 and 100 to 199 give 1 + 10 + 100. So why do real-life lists of amounts so often start with 1 far more than 9? The lesson below is about exactly that.)

An Arequipa maths lesson

Why do so many real numbers start with 1?

Benford's law, named after the physicist Frank Benford, says that in many collections of real-world numbers about 30% begin with the digit 1 and fewer than 5% with 9. This lesson, ours, tests that idea on an Arequipa open dataset.

The law

Benford's law predicts that the share of numbers whose first digit is d is log10(1 + 1/d). For d = 1 that is log10(2), about 0.301; for d = 9 it is log10(10/9), about 0.046.

It tends to hold for quantities that span several orders of magnitude and grow by multiplying, such as populations or amounts of money. It fails for numbers with a fixed range, like ages or exam scores.

The data

The Gobierno Regional de Arequipa releases "Proyectos Cofinanciados por Prociencia en Arequipa" as open data, licensed ODC-By (Open Data Commons Attribution), through the national data portal. It lists 276 science and innovation projects co-financed by Prociencia from 2013 to 2024, with a funded amount for each.

We exported four columns, year, type, currency and amount, to our own file prociencia-arequipa.csv. We use only the first digit of each amount; no amount and no person's name appears on this page.

The digit 1 leads 29.6% of the 270 non-zero amounts, almost exactly Benford's 30.1%. But 3, 4 and 5 are each well above the prediction, and 6 and 7 well below. Version 2 lines the two up, reads the first digit more safely from scientific notation, and checks one suspicion: grants set by competition rules are often round numbers.

Six rows have no positive amount; most amounts are in soles and eight rows in other currencies, which does not change the idea, because Benford's law does not depend on the unit.

So what happened? 109 of the 270 amounts are exact multiples of 10,000. When a call for projects offers a fixed ceiling, many applicants ask for exactly that, and a few popular ceilings starting with 3, 4 or 5 lift those digits above the curve. The sample is also small: with 270 numbers, a difference of two or three percentage points can be chance. The honest conclusion is that the data follows Benford on the digit 1, departs from it where rules shape the amounts, and proves nothing about anyone. Auditors use Benford's law as a reason to look closer, never as evidence on its own.

  1. Primaria, 9 to 11. Write the populations from the table above and the ages of everyone in your family. Which list starts with 1 more often? Why might ages not follow the law?
  2. 1° to 3° de secundaria. Without a calculator, show that log10(2) is between 0.3 and 0.31, using 2 to the power 10 = 1,024.
  3. 4° and 5° de secundaria. Add the nine Benford shares log10(1 + 1/d). Why do they add to exactly 1? (Hint: the logarithm of a product.)
  4. Statistics students. Remove the 109 round amounts and count again. Does the gap shrink? Is 161 numbers enough to say?
  5. Going further. Multiply every amount by 3.7 and recount the first digits. Benford's law predicts the shares hardly move. Test it.

Maths tutor in Arequipa: questions and answers

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. Our maths classes are live, online and given only in English. Tutors write what they say on the whiteboard, keep a steady pace, adapt to each student's accent, and turn Spanish replies into English ones.

My child already does well in maths. Is a tutor still useful?

Often more so. Many Arequipa students find class easy and want harder problems. We add depth, proofs and olympiad-style reasoning beside the school topics.

Can you prepare my child for the ONEM?

We teach the reasoning the ONEM rewards with original problems of our own, never the organiser's papers. Students enter through their school; in 2026 the school stage was on 8 July. We are not connected with MINEDU or the ONEM.

Which school years do you cover?

All of them, from 1° de primaria to 5° de secundaria, plus IGCSE, university maths and adult refreshers. Plans follow the CNEB topics your child is meeting in class.

What time are classes?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. Arequipa clocks never change, and India is ten and a half hours later: 10:00 here is 20:30 there.

How does the tutor see my child's working?

Students write on a whiteboard both sides can see, or hold their notebook up to the camera, and talk the tutor through each step.

Can adults in Arequipa take maths classes?

Yes. Adults refresh school maths, prepare for university courses or learn statistics for work, in adult groups or one to one.

How are fees paid?

Once a month, by card, in the US dollars shown here; the bank turns that into soles. From India, the page shows rupees instead. There is no charge for the first class.

Do you award a certificate?

Some courses finish with a completion certificate issued by us. It records the work done; it is not a Peruvian school grade, and neither MINEDU nor any university issues or recognises it.
