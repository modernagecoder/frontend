Informatik in the Land Bremen

Do Bremen schools teach coding?

Not yet as a subject. The Gesellschaft für Informatik, which compares every Land each year, finds no curricular Informatik in Bremen's lower secondary schools in 2026/27. A compulsory subject is on its way.

The position in 2026/27

Bremen children go to Grundschule for grades 1 to 4 and then to an Oberschule or a Gymnasium for grades 5 to 10. For those secondary years the GI Informatik-Monitor 2026/27 is blunt: "Es finden weder an der Oberschule noch am Gymnasium curricular hinterlegte Angebote mit Informatikbezug statt." Neither school type has any curricular offer connected to Informatik.

In grades 5 and 6, the subject Wirtschaft, Arbeit, Technik (WAT) includes digital content, but the monitor notes that it aims at media skills, not at programming.

What changes from 2027/28

The same report says Bremen will introduce Informatik as a compulsory subject at all schools from the school year 2027/28, with two to four weekly hours in total. Until then the Land stays on the list of six Länder that have no Informatik lesson for every pupil.

Later on, in the Oberstufe, Informatik can be chosen as a course, but the GI finds that in Bremen only about one pupil in twelve at that stage takes it.

For a parent this is unusually clear. A Bremen child who wonders how a game decides who has won will not meet that question in a school lesson before the new subject arrives, and a child who is ten today will already be in grade 7 or 8 when it does. Starting at home or online is not getting ahead of school here; it is simply the only way to start.

Taught in English

Will my child understand a coding class in English?

Almost always, yes. The classes are live and in English only, and the mentor's job is to make that work for your child.

No German lessons

We teach in English and only in English. That is said here and again before any trial, so no family is surprised. Bremen also has international schools, such as the International School of Bremen, which lists the IB and IGCSE in the AGIS directory; families whose children attend German schools tend to see the English as a bonus.

Matched to the child

Mentors adjust their speed, choose simple words and repeat a step in a different way when a face looks puzzled. Half-sentence answers are fine, and if a German word slips in, the mentor simply says the English word back and carries on.

Show, then say

Most of a lesson happens on the shared screen. The mentor shows a block or a line, the child copies it and changes it, and the result appears at once. The words follow the picture, which keeps the English load small.

Mentors teach from India over live video; a Bremen afternoon is an Indian evening, which is why the class times below work. A group keeps its mentor from month to month, and the mentor keeps short notes on each child: who rushes, who hesitates, who learns by breaking things first.

A real Bremen project

Which Bremen library opens first on a Thursday? A project on times stored as text

Bremen publishes a list of its libraries, with opening hours for every weekday, as open data. Asking the computer a simple question about it teaches one of the commonest mistakes in programming: comparing words when you meant to compare numbers.

The data

The file "Bibliotheken in Bremen" comes from the Transparenzportal of the Freie Hansestadt Bremen and is published under a CC BY licence, so it may be reused with credit. It lists 30 libraries in Bremen and Bremerhaven: the Stadtbibliothek and its branches, the Staats- und Universitätsbibliothek, museum and research libraries.

Each weekday has two columns, "von" (from) and "bis" (until), filled with text such as "9.00 Uhr". The portal's own description even says that this static list cannot answer a question like which library is open on Saturday. A short program can.

The question

On a Thursday morning, which library opens first? A child scanning the list by eye finds it quickly. The program should too, and the first version uses Python's min() to pick the smallest opening time.

Before running it, we ask the child to predict the answer. Then we look at what the computer says, and whether it agrees.

The answer looks sensible until someone notices that the Staats- und Universitätsbibliothek opens at 8.00, an hour earlier. Why did the computer miss it? Because it was comparing text, letter by letter, like words in a dictionary. "09.00" begins with 0, so it comes first; "8.00" begins with 8, which comes after 1, so it lands behind "10.00" and "11.00". Text that looks like a time is still only text.

Now the library opening at 8.00 wins. Two more surprises sit in that output, and children enjoy both. First, Python really does say that the text "9.00" is bigger than "10.00". Second, the name shows "UniversitÕ_tsbibliothek" instead of "Universitätsbibliothek": the umlauts were damaged inside the file itself, long before we opened it. Real data carries the marks of how it was made, and part of the job is spotting them.

  1. Ages 6 to 8, with a clock. Write five opening times on cards, some as "9.00" and some as "10.00". Sort them by the first digit only. Then sort them by the clock. Which order is right?
  2. Ages 8 to 10, in Scratch. Store opening times as hours and minutes in two lists, work out minutes since midnight, and let a sprite announce the earliest.
  3. Ages 10 to 12, in Python. Run Version 1, explain the wrong answer to someone else, then write minutes() before you read ours.
  4. Going further. Use the same function on the "bis" columns to find which libraries stay open after 18.00, and the Saturday columns to rebuild the table above.

A free first contest

What is the Informatik-Biber, and can a Bremen child enter?

A free online contest of logic puzzles, open to every school year from grade 3 to grade 13, with no coding knowledge required. The 2026 contest weeks are 9 to 20 November. It suits Bremen children well, because it asks for exactly the thinking their timetable does not yet teach.

How it works

The Informatik-Biber is run by Bundesweite Informatikwettbewerbe (BWINF), which calls it Germany's largest school informatics competition. In 2025 the organisers counted over 580,000 participants.

The tasks are puzzles about order, rules, codes and routes, and the length grows with age: 30 minutes for nine tasks at primary level, 35 minutes for twelve in grades 5 and 6, and 40 minutes for fifteen from grade 7 upwards.

Taking part from Bremen

Entry goes through the school, and the contest fits into a single lesson, so the first step is a quick question to the class teacher about 9 to 20 November 2026. For practice at home, BWINF opened its Schnupper-Biber on 14 September 2026.

BWINF publishes results in December 2026 and posts certificates and prizes in January 2027.

We have no connection with BWINF and do not reuse its tasks. In class we write puzzles of our own in the same spirit; the library question above is one of them, since putting times in order is a Biber kind of problem. If your child's school is not taking part this year, the practice round is a pleasant free afternoon all the same.

Coding classes for kids in Bremen: frequent questions

Are the classes in German?

No, they are not. We teach only in English, always live. Your child's mentor slows down, uses simple words and gets used to how your child speaks, which is why children with limited English manage well. The code on screen is identical whatever language the lesson is in.

Is Informatik taught in Bremen schools?

Not as a curricular subject in grades 5 to 10 in 2026/27. The GI Informatik-Monitor finds no curricular Informatik offer at Bremen Oberschulen or Gymnasien. Bremen plans compulsory Informatik at all schools from 2027/28, with two to four weekly hours in total.

What age can my child start?

Six is a good age to begin. The youngest work with Scratch blocks and spoken puzzles, which needs neither typing nor much reading. Around nine most children are ready to type Python.

When are classes in Bremen time?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. In Bremen the late afternoon is the usual choice.

Where do the mentors teach from?

From India, live over video. A class at 15:15 in Bremen is at 19:45 in India this winter and at 18:45 after the clocks change on 28 March 2027.

Can my child take part in the Informatik-Biber?

Yes, through the school. The contest costs nothing, takes pupils from grade 3 upwards and asks for no coding. This year's contest weeks are 9 to 20 November 2026, so speak to the class teacher soon. We have no link with the organisers.

What is the library project?

An original Python project on Bremen's open data list of libraries. Children ask which library opens first on a Thursday, see the computer get it wrong by comparing text, and fix it by turning each time into minutes.

Do you teach children from Bremerhaven too?

Yes. Classes are online, so any child in the Land Bremen, or anywhere in Germany, can join the same groups at the same times.

How large is a group?

A group has fifteen to twenty children close in age. Private one-to-one lessons are the other option, scheduled around your family.

How are fees shown and paid?

This page displays the monthly fee in euro, converted with the reference rate of the European Central Bank. We charge in US dollars, so your bank handles the conversion. The trial class is free and asks for no card details.
