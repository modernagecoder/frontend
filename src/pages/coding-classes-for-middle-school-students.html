Grades 6 to 8

Middle school in the US, and where our courses fit

Middle school is the hinge between children’s and teenagers’ coding. A sixth grader and an eighth grader can be in the same building and need quite different classes.

How NCES defines it

The National Center for Education Statistics defines a middle school as "A school that offers more of grades 5 through 8 than higher or lower grades but does not offer both kindergarten through grade 4 and grades 9 through 12."

Middle school usually means grades 6 to 8, though some districts start it at grade 5 and some run grade 7 to 8 junior highs. The Congressional App Challenge rules say the same thing in plain words: "Middle school varies by region but typically encompasses 6th-8th grades".

Kids course or teens course?

Our courses for kids run to age 12 and our teen courses start at 13, so the line falls inside middle school. Most sixth graders join a kids course; most eighth graders are ready for the teen version; seventh grade can go either way.

We decide in the free first lesson by looking at typing speed, reading level and what the student has already built, not birthday alone. A confident twelve-year-old who has finished a lot of Scratch may start straight in a teen course.

By the end of grade 8

What the K-12 Computer Science Framework expects by eighth grade

The K-12 Computer Science Framework, which states may use to develop their own standards, sets an endpoint for grade 8. Read together, its programming statements describe a student who writes code other people can read and reuse.

The grade 8 endpoint, word for word from the framework (k12cs.org, 17 October 2026)
Idea in the frameworkGrade 8 statementSeen in class as
Algorithms"Algorithms that are readable are easier to follow, test, and debug."Rewriting a messy solution so a classmate can follow it
Variables"A meaningful identifier is assigned to each variable to access and perform operations on the value by name."lives_left, not x
Control"Programmers select and combine control structures, such as loops, event handlers, and conditionals, to create more complex program behavior."A loop inside a condition inside an event
Modularity"Programs use procedures to organize code, hide implementation details, and make code easier to reuse."Writing a function once and calling it many times
Parameters"Defining parameters for procedures can generalize behavior and increase reusability."The same function working for any message or any number
Troubleshooting"A systematic process will identify the source of a problem"Testing one change at a time and reading error messages
Online safety"People can be tricked into revealing personal information when more public information is available about them online."Thinking before posting a project with a real name or school

The shift from elementary

In the elementary statements the emphasis is on following and creating instructions. By grade 8 the emphasis moves to other people: readable algorithms, meaningful names, and solutions designed by "defining a problem’s criteria and constraints" and testing whether they were met.

That is a big change in mindset. A sixth grader often writes code only they understand; an eighth grader who meets these statements writes code a teacher, a teammate or their future self can pick up.

Where privacy and security come in

The grade 8 statements also bring in protection of information "such as encryption to maintain its confidentiality", which is why our example program below is a simple cipher.

They connect it to students’ own lives as well: the more that is public about someone online, the easier it is to trick them. We talk about that directly when students publish their first projects.

Quoted from the statements-by-grade-band page on k12cs.org, which we checked on 17 October 2026; short extracts only, as its licence allows. The framework sets grade-band endpoints, not a lesson for each grade; your state’s standards decide the detail.

What this means for your student

If your child’s school adopts AI Discoveries, their first unit of computer science may be about evaluating AI systems, bias and misinformation before they write much code. That is a sensible order, and it raises a question families ask us: will they still learn to program?

They will meet programming in later units, but a school elective is often a quarter or a semester. A weekly class outside school is where students go further with typed code and longer projects.

How we line up with it

We teach the same ideas in a different order: programming first, then AI, then the two together. A student who has written their own small classifier understands far better why an AI system can be confidently wrong.

Code.org and CodeAI have no connection with us. Their catalogue simply shows what many US middle schools teach, so we can start new students in the right place.

A real program

A Caesar cipher in Python: one function, two parameters

This is a program we write with middle schoolers in their first month of typed code. It is short, it does something they want (secret messages), and it meets two of the grade 8 statements head on: procedures that organise code, and parameters that make it reusable.

The procedure

def shift_message wraps the whole idea in one named block that hides the detail from the code that calls it.

The parameters

message and shift let the same function encrypt with 3 and decrypt with -3.

The condition

isalpha() leaves spaces and punctuation alone, a decision students usually add after their first bug.

Predict the output before you run it: four practice calls (ours; answers checked by running the function)
CallWhat it printsWhat it tests
shift_message("xyz", 3)abcWrapping past the end of the alphabet
shift_message("Zoo!", 1)App!Capitals and punctuation
shift_message("abc", 26)abcA full turn of the alphabet changes nothing
shift_message("Hi", -1)GhA negative shift goes backwards

The honest limitation

A Caesar cipher is easy to break: there are only 25 useful shifts, so a program can try them all in an instant. Students usually discover this themselves, and writing that brute-force loop is the next lesson.

That discovery is worth more than the cipher. It shows why real encryption is mathematical and why the grade 8 framework talks about protecting information rather than hiding it.

Where it leads

From here students write a function that counts letters, then use the counts to guess the shift automatically. That is their first taste of using data to make a decision.

Older middle schoolers take the same idea into JavaScript and put it on a web page their friends can use, which is a project for a real user, exactly what the framework asks for.

Which language

Choosing a first typed language in middle school

Parents often ask which language a middle schooler should learn. There is no single right answer, but there is a sensible order for most students, and it depends on what they want to make.

Typing matters more than the language

The real jump in middle school is from dragging blocks to typing exact syntax. A missing bracket or a wrong indent stops a program, and students need a few weeks of patient practice before that stops being frustrating.

Whichever language a student picks, the first month should be full of small programs that work, so the frustration never wins.

What about AI tools?

Many AI services set a minimum age in their terms, and those rules change. We never ask a student to create an account on a service that does not allow their age, and in class AI is something students study and test, not a shortcut.

Code.org lists an AI Chat Lab among the tools in its AI Discoveries course. If your student uses one at school, ask them to show you how they check its answers.

Why a real user in week 13

The grade 8 framework statement on program development is about solutions for other people. Building for a grandparent, a younger sibling or a club teaches more about clear design than any exercise, because real users press the wrong buttons.

The projects students remember are often tiny: a chore tracker, a spelling quiz for a little brother, a scoreboard for a family board game.

What the presentation is for

Explaining code out loud is how we check understanding. A student who can say why a function takes a parameter, in their own words, owns that idea.

Parents are welcome at the presentation lesson. It is usually the moment a family sees how much has changed since week one.

Beyond class

Contests and projects open to middle schoolers

Middle school is when students can start entering real competitions, and a project with a deadline is a strong reason to keep coding.

The Congressional App Challenge

Under the 2026 rules, students must be enrolled in middle school or high school on October 26th, 2026 to enter. Students compete in a congressional district that is hosting the challenge, where they live or where they go to school, and entries are judged by that district’s Member of Congress and their office, so the competition is local even though it runs across the country.

Our Congressional App Challenge guide covers the rules, the demo video and a ten-week build plan.

Other ways to stretch

Students who like puzzles can start on contest-style problems, and our USACO preparation page explains how that path works. Those who like making things can enter school science fairs with a coding project.

Our page on computer science extracurriculars lists more options, most of which open up in the last year of middle school.

Four stages through middle school coding

Where most students are when they join, and where they can be by the end of eighth grade.

Stages of a middle school coder (ours)
StageSigns of it
1. Block expertBuilds big Scratch projects, finds typing slow and fiddly
2. TypistWrites short Python programs with loops and conditions, fixes syntax errors alone
3. OrganiserSplits programs into functions with parameters, names things clearly
4. Builder for othersMakes a tool or game for a real user, tests it with them, improves it

For parents

Stage 2 is the hardest month. Encourage short, frequent practice rather than long weekend sessions.

Ask who the project is for. A student who can answer that is thinking at stage 4.

For students

Every error message is a clue. Read the line number first, then the last line of the message.

Write the function name before the function. If you cannot name it, you have not decided what it does yet.

Coding for middle schoolers: common questions

What coding should a middle school student learn?

By the end of grade 8 the K-12 Computer Science Framework expects readable programs that combine loops, events and conditionals, meaningful variable names, and procedures with parameters. In practice that means typed code, usually Python or JavaScript, and projects built for other people.

Is middle school too late to start coding?

No. Many students start in sixth or seventh grade and catch up quickly, because they read and type well. A beginner in middle school can go from first program to functions with parameters within a few months of weekly classes.

Should a middle schooler learn Python or JavaScript?

We start most students on Python: the code is short, it reads clearly, and what they learn transfers to any other language. JavaScript suits students who mainly want to build websites. Many learn Python first and add JavaScript in eighth grade.

What grades are middle school in the US?

Usually grades 6 to 8. NCES defines a middle school as one offering more of grades 5 through 8 than other grades, so some districts start middle school at grade 5 or use a grade 7 and 8 junior high.

What is AI Discoveries?

A Code.org middle school course for grades 6 to 8 that replaces CS Discoveries for the 2026 to 2027 school year. Its first unit is Thinking Critically About AI, and it can run as a full year, a semester or a quarter.

Can middle school students enter the Congressional App Challenge?

Yes. The 2026 rules say students must be enrolled in middle school or high school on October 26th, 2026. Our Congressional App Challenge guide covers the rules and a build plan.

Does my child join a kids course or a teen course?

Our kids courses run to age 12 and teen courses start at 13. Most sixth graders join a kids course and most eighth graders a teen course; we decide in the free first lesson by level, not birthday alone.

What times are classes for US families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible. Choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

How big are the classes?

Batches are fifteen to twenty students with one teacher who stays with them. If you want a teacher just for your child, 1-on-1 lessons are available too.

How much do middle school coding classes cost?

You pay nothing for the first lesson. After it a fee is charged each month, and the amount appears in US dollars in the fees section above.
