## Bigger buildings, more electricity: correlation in Oakville&rsquo;s 2017 facility energy file

The Town of Oakville publishes the energy used by its own facilities. We took the 2017 file and asked an MDM4U question: how strongly does floor area predict electricity use? Every statistic below is our calculation.

The Town describes the file as energy consumption data for town-owned infrastructure to meet the Green Energy Act 2009 (O. Reg. 397/11) annual reporting requirements. Each row is a facility, with its type, floor area in square feet, average hours open per week, electricity in kWh, natural gas in cubic metres and an energy intensity in equivalent kWh per square foot. Our download had 55 rows, of which 54 list both a floor area and an electricity figure.

**Our statistics on the Town of Oakville Energy Consumption at Town Facilities (aggregate) 2017 layer, downloaded 22 October 2026**

| Measure | All 54 usable rows | Without the eight 1 sq ft rows (46) |
|---|---|---|
| Correlation, floor area against electricity | r = 0.721 | r = 0.894 |
| Least-squares slope | 16.01 kWh per sq ft | 17.32 kWh per sq ft |
| Median energy intensity | 41.5 ekWh per sq ft | 32.72 ekWh per sq ft |
| Largest energy intensity | 5,190,942.75 ekWh per sq ft | 98.17 ekWh per sq ft |

Eight rows, all of type Other, give a floor area of exactly 1 square foot. No Town building is one square foot, so the 1 is a placeholder, and the file does not say what those rows measure. Left in, those rows sit at the far left of the scatter plot with large electricity values, pulling the correlation down to 0.721 and producing an energy intensity in the millions. Taken out, with the reason stated, the relationship is plainly strong. The program below computes both versions:

```
import json, math

def pearson(xs, ys):
    n = len(xs)
    mx, my = sum(xs) / n, sum(ys) / n
    sxy = sum((x - mx) * (y - my) for x, y in zip(xs, ys))
    sxx = sum((x - mx) ** 2 for x in xs)
    syy = sum((y - my) ** 2 for y in ys)
    return sxy / math.sqrt(sxx * syy), sxy / sxx

rows = [f["attributes"] for f in json.load(open("energy-2017.json"))["features"]]
usable = [r for r in rows if r["Total_Floor_Area"] and r["Electricity___Quantity"]]

for label, keep in (("all usable rows", usable),
                    ("floor area of 100 sq ft or more", [r for r in usable if r["Total_Floor_Area"] >= 100])):
    area = [r["Total_Floor_Area"] for r in keep]
    kwh = [r["Electricity___Quantity"] for r in keep]
    r, slope = pearson(area, kwh)
    print(label, "| n =", len(keep), "| r =", round(r, 3), "| kWh per sq ft =", round(slope, 2))
```

### Say why a row goes

Removing data to improve r is cheating; removing data because it cannot be a floor area is cleaning. The difference is the stated reason, and a student should write it down before running the numbers again.

### Strong is not causal

An r of 0.894 says bigger facilities tend to use more electricity. It does not say why: hours open, ice plants and pools all matter. Six of the rows are an indoor pool or ice rinks, which are energy-hungry for their size.

### Hours tell a story too

The average-hours field has a median of 65 hours a week and a maximum of 168, which is every hour of the week. Fourteen rows say 1 hour, probably another placeholder, and a good report flags them.

## Find the hidden number: how the Town turns gas into equivalent kWh

The energy intensity column combines electricity and gas into one unit, but the file never says how. A Grade 9 or 10 student can recover the rule with one equation. The data are the Town&rsquo;s; the problem and solution are ours.

**One row of the 2017 file: a Town community centre, ObjectId 2**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Floor area | 9,000 sq ft |
| Electricity | 24,380.63 kWh |
| Natural gas | 6,061.24 cubic metres |
| Energy intensity | 9.86646 ekWh per sq ft |

**Step 1, guess the model.** For rows with no gas, we found the intensity is exactly electricity divided by floor area. So suppose that for every row, intensity = (electricity + f &times; gas) &divide; area, where f is an unknown number of equivalent kWh per cubic metre of gas.

**Step 2, substitute.** 9.86646 = (24,380.63 + 6,061.24f) &divide; 9,000. Multiply both sides by 9,000: 88,798.14 = 24,380.63 + 6,061.24f.

**Step 3, solve.** 6,061.24f = 88,798.14 &minus; 24,380.63 = 64,417.51, so f = 64,417.51 &divide; 6,061.24 &asymp; 10.628.

**Step 4, test it on other rows.** One row could be a coincidence. Our program applied the same calculation to all 40 rows with a gas figure, and every one gave 10.628 to three decimal places. A constant that survives forty tests is very likely the conversion factor the Town used. We still call it our inference, because the file does not state it.

**Try it yourself.** Using f = 10.628, compute the energy intensity of a facility with 20,000 sq ft, 300,000 kWh of electricity and 15,000 cubic metres of gas. Then explain in a sentence why intensity, not total energy, is the fairer way to compare a small library with a large arena.

## If AI can compute r, what is left for an Oakville student to learn?

Deciding whether r means anything. That part has not been automated.

A spreadsheet or AI assistant will report r = 0.721 for the energy file in an instant. It will not stop to ask why eight buildings are one square foot, or whether an intensity of five million makes physical sense. The student who asks those questions is doing statistics; the one who copies the number is not.

In our lessons the student plots first, predicts the shape, then computes and compares, and always explain the result in words. School work that will be marked is written by the student.

MDM4U is a course in judging evidence. Tools make the arithmetic faster; they do not make the judgement for you. The longer argument is in [why the thinking behind the numbers still matters](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
