AI course for kids in Peru

Most children in Peru meet artificial intelligence as a voice that answers back. Very few get to see inside it. This page is for parents of children aged 6 to 12 who want the second thing: a course where a child builds a tiny machine that guesses, watches it get something wrong, and works out why. Below you will find what a child can realistically learn at each age, what INEI's 2026 figures say about how Peruvian children get online, which AI tools a child may and may not use, and one complete lesson.

In one paragraph

Modern Age Coders teaches an AI course for kids in Peru: live online classes, taught in English, for children aged 6 to 12. Children learn how a machine sorts, guesses and learns by building small projects themselves, first with cards and Scratch blocks and later in short Python programs, rather than by chatting to an adult AI app. Each class has a mentor based in India and either a group of fifteen to twenty classmates close in age or just your child, at an hour you pick on the Peru clock. What kids learn

What can a child of 6 to 12 actually learn about AI?

Far more than how to type a question into a chatbot. A child can learn the ideas that sit underneath every AI system, as long as each idea is something they can touch, sort or build.

A useful map comes from AI4K12, a United States initiative that writes guidelines for teaching AI in school from kindergarten to the end of secondary. In 2019 it published a poster of what it calls the "Five Big Ideas in AI", and its guidelines are arranged in grade-band progression charts, so the same idea returns at a deeper level as a child grows. We are not connected with AI4K12; we use its five ideas because they are clear, and because they make a parent's question easier to answer: which of these can my child do now?

What we leave out on purpose

Young children do not need neural network jargon, and they do not need to train a model on their own photos. Both make a course sound impressive while teaching very little, and the second hands a child's images to a service that was not built for them.

We also avoid "magic" demonstrations, where a screen does something clever and the child claps. If a child cannot explain, in their own words, why the machine gave its answer, the lesson has not finished.

What we keep

Sorting, guessing, counting mistakes and fixing them. These are the moves every AI system makes, and every one of them can be acted out with cards on a table before it becomes code on a screen.

The habit of asking "how would it know that?" A child who asks that question about a game, a search result or a video recommendation has understood the most important thing a course like this can teach.

Kids online in Peru

How many children in Peru use the internet, and on what?

About two in three children of primary age, mostly on a phone. INEI's household survey for the second quarter of 2026 puts internet use among 6 to 11 year olds at 68.2%, the lowest of any age group except people over 60, and rising faster than any other.

What the figures mean for a course

Subtract 68.2 from 100 and you get 31.8: our own sum says close to one primary-age child in three in Peru was not online at all during those three months. Any family reading this page is already in the connected two thirds, but the gap is a reminder that a live class needs planning, not just a link.

Among everyone who goes online, INEI found that 93.1% used the internet for entertainment such as games, films and music. For most children, AI arrives the same way: inside a game, a video feed or a voice assistant, long before anyone explains it.

The device question

INEI also reports that 90.2% of internet users aged 6 and over connect through a mobile phone, against 18.9% on a laptop, 14.0% on a desktop computer and 2.9% on a tablet. Computers are scarcer still: INEI counts one or more in just 36.4% of homes nationwide for April to June 2026.

A phone is fine for watching a class, but building a program on one is miserable for a child. Before the first lesson we ask for a laptop or desktop, even a shared or borrowed one, a stable connection, and a headset if possible. For 6 and 7 year olds, a parent nearby for the first few sessions helps.

What school covers

Do Peruvian primary schools teach artificial intelligence?

Not as a subject. Peru's national curriculum, the CNEB, has no computing or AI area in primary or secondary; digital skills appear as a transversal competency that every subject is meant to share.

In the curriculum

The CNEB lists competency 28, "Se desenvuelve en entornos virtuales generados por las TIC", among its competencies that cut across the subject areas. In practice that means a child may use a tablet in science or make a slideshow in communication, but nobody is required to teach how a program or an AI system works.

So a ten year old in a Lima or Arequipa primary school can be a skilled user of apps and still have no idea that a recommendation is a guess based on counting. That gap is not the school's fault; it is simply not in the plan of studies.

In the 2025 AI regulation

Peru regulated artificial intelligence through DS 115-2025-PCM, the regulation of Law 31814, published in El Peruano on 9 September 2025. Article 13.2 asks the government's digital transformation office to work with MINEDU on promoting the "uso responsable, ético, transparente e inclusivo de la IA" for student competencies within the national curriculum.

Article 12 adds that digital services built on AI must take full account of the different needs of girls, boys and adolescents, among other groups. We quote both as published context about where Peru is heading. They are not legal advice, and they do not mean any course, ours included, is approved by anyone.

Our classes sit alongside school and never replace it. If you want the Spanish-language version of this guide, written separately for Peruvian parents, read our curso de inteligencia artificial para niños page; the lessons themselves are taught in English on both.

This is why our AI course for kids is built mostly from things the child makes. A decision tree written in twenty lines of Python teaches more about how an AI system decides than an hour of chatting, and it involves no account, no data sent anywhere and no terms of service a child cannot legally accept.

From 13, the picture changes: with a parent's permission, a teenager can use some AI tools under supervision, and our teen courses introduce them carefully. Under 13, the honest course teaches the ideas and leaves the adult apps alone.

A real lesson

How does a child build an AI that guesses? A decision tree by hand

This is a lesson we teach to children from about 9, after a first session with paper cards. The program plays "guess the animal" with yes-or-no questions, and it chooses its own questions. Then we show it an animal it has never met.

  1. On paper first. Six cards: condor, hummingbird, llama, guinea pig, dolphin, frog. Each card says yes or no to four questions: has feathers, swims, has four legs, is bigger than a cat. The child must find a question that splits the pile as evenly as possible.
  2. Why evenly? A question that splits six cards into three and three always leaves at most three animals to sort; a question that splits them five and one usually leaves five. Fewer cards left means fewer questions to ask.
  3. Then in Python. The function best_question does exactly what the child did with the cards: it tries every question and keeps the one whose bigger half is smallest. The function build asks it again on each smaller pile until one animal is left.

The surprise

The bat has no feathers, does not swim, does not have four legs and is not bigger than a cat. The tree asks "four_legs?" (no), then "feathers?" (no), and lands on the only animal left on that branch: bat -> dolphin.

Children laugh, then go quiet. The program did nothing wrong by its own rules. It had never seen a bat, so it could only answer with something it already knew. That is the honest limit of every system that learns from examples.

The fix teaches the idea

We add one row for the bat and run the same code again. The tree grows a new question at the bottom: after "feathers?" it now asks "swims?", and the answer becomes bat -> bat.

Nothing about the code changed. Only the examples changed. A child who sees that has understood, in one afternoon, the sentence adults find hard: an AI system is only as good as what it was shown.

  1. Ages 6 to 8, cards only. Play the game with a friend using the six cards. Then add a card from your own garden or park. Did your questions still work?
  2. Ages 8 to 10, Scratch. Make a sprite that asks two yes-or-no questions and names one of four animals. Try it on an animal you did not plan for.
  3. Ages 10 to 12, Python. Add a fifth question of your own to the list in best_question. Does the tree choose it? Why or why not?
  4. Challenge. Find a pair of animals that no question in the list can tell apart, and explain in one English sentence what the tree would need.

The animals and their yes-or-no answers are our own simplification for a children's lesson; "bigger than a cat" is a judgement, and children are encouraged to argue about it. That argument is part of the lesson: somebody always decides what goes into the data.

Safety and fairness

Is AI safe for children, and how do you keep a course child-centred?

AI can be safe for children when adults make careful choices about tools, data and supervision. UNICEF's Guidance on AI and children, updated to version 3.0 in December 2025, sets out 10 requirements for child-centred AI, and three of them shape our kids' classes directly.

Protect children's data and privacy

UNICEF lists this among its 10 requirements. In our classes a child never uploads photos, voice recordings or personal details to an AI service. The data in our lessons is invented by us or typed by the child, like the six animals above.

Transparency and explainability

UNICEF asks that AI be transparent and explainable for children. We take that literally: every model a child meets in our course is small enough to print and read, so the child can point to the line that made the decision.

Prepare and skill children

The guidance also asks adults to "prepare and skill children for present and future developments in AI". That is the purpose of the course: not to make children fans of a product, but to make them the people who understand and question it.

UNICEF published its first draft guidance in September 2020 and a second version in 2021, before the third in December 2025. We are not connected with UNICEF; we cite its guidance because it is the most widely used child-rights framework for AI, and because parents deserve a standard they can check us against.

Two classroom rules follow. Children only use AI tools whose terms allow their age, and a child's mentor never asks for a home address, school name or photograph. If your child is shy on camera, cameras can stay off; we need to hear them and see their screen, not their face.

In English

Can my child learn AI in English if we speak Spanish at home?

Usually yes, if they can follow simple spoken instructions. The lessons are mostly short sentences, demonstrations on a shared screen and lots of doing, and the code itself reads the same in any language.

How mentors help

Mentors slow down, repeat an instruction in simpler words and show each step before asking the child to try it. Words like "question", "yes", "no", "guess" and "wrong" turn up so often that children absorb them within a few sessions.

For a 6 or 7 year old with very little English, we usually suggest one-to-one classes at first, so the mentor can pause as often as needed without holding a group back.

What families tell us

Many parents choose an English-taught course on purpose, because the child practises listening and speaking with a reason to do it: the game only works once they understand the instruction. Our page on clases de programación para niños en inglés explains that choice in Spanish for parents.

We do not teach in Spanish, and we would rather say so here than after a trial class. The free first class is the truest test: you will see in forty minutes whether your child can follow.

Parents in Peru ask

AI course for kids: questions parents ask

Are the classes in Spanish?

No, never: the teaching language is English from the first minute to the last, live over video. Our mentors keep their sentences short, slow down for a child who needs it, get used to the way your child speaks, and demonstrate before they ask. Most Spanish-speaking children settle in within a handful of lessons.

What age can a child start an AI course?

From 6, with sorting games and Scratch blocks that need no typing. From about 9 or 10, once reading and a little typing are comfortable, children can write short Python programs such as the decision tree on this page.

Does my child chat with ChatGPT in the course?

Not under 13. OpenAI's terms set a minimum age of 13 and require a parent's permission under 18; Anthropic sets 18 for Claude. Our kids' lessons are built from programs the child writes, which need no AI account at all.

What does a child actually build?

Small programs that sort, guess and learn from examples: a quiz that counts its own mistakes, a sprite that reacts to sound, a decision tree that plays "guess the animal". Each one is small enough for the child to explain line by line.

At what hours do the kids' AI classes run?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. Peru stays on UTC minus 5 all year and India on UTC plus 5:30, so 16:00 in Lima is 02:30 the next morning in India.

Does Peruvian school already teach AI?

Not as a subject. The CNEB treats digital skills as a transversal competency shared across subjects, with no computing or AI area. Peru's 2025 AI regulation asks for responsible AI to be promoted in the curriculum, but that is a direction, not a timetabled class.

What does my child need at home?

A laptop or desktop computer, a stable internet connection and ideally a headset. INEI found that 90.2% of Peruvian internet users connect by phone, but a phone is too small for building programs, so please plan a computer for class time.

How do you keep my child's data safe?

Children never upload photos, voice recordings or personal details to an AI service in our classes. The data they work with is invented for the lesson or typed by them. Cameras can stay off if your child prefers.

Are you connected with MINEDU or any school in Peru?

No. We are an independent online school with mentors in India, not connected with MINEDU, any school, AI4K12 or UNICEF. We do not award Peruvian qualifications; children who finish a course receive our own certificate of completion.

What happens in the free first class?

Your child plays a short sorting game, writes a first program and talks with the mentor. Then you hear the mentor's honest recommendation: which course, whether a group or one-to-one suits better, and which hours could work. No card details are needed to book it.
