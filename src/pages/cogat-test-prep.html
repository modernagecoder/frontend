The CogAT (Cognitive Abilities Test), published by Riverside Insights, measures verbal, quantitative and nonverbal reasoning in three batteries of three subtests each, and many US districts use it to screen for gifted programs, often in grade 2. What CogAT is

What is the CogAT test, and what does it measure?

The CogAT is a group ability test from Riverside Insights that samples reasoning rather than school learning. It reports three separate reasoning scores and a composite, compared with children of the same age.

In the publisher’s words

Riverside Insights’ overview brochure says that since 1968 the test has assessed "general, abstract reasoning skills", and that it reports "Verbal, Quantitative, and Figural (Nonverbal) reasoning in less than two class periods." Its product page puts it more simply: the CogAT "measures verbal, quantitative, and nonverbal reasoning".

The brochure describes "separate test levels for Grades K through 6 and banded levels for Grades 7-12", along with "two equivalent forms for quick retesting and two brief Screening Forms". The current full forms are called Form 7 and Form 8.

What that means for a young child

For kindergarten to grade 2, the brochure lists "Picture-based subtests and Alternate-Verbal administration for grades K through 2, requiring no specific language". A six-year-old does not need to read to take the primary levels; directions are read aloud, and online audio directions come in English, Spanish, Chinese (Cantonese and Mandarin), Arabic, Somali, Russian and Vietnamese.

From Level 9, which the brochure ties to grade 3, it says the Verbal and Quantitative items become text-based. That is the main reason a third grader’s experience of the test differs from a second grader’s.

Three batteries

What is on each CogAT battery for kindergarten to grade 2?

Each battery holds three short subtests, and each subtest asks the child to spot a relationship and apply it. The descriptions below are summarised from the publisher’s own table for Forms 7 and 8; we show no items.

One idea runs through it

Read the table again and the word "relationship" sits under almost every row. Analogies ask how two things relate. Classification asks what a group has in common. Series ask what rule moves from one step to the next. Paper folding asks how an action changes a shape.

That is good news for parents. A child who is used to saying "these go together because..." and "this one changes by..." already has the habit the whole test leans on, whatever the picture or number in front of them.

Grade 3 and up

From Level 9 the brochure lists the same nine subtests with text-based Verbal Analogies, Sentence Completion and Verbal Classification, a Number Puzzles test built on equations with missing values, and a Number Series test of plain numbers. Each subtest is listed at about 10 minutes.

The Screening Form, which some districts use first, takes only the three analogies subtests: Verbal or Picture Analogies, Number Analogies and Figure Matrices.

The brochure includes sample items for families. We do not copy or imitate them here: the publisher owns them, and a child who has memorised a look-alike question has learned a trick, not a way of thinking. If your district sends home its own familiarisation sheet, that is the right place to see the format.

How districts use it

How do school districts use CogAT scores?

Every district sets its own rules, so the same score can mean different things in different towns. Four public districts that publish their process show the range.

How four public school districts describe their CogAT use (district pages read 31 October 2026)
DistrictWho is testedWhat the district says
San Ramon Valley Unified, CaliforniaAll grade 2 students offered it in spring; newer students in grades 3 to 7 by registrationIdentified as GATE with a National Age Percentile Rank of 98 or above, or 97 plus a 99 in one battery

Dorchester School District Two, South CarolinaEvery grade 2 student, CogAT Level 8"The tests are not timed." Questions are pictorial apart from Sentence Completion
Charlotte-Mecklenburg Schools, North CarolinaAll second graders in general educationCogAT, i-Ready and the Gifted Rating Scales, with a portfolio route; outside testing not accepted

What the four have in common

Grade 2 is the common screening year. Rock Hill explains that "All students enrolled in a South Carolina public school participate in universal gifted and talented (GT) screening in Grade 2" through the CogAT and the Iowa Assessments, under State Regulation 43-220. Dorchester Two gives the state window as October 6 to 28, 2025.

Each district treats the CogAT as one piece of evidence. Charlotte-Mecklenburg gives each second grader "three opportunities- two formal and one informal", and lets students be rescreened after "at least 24 months".

Where they differ

Cut-offs differ. San Ramon Valley publishes its percentile rule; the South Carolina districts point to state option charts; Charlotte-Mecklenburg uses a rubric across several measures.

Outside tests differ too. Charlotte-Mecklenburg says plainly: "Outside testing is not accepted for gifted identification." Before paying anyone for anything, read your own district’s page and ask the gifted coordinator what counts.

Sources: San Ramon Valley Unified GATE Testing, Rock Hill Schools identification criteria, Dorchester School District Two, Grade 2 and Charlotte-Mecklenburg Schools gifted identification, all read 31 October 2026.

Should you prep?

Should you prepare your child for the CogAT?

Not in the cramming sense. The fairest preparation is a calm child who knows roughly what the session will feel like, and who has spent months playing with patterns, numbers and shapes for their own sake.

What one district says

San Ramon Valley Unified is unusually direct. Its GATE page says the district uses the CogAT "to measure the reasoning skills of students in the elementary grade level", and then: "Our district does not recommend any materials for CogAT preparation." It adds that students are encouraged to take the test with "minimal test preparation" so the district gets an accurate measure of reasoning.

The same page notes that "the testing may not be necessary for all students" and that it "may cause anxiety in some students". Parents there can exempt a second grader by telling the teacher.

Why drilling backfires

A child who has rehearsed hundreds of look-alike items may score higher than their reasoning on an ordinary day, and then land in a program paced for that score. That mismatch is rarely kind to a seven-year-old.

Drilling also teaches the wrong lesson: that there is a trick to find, rather than a pattern to understand. The habits that help on the test, such as slowing down, describing a relationship and checking it, are the same habits that help in class. Build those, and the test takes care of itself.

Reasoning at home

How does reasoning grow at ages 6 to 9, and what can you do at home?

Reasoning grows out of play, talk and number sense, not out of worksheets. These four activities are ours; they build the underlying skills and are deliberately unlike test items.

  1. Fold and predict (ours, spatial). Fold a square of paper in half, then in half again, and punch one hole through all the layers near the middle. Before unfolding, ask: how many holes will there be, and where? Then open it and check. Change one thing at a time: fold once, or three times, or punch near an edge.
  2. The input-output machine (ours, quantitative). You are the machine. Your child says a number; you say what comes out. Keep the rule secret, for example "add 3": 2 becomes 5, 4 becomes 7, 10 becomes 13. Your child wins by guessing the rule and predicting the next output before you say it. Then swap roles.
  3. Sort and say (ours, verbal and nonverbal). Tip out a handful of household things: a spoon, a pencil, a sock, a key, a leaf. Ask your child to make a group of three and say why they belong together. Then ask for a different group of three with a different reason. There is no single right answer; the explanation is the point.
  4. Equal towers (ours, number sense). Build one tower of blocks in two colors, say four red and three blue. Ask your child to build a tower with the same number of blocks using a different mix of colors, and to say how they know it matches. Move to bigger numbers as it gets easy.

Talking through the fold (ours)

Each fold stacks the paper on itself, so the number of layers doubles: one becomes two, two becomes four, four becomes eight. One punch goes through every layer, so the holes match the layers.

Where the holes land follows the folds too: they mirror across each crease. A child who says "it doubles every time I fold" and "the holes copy across the line" has discovered a rule, which is worth far more than one right answer.

Why these four, at this age

Between six and nine, children move from noticing to explaining. They can see that two things match before they can say why. Activities that ask for the "why" out loud speed that step up.

Number sense matters as much as puzzles. A child who knows that seven is four and three, or that ten is five and five, sees amounts in a picture without counting one by one, and that frees attention for the reasoning.

A note on screens. Short, well-made reasoning games are fine, but real objects teach spatial thinking better at this age: paper, blocks, puzzles, LEGO, cooking with measuring cups. For children under 13 we recommend only age-appropriate tools, and none that need an adult account.

Where your child is

Four stages of reasoning between six and nine

Our description of how young children usually grow, so you can meet your child where they are.

Stages of explaining a relationship (ours)
StageWhat you might hear
1. Noticing"These are the same." Points, but cannot say how.
2. Naming"They are both round." One feature at a time.
3. Ruling"It gets one bigger every time." States a rule and tests it.
4. Checking"It cannot be that one, because..." Rules out answers with a reason.

For parents

Most six-year-olds sit between stages 1 and 2; many eight-year-olds reach stage 3. Moving up a stage is a real gain, test or no test.

Ask "how do you know?" warmly, never as a challenge. The answer matters more than speed.

For children

If you are stuck, say out loud what is the same and what is different. That often shows you the answer.

It is fine to change your mind when you check. Good thinkers do it all the time.

Questions from parents

CogAT test prep: common questions

What is the CogAT test?

The Cognitive Abilities Test is a group ability test published by Riverside Insights. It measures verbal, quantitative and nonverbal reasoning in three batteries, and many US districts use it to screen children for gifted programs.

What grade do children take the CogAT?

The test has levels from kindergarten up, but grade 2 is the common screening year. San Ramon Valley, Rock Hill, Dorchester Two and Charlotte-Mecklenburg all screen second graders.

How long is the CogAT?

Riverside Insights lists each subtest at roughly 10 to 15 minutes at the primary levels, and the Screening Form at about 30 minutes. Dorchester School District Two tells parents its grade 2 tests are not timed, so check your own district letter.

Does my child need to read for the CogAT?

Not at the primary levels. The publisher describes picture-based subtests for kindergarten to grade 2, with directions read aloud. From Level 9, around grade 3, the Verbal and Quantitative items are text-based.

Can you study for the CogAT?

You can make a child comfortable with the format and grow their reasoning, but cramming look-alike items is not real preparation. San Ramon Valley Unified says it does not recommend any materials for CogAT preparation and encourages minimal test preparation.

What CogAT score is gifted?

There is no national cut-off; each district sets its own. San Ramon Valley, for example, publishes a National Age Percentile Rank of 98 or above, or 97 with a 99 in one battery.

Will a private CogAT test count?

It depends on the district. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Schools says outside testing is not accepted for gifted identification, so ask your district before arranging one.

How do your reasoning lessons work?

Children aged 6 to 9 meet a teacher live online for short sessions of original puzzles, real-object activities and talk about why an answer works. We show the general format once so the day feels familiar, and never drill test items.

When are classes held?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

Are you connected with Riverside Insights or my district?

No. We are an independent online school. We quote the publisher and district pages and link to them, and we cannot promise any score or gifted placement.
