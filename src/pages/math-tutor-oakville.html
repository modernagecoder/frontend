How can an Oakville student get live math tutoring?

Oakville students from Grade 1 to Grade 12 take live online math with Modern Age Coders, in a class of 15 to 20 working at one level or one to one with a teacher. Older students mostly join Ontario Grade 12 University Math (MHF4U and MCV4U) or Waterloo Math Contests: Gauss to Euclid; younger ones join a maths course matched to their age. The first lesson is free.

Much of the math an Oakville student meets after Grade 8 asks the same question in different clothes: when one quantity changes, what happens to another? Unit rates in Grade 7, linear relations in MTH1W, functions in Grade 11 and correlation in MDM4U are all versions of it. This page lines those courses up so a family can see where their student is on the path, with a link to our detailed page for each. Then it works through a real example from the Town of Oakville’s own open data: the energy used by Town facilities in 2017, where bigger buildings use more electricity, as expected, until eight strange rows make the relationship look weaker than it is.

The MTH1W row deserves a closer look, because it explains why the lesson below uses code. The ministry’s coding expectation C2.2 asks Grade 9 students to create code by decomposing situations into computational steps in order to represent mathematical concepts and relationships. In practice, that means writing the steps of a calculation clearly enough that a computer can follow them, which is also the clearest way for a student to show a teacher that they understand the steps.

MDM4U is usually the last place an Oakville student meets the full picture: two variables, a scatter plot, a correlation coefficient and a line. It is also where the most important caution in statistics is taught, that a summary number is only as trustworthy as the rows behind it.

Oakville data lesson

Bigger buildings, more electricity: correlation in Oakville’s 2017 facility energy file

The Town of Oakville publishes the energy used by its own facilities. We took the 2017 file and asked an MDM4U question: how strongly does floor area predict electricity use? Every statistic below is our calculation.

The Town describes the file as energy consumption data for town-owned infrastructure to meet the Green Energy Act 2009 (O. Reg. 397/11) annual reporting requirements. Each row is a facility, with its type, floor area in square feet, average hours open per week, electricity in kWh, natural gas in cubic metres and an energy intensity in equivalent kWh per square foot. Our download had 55 rows, of which 54 list both a floor area and an electricity figure.

Eight rows, all of type Other, give a floor area of exactly 1 square foot. No Town building is one square foot, so the 1 is a placeholder, and the file does not say what those rows measure. Left in, those rows sit at the far left of the scatter plot with large electricity values, pulling the correlation down to 0.721 and producing an energy intensity in the millions. Taken out, with the reason stated, the relationship is plainly strong. The program below computes both versions:import json, math def pearson(xs, ys): n = len(xs) mx, my = sum(xs) / n, sum(ys) / n sxy = sum((x - mx) * (y - my) for x, y in zip(xs, ys)) sxx = sum((x - mx) ** 2 for x in xs) syy = sum((y - my) ** 2 for y in ys) return sxy / math.sqrt(sxx * syy), sxy / sxx rows = [f["attributes"] for f in json.load(open("energy-2017.json"))["features"]] usable = [r for r in rows if r["Total_Floor_Area"] and r["Electricity___Quantity"]] for label, keep in (("all usable rows", usable), ("floor area of 100 sq ft or more", [r for r in usable if r["Total_Floor_Area"] >= 100])): area = [r["Total_Floor_Area"] for r in keep] kwh = [r["Electricity___Quantity"] for r in keep] r, slope = pearson(area, kwh) print(label, "| n =", len(keep), "| r =", round(r, 3), "| kWh per sq ft =", round(slope, 2))

Say why a row goes

Removing data to improve r is cheating; removing data because it cannot be a floor area is cleaning. The difference is the stated reason, and a student should write it down before running the numbers again.

Strong is not causal

An r of 0.894 says bigger facilities tend to use more electricity. It does not say why: hours open, ice plants and pools all matter. Six of the rows are an indoor pool or ice rinks, which are energy-hungry for their size.

Hours tell a story too

The average-hours field has a median of 65 hours a week and a maximum of 168, which is every hour of the week. Fourteen rows say 1 hour, probably another placeholder, and a good report flags them.

A worked problem, ours

Find the hidden number: how the Town turns gas into equivalent kWh

The energy intensity column combines electricity and gas into one unit, but the file never says how. A Grade 9 or 10 student can recover the rule with one equation. The data are the Town’s; the problem and solution are ours.

One row of the 2017 file: a Town community centre, ObjectId 2
FieldValue
Floor area9,000 sq ft
Electricity24,380.63 kWh
Natural gas6,061.24 cubic metres
Energy intensity9.86646 ekWh per sq ft

Step 1, guess the model. For rows with no gas, we found the intensity is exactly electricity divided by floor area. So suppose that for every row, intensity = (electricity + f × gas) ÷ area, where f is an unknown number of equivalent kWh per cubic metre of gas.

Step 2, substitute. 9.86646 = (24,380.63 + 6,061.24f) ÷ 9,000. Multiply both sides by 9,000: 88,798.14 = 24,380.63 + 6,061.24f.

Step 3, solve. 6,061.24f = 88,798.14 − 24,380.63 = 64,417.51, so f = 64,417.51 ÷ 6,061.24 ≈ 10.628.

Step 4, test it on other rows. One row could be a coincidence. Our program applied the same calculation to all 40 rows with a gas figure, and every one gave 10.628 to three decimal places. A constant that survives forty tests is very likely the conversion factor the Town used. We still call it our inference, because the file does not state it.

Try it yourself. Using f = 10.628, compute the energy intensity of a facility with 20,000 sq ft, 300,000 kWh of electricity and 15,000 cubic metres of gas. Then explain in a sentence why intensity, not total energy, is the fairer way to compare a small library with a large arena.

Math and AI

If AI can compute r, what is left for an Oakville student to learn?

Deciding whether r means anything. That part has not been automated.

A spreadsheet or AI assistant will report r = 0.721 for the energy file in an instant. It will not stop to ask why eight buildings are one square foot, or whether an intensity of five million makes physical sense. The student who asks those questions is doing statistics; the one who copies the number is not.

In our lessons the student plots first, predicts the shape, then computes and compares, and always explain the result in words. School work that will be marked is written by the student.

MDM4U is a course in judging evidence. Tools make the arithmetic faster; they do not make the judgement for you. The longer argument is in why the thinking behind the numbers still matters.
