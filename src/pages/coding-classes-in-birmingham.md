## One number that reconciles, and one claim that cannot be tested

Start with the number that holds up. The 2021 Census recorded Birmingham's resident population at **1,144,900**, an increase of **6.7 per cent, or 71,900 people**, since 2011.

That trio passes the simplest test there is. Total minus increase gives 1,073,000 for 2011. Divide 71,900 by 1,073,000 and you get 6.70 per cent. **The parts agree with the whole**, which is not something you can assume, and is the first thing worth checking about any published set of figures.

Now the claim. *Birmingham is the youngest major city in Europe, with under 25s accounting for nearly 40 per cent of its population.* It appears in city marketing, property brochures, news pieces, and the University of Birmingham's own City-REDI economic analysis. In the version we read, it is asserted **without a source citation and without a stated methodology**.

### Three unstated choices, each of which changes the answer

- **Which Birmingham?** The city council area, the built-up area and the wider travel-to-work area have different populations and different age profiles
- **Which cities were compared?** Europe has several hundred places that call themselves cities, and the answer depends entirely on which ones made the list
- **Which threshold?** Under 25 and under 30 give different rankings, and both numbers circulate

None of those three is usually specified, which means the claim cannot be checked, confirmed or refuted by anybody reading it.

To be clear, this does not make the claim false. Birmingham may well be young by any reasonable definition. The point is narrower: **a sentence that cannot be checked is not evidence, however often it is repeated and however respectable the person repeating it.**

## The Birmingham student data project

Every market page here works through one analytical trap. Birmingham's is the one we very nearly fell into while writing this page: **the boundary is part of the statistic.**

While researching, we found a figure that looked ideal: around 538,290 students in schools. Big, specific, quotable.

Read the label properly and it is students in **Birmingham and the West Midlands Metropolitan County**. That county also contains Coventry, Wolverhampton, Dudley, Sandwell, Solihull and Walsall. Quoting it as a Birmingham figure would have inflated the city by several other cities, and almost nobody would have noticed.

The learner is given several real statistics whose labels name different geographies: a council area, a metropolitan county, a built-up area, a travel-to-work area. They are asked to build a comparison table and quickly discover they cannot, because the rows are not about the same place. The programming task is then to attach a geography field to every figure and make the comparison function **refuse to compare** two records whose geographies differ.

That refusal is the lesson. Most tools will happily divide any two numbers you hand them. A function that checks whether a comparison is meaningful before performing it is a small amount of extra code and the difference between analysis and nonsense.

Then the same idea goes back to the claim at the top. Is Birmingham the youngest city in Europe? The learner writes a function to answer it and finds they cannot even start, because the function needs three parameters nobody has supplied. **Discovering a question is unanswerable as posed, and saying precisely which definition is missing, is a more useful outcome than an answer.**

The geographies and labels are real and are the ones we encountered. The 538,290 figure is quoted only as the example of the trap, and is not used anywhere as a Birmingham statistic.

## What is left for a person if AI writes the code?

Ask any assistant whether Birmingham is the youngest city in Europe. You will almost certainly get a yes with the under-25 figure attached, because that sentence appears thousands of times in the material these systems learned from. Repetition is exactly what they reproduce well.

What you will rarely get unprompted is that the claim has no published method, and that answering it needs a boundary, a comparison set and a threshold nobody specified. Not because the machine is stupid, but because the question did not ask, and it has no reason to volunteer that its answer rests on definitions it cannot see either.

So the work that stays with people is upstream of the code: deciding what a claim would have to mean before it could be tested, noticing when two numbers describe different places, and being willing to report that a question is unanswerable as asked.
