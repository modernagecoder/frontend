CyberPatriot preparation

CyberPatriot is the Air & Space Forces Association’s youth cyber defense competition. Teams of middle and high school students get virtual Windows and Linux computers full of security problems and have four hours to find and fix as many as they can, plus a Cisco networking quiz and exercise. This guide is built from the official CyberPatriot 19 (2026-27) pages: who is on a team, the round dates, the published points, the computer you need, the penalties that catch new teams, and a defense-only practice plan with a small script we wrote.

In short

CyberPatriot is a US team competition in which middle and high school students secure virtual Windows and Linux machines and complete a Cisco networking challenge in four-hour online rounds. Teams have one adult coach and two to six students, with five competing at a time. For CyberPatriot 19 (2026-27), Round 1 runs October 22 to 25, 2026, the State Round December 10 to 13, 2026, and the National Finals March 12 to 16, 2027.

Who is on a team

Coach, competitors, mentors: the CyberPatriot team rules

A team is built around one adult coach. Students cannot register themselves.

Team roles, from the official Competition Overview and Join the Competition pages
RoleRule
CoachAn adult approved by a participating organization, such as a teacher, JROTC instructor, administrator or parent. "A team can only have one coach."
Competitors"Each CyberPatriot team must consist of between two and six competitors" from the organization; five at most compete at once, with one substitution per round
Technical mentorAn IT-experienced volunteer who must register and pass a background check (Minimum age: 18)

Student sign-upStudents do not create volunteer accounts; the coach adds them to the roster, then each completes a questionnaire

If your child missed registration

For CyberPatriot 19, teams had to be created by October 1, 2026, and "Competitors must be added to a roster no later than October 21, 2026". After that, the season’s rosters are set.

A student who missed it can still prepare for the next season, CyberPatriot 20, and ask a teacher, JROTC instructor or club leader now about coaching a team when registration reopens.

The coach is present every round

The FAQ is clear that coaches must be present to monitor students during a round and make sure the rules are followed. A mentor can stand in only through the official alternate coach request.

Coaches also control the images and passwords, so practice outside the official rounds depends on what the coach makes available.

Choosing your four hours

Each competition weekend, a team picks one continuous four-hour block inside the published windows, in Eastern Time: Thursday 9:00 a.m. to 10:00 p.m., Friday 9:00 a.m. to 11:59 p.m., Saturday 8:00 a.m. to 11:59 p.m. and Sunday 10:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m.

For a team in California, our conversion: the Saturday window runs from 5:00 a.m. to 8:59 p.m. Pacific, so a 9:00 a.m. Pacific start is well inside it.

No make-up days

The FAQ says "There are no scheduled backup dates in CyberPatriot." An emergency date exists only for genuine emergencies, and the schedule page adds that scheduling conflicts are not considered emergencies.

Mark all the round weekends in the family calendar in September, before sports and travel fill them.

Inside a round

What happens in a four-hour CyberPatriot round

Two challenges share one four-hour period. Understanding the split is half the preparation.

The two CyberPatriot challenges, from the official Competition Overview and Training Materials pages
ChallengeWhat teams do
Network Security ChallengeSecure Windows and Linux virtual machine images; past tasks include hardening services, fixing vulnerabilities, removing malware and "Answering forensics questions"
Cisco Networking ChallengeAn online quiz and a Packet Tracer exercise on assigned Cisco Networking Academy training; past topics include subnetting and IP addressing
  1. Before the round. Image download instructions reach approved coaches on the Monday; check each download’s checksum and unzip it in advance.
  2. Start. On Thursday the StartEx email gives the image password. The four hours begin when the first image opens, so open nothing early.
  3. Read before you change. Go through everything the image gives you before touching a setting; training images, for example, carry a ReadMe file. Every fix should respect the scenario you are given.
  4. Fix and check. The Scoring Report on the image desktop shows points as fixes are recognised. Note every change, so a broken image can be rebuilt quickly.
  5. Cisco tasks. Someone on the team takes the networking quiz and the Packet Tracer exercise, which count separately.
  6. Finish. Stop within four hours, shut the image down properly and make sure every team member deletes the images afterwards.

Our suggested split for a five-person team (ours, not an official rule): two students per operating system image and one on the Cisco tasks, with the coach keeping time and the team swapping notes at the two-hour mark.

Tiers after Round 2

High school results from Round 2 divide teams into tiers for the State Round: Platinum for the top 30% of teams, Gold for the middle 40% and Silver for the remaining 30%. Middle school teams are not tiered.

For the Semifinals, the FAQ says the top 25% of Open Division teams in each tier advance, with wildcard places for unrepresented states, and the top 60% of Middle School teams advance on combined scores.

What the numbers mean for practice

Our reading: in Round 1 the two images carry most of the points and the Cisco challenge the rest, so image hardening is where most practice time belongs. From the State Round, the high school Cisco section grows, and networking deserves more time.

Scores are published 7 to 10 business days after a round ends, so a team learns its standing well after the weekend.

Penalties

Rules that cost new teams points

Most lost points in a first season come from procedure, not from skill. These are the rules the FAQ spells out.

Common penalties and procedures, from the official Competition FAQ
RuleWhat it means in practice
Overtime penaltyThe four-hour window "begins the moment they open the first virtual image in VMware"; opening an image early to test it starts the clock
Multiple instance penaltyThere must be "no more than a single copy of the same virtual image" open at once, even on different computers
No cloud hostingImages must not be run from vSphere or cloud hosting during rounds
No sharing between teamsTeams from the same school cannot share information from the images or the Cisco challenge
Restarting an imageA fresh copy starts at zero points, which is why notes of every change matter
Score correctionsOnly through the official form, by 11:59 p.m. Eastern on the last day of the round

The overtime rule is the one we drill first with new students. In a practice session we time four hours from the moment an image opens, so the habit of not peeking early is in place before Round 1.

The computer

The computer you need for CyberPatriot 19

The program keeps the requirements modest, but a few are firm. Check them in September, not on the Thursday of Round 1.

CP-19 technical requirements, from the official Technical Specifications page
ItemRequirement
ComputerA 64-bit host computer; all teams are required to have one
ProcessorIntel 64-bit i3 from the Sandy Bridge generation or better, or AMD Bulldozer or better, with virtualization enabled in the BIOS
Memory and disk8 GB of RAM and 40 GB of free disk space
Operating system64-bit Windows 10 with security updates after October 14, 2025, or Windows 11
Virtual machine softwareVMware Workstation Pro 26H1, the standard for CP-19, a free download
Other toolsWinMD5 to verify downloads and 7-Zip to unzip images; Cisco Packet Tracer 9.0.0
Mac or Linux hostsAllowed at the team’s own risk; problems are not grounds for appeal

Why a Chromebook will not do

The images run inside VMware Workstation Pro on a 64-bit Windows host. A typical school Chromebook cannot run it, so a student practising at home needs a Windows laptop that meets the specification.

The program recommends one host computer per image in a round; running two images on one machine makes the minimum specification inadequate.

A September checklist (ours)

Confirm 64-bit Windows under Settings, System, About. Turn on virtualization in the BIOS. Install VMware, 7-Zip and WinMD5. Open a training image once and shut it down cleanly.

Ask the school IT team to allow the sites on the official access list, such as uscyberpatriot.org and netacad.com, before the first round.

Practice, safely

Practice images and a small defensive script

Official practice images come only to registered coaches. We never distribute images or answer keys; we teach the thinking behind each fix.

Where official CyberPatriot practice comes from (Training Materials page)
PracticeWhat it is
Exhibition RoundsUnscored images for recruiting and training, in summer
Training RoundImages with answer keys for guided training
Practice RoundUnscored images just before the season, to check access
Cisco Networking AcademyA content course for the quiz and Packet Tracer, plus free self-paced courses such as Linux Unhatched and Introduction to Cybersecurity

The idea behind it: least privilege

A defender’s first question is always who has access, and should they? The script automates the reading of a long account list; the decision still belongs to the student, checked against whatever the scenario says.

Never delete an account just because a script flagged it. The scenario may require it, and removing an authorized user can cost points.

A hardening checklist (ours)

Accounts and passwords, then updates, then the firewall, then services that should not be running, then software that should not be installed, and finally the forensics questions, which are worth reading first because fixes can destroy the evidence they ask about.

Write every change in a shared team note with the time. It is the only way back from a frozen image.

CyberPatriot: common questions

What is CyberPatriot?

The National Youth Cyber Education Program created by the Air and Space Forces Association. Its core is the National Youth Cyber Defense Competition, where student teams secure virtual computers.

Who can compete in CyberPatriot?

High school students in the Open or All Service Divisions and middle school students in the Middle School Division, on a team from a participating school or approved youth organization.

How big is a CyberPatriot team?

Two to six competitors with one adult coach. Five students at most compete at one time in a round, with one substitution allowed each round.

Can my child sign up alone?

No. Students do not create accounts; a coach registers the team and adds competitors to the roster. For CyberPatriot 19, rosters had to be complete by October 21, 2026.

When are the CyberPatriot 19 rounds?

Round 1 is October 22 to 25, 2026, Round 2 and the Middle School Introductory Round November 12 to 15, the State Round December 10 to 13, the Semifinals January 21 to 23, 2027, and the National Finals March 12 to 16, 2027.

How long is a round?

Each team picks one continuous four-hour block in the competition weekend window, and the clock starts when the first image is opened.

What computer does a team need?

A 64-bit host with at least 8 GB of RAM, 40 GB of free disk space and virtualization enabled, running 64-bit Windows 10 with recent updates or Windows 11 and VMware Workstation Pro.

Are there practice images?

Yes, through registered coaches: summer exhibition rounds, a training round with answer keys, and a practice round in October. We do not share images or answer keys.

Does my teen need cybersecurity experience first?

No. The official overview says CyberPatriot is designed for any student, regardless of prior cybersecurity knowledge, and training materials come through the coach and Cisco Networking Academy.

When are your classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

How does Modern Age Coders help a CyberPatriot student?

We teach the defensive skills behind the rounds, from Windows and Linux hardening to networking and forensics questions, and rehearse the four-hour format. We never help during a round.
