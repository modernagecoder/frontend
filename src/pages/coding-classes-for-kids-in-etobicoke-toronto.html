Coding classes for kids in Etobicoke

Can an Etobicoke child aged 6 to 12 take coding classes with a live teacher?

Yes. Modern Age Coders gives Etobicoke children aged 6 to 12 live online coding lessons, either one to one or in a group of fifteen to twenty. Most start with Scratch Coding for Kids, web pages or app building; from about 9 they move to Python and AI for Kids. A real teacher leads every class, and the first lesson is free.

Patterns are the heart of Grade 3 math in Ontario, and coding is one of the ways children are asked to build them. A loop that adds 2 ten times is a skip-counting pattern a child can watch grow. In Etobicoke, as across the province, that happens inside the math timetable, between place value and fractions. Our classes give the pattern more room: a child sees it on paper, writes it as blocks, then makes it do something bigger. Below you will find the Grade 3 expectations in the ministry’s own words, a project that turns the City of Toronto’s public art list for the three Etobicoke wards into a decade chart, and what to know about this year’s Beaver Computing Challenge.

Grade 3 coding in Etobicoke

Repeating patterns: the Ontario Grade 3 coding an Etobicoke child meets

Etobicoke public schools teach the Ontario curriculum. The quotes below come from the Grade 3 math expectations and notes, read from the ministry’s curriculum content service on 22 October 2026; the second column is our approach.

Grade 3, overviewThe ministry’s summary of the year: “Students will write code to perform a repeating operation, such as a repeating number pattern.”
We start where Grade 3 already is: counting by 2s, 5s and 10s. A child says the pattern out loud, then builds the loop that says it for them.
Grade 3, C3.1Code that involves “sequential, concurrent, and repeating events”, now with repeating added to the list.
Repeat is the new block this year. We set one challenge in every lesson: find the part of your program that happens more than once and put a loop around it.
Grade 3, exampleA program in the notes: “Set value to 0. Repeat 10 times: Add 2 to Value, Output value.” It prints 2, 4, 6 and so on up to 20.
Children run it, then change the 2 to a 5 and the 10 to a 4 and predict the last number before pressing go. Guessing first, checking second, is the habit we want.
Grade 3, teacher notes“Loops can also help to emphasize the repetitive properties of some mathematical tasks and concepts.”
We use loops to show why multiplication is repeated addition: four loops of 3 steps land the sprite on 12, every time.

The ministry also suggests unplugged coding, away from the screen, such as “two characters mirroring one another’s patterns of movement”. We use the same idea at the start of class: one child calls out turns and claps, the other follows, and both find out quickly how exact an instruction has to be. The Grade 3 number strand has a line that ties straight into the project below: “Each decade repeats the 0 to 9 counting sequence.” Grouping years into decades is exactly that pattern, used on real dates. For the years before and after Grade 3, our Ontario coding guide for Grades 1 to 8 walks through each grade.

The Grade 3 EQAO year

Grade 3 is an EQAO year. EQAO lists 4 May to 8 June 2027 for the Reading, Writing and Mathematics assessment. Our classes are not test coaching, but patterns and number sense help with both.

Short, hands-on lessons

Eight-year-olds learn by doing. Our Grade 3 lessons switch every few minutes between talking, trying and fixing, so attention never has time to drift far.

Reading still growing

At this age reading speed varies a lot, so we keep instructions spoken and visual. Blocks carry short words, and the teacher reads them aloud whenever a child asks.

Our place beside school

Modern Age Coders teaches outside school hours and gives no school marks. It has no connection with the Ontario Ministry of Education, EQAO, any Toronto school board or the City of Toronto.

Etobicoke data project

When did Etobicoke get its public art? A decade chart from the City’s own list

The City of Toronto publishes its public art inventory as open data: artworks owned by the City and works delivered through its percent-for-art program with private developers. Our older kids keep the rows in wards 1, 2 and 3, the three wards whose names begin with Etobicoke, and count the works by decade. That ward choice is ours, and every number below is our own count from the file downloaded on 22 October 2026.

The file holds 459 rows for the whole city; 25 of them sit in the three Etobicoke wards, 15 in ward 3, 7 in ward 1 and 3 in ward 2. The newest work is dated 2024 and the oldest 1930, and the 2020s already have more works than any earlier decade. Here is the program an 11-year-old writes, using only the Python standard library:import csv decades = {} with open("public-art.csv", encoding="utf-8") as f: for art in csv.DictReader(f): if art["WARD"] in ("1", "2", "3"): year = art["YEAR"] if year.isdigit(): label = str(int(year) // 10 * 10) + "s" else: label = "unknown" decades[label] = decades.get(label, 0) + 1 for label in sorted(decades): print(label, "#" * decades[label])

Decades by division

Integer division throws away the remainder: 1997 // 10 is 199, and times 10 is 1990. Adding an s gives the label. It is the Grade 3 decade pattern, done by a computer.

When the year is missing

One row says unknown instead of a year. Calling int on it would crash the program, so isdigit checks first, and the row lands in its own group rather than vanishing.

A chart from symbols

Multiplying a string repeats it, so "#" * 8 prints eight marks. A child sees the 2020s bar is longest without any charting library at all.

Why we match the number, not the name

Our first try matched the ward names exactly as the City’s ward file spells them and found 21 works. Four were missing. The art file writes ward 2 as Etobicoke-Centre, with a hyphen, and one ward 3 row adds (3) to the name. Matching the WARD number instead finds all 25. Across the city, 36 rows put a number in brackets after the ward name. Real files are typed by people, and a good program trusts the code column over the label.

There is more for a curious class. Sculptures make up 20 of the 25, with two murals or paintings, two pieces of architecture or landscape and one multidisciplinary work. 13 belong to the City’s own collection and 11 came through the developer program. Searching the materials column, after making everything lower case, finds steel in 9 rows and bronze in 5; a single row can name more than one material, so those numbers overlap and must not be added together. The package notes say the inventory “shows over 400 works of public art throughout the City of Toronto”, which matches the 459 rows we downloaded. We print counts, decades and materials only, never artists, titles or descriptions. Contains information licensed under the Open Government Licence - Toronto (licence terms, Public Art dataset).

Contest season

The Beaver Computing Challenge in November 2026: what Etobicoke parents should know this week

The Beaver Computing Challenge (BCC) from CEMC at the University of Waterloo is a logic contest with a level for Grade 6 and younger. It runs in schools, so timing depends on a school ordering it. Details are CEMC’s, read on 22 October 2026.

The deadline is close

CEMC gives Monday 26 October 2026 as the ordering deadline for this year’s BCC, which runs from 9 to 20 November 2026. A school that has not ordered by then may miss this year, so a quick question to the teacher now is worth it.

Last year’s numbers

CEMC reports 14 260 contestants at the Grade 5/6 level in 2025, with a mean score of 37.22 out of 60. It is a contest many children write, and an average score sits well below full marks.

Not a test

CEMC puts it plainly: “Our contests are not tests or exams. There is no passing or failing score.” We tell children the same: the point is to enjoy hard puzzles, not to be perfect.

Entry goes through school, since CEMC says all students must participate through their school. Most Etobicoke children in our Grade 3 and 4 classes are still a year or two from the Grade 5/6 level, and that is fine: a child who has spent those years predicting what a loop will print is already practising the reasoning the BCC rewards. We teach with puzzles we write ourselves, labelled as ours, and send families to CEMC for past contests. One of ours for an eight-year-old: a sprite starts at 0 and repeats “add 3” seven times; where does it stop, and how many times would it need to repeat to pass 30? The Beaver Computing Challenge guide covers every level, and the Canada contest calendar lists the rest of the year. Modern Age Coders has no tie to CEMC or the University of Waterloo.

Class times

Etobicoke class times, the 1 November clock change, and India time

Etobicoke keeps Eastern time. Clocks go back on 1 November 2026 and forward on 14 March 2027, while India’s clock never moves, so the gap between the two changes by an hour. Our conversions, from the IANA time zone offsets (release 2026e):

Weekday, 5:30 pm

Until 1 November 2026 (Eastern at UTC-4) that is 3:00 am the next day in India. From 1 November to 14 March 2027 (UTC-5) it is 4:00 am the next day.

Sunday, 11:00 am

Until 1 November 2026 that is 8:30 pm the same day in India. During the winter months it is 9:30 pm, then 8:30 pm again from 14 March 2027.

What we send

Every Etobicoke family gets times in Eastern. Ahead of each change we confirm the next class again, because a child logging in an hour off is a lesson nobody needs.

Etobicoke phone numbers use the City of Toronto area codes, 416 and 647, with 437 and 942 in the same area. Enter the ten digits in the form; the +1 is filled in already. Coding and AI

Does an Etobicoke child still need to code if AI can do it?

Yes, because a child who can code can tell when the computer is wrong.

The public art project shows it. A program that matched ward names would have reported 21 works and looked perfectly confident. Only someone who checked the result against the ward numbers noticed four were missing. AI tools make that kind of quiet mistake too, and the defence is a person who knows what the right answer should look like.

We do not put adult chatbots in front of children under 13. Instead they learn, through teacher-led activities, how AI systems learn from examples and why they get things wrong. Most of each lesson is the child thinking: breaking a problem into steps, trying one, and checking whether it worked.

Grade 3 asks children to spot what repeats in a pattern, and seeing structure for yourself is the skill that keeps a person in charge of the tools. The longer argument is in the case for coding in an AI world.

FAQ

Etobicoke parents’ questions

The questions Etobicoke families send us most.

Are there coding classes for kids in Etobicoke?

Yes. We teach Etobicoke children live and online, so there is no centre to drive to. Your child joins a group of fifteen to twenty or learns one to one.

What coding does my child do in Grade 3?

Ontario Grade 3 math asks children to write and change code with repeating events, which usually means loops that build number or shape patterns. It is taught as part of math.

Is my 6-year-old too young?

No. Six, around Grade 1, is a good time to start with simple sequences and unplugged games. Our kids classes continue to age 12.

Does my child need to read well to code?

Not at first. Younger children work with blocks and pictures, and the teacher reads instructions aloud. Typing Python comes later, once reading is easy.

Scratch, web pages or Python: which should we choose?

For children under 9, usually Scratch. Children who love making things to show others often enjoy web pages from about 8. Python suits most children from 9 or 10.

What times can my child take classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Times are given in Eastern time.

How does my child enter the Beaver Computing Challenge?

Through school only. CEMC lists Monday 26 October 2026 as the ordering deadline for the November 2026 contest, so ask the teacher soon. The youngest level is for Grade 6 and below.

Will my child use AI chatbots?

No. Our students under 13 do not use adult chatbots, and ChatGPT needs a parent's permission for under-18s. Children learn how AI works through activities led by the teacher.

Is there a classroom we can visit?

No. Modern Age Coders has no Etobicoke premises. All classes are live online, which also means no travel time after school.

How much do the classes cost?

The fees block on this page lists the free first lesson and the monthly group and private options in your currency. You pay nothing until after the free lesson.
