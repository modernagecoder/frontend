## Grade 1 and Grade 2: the coding a Scarborough child meets first

Scarborough public schools teach the Ontario curriculum. We read the coding expectations from the ministry&rsquo;s curriculum service on 22 October 2026. Quoted words are the ministry&rsquo;s; the right-hand column is what we do with them.

- **Grade 1, C3.2**: Children &ldquo;read and alter existing code, including code that involves sequential events, and describe how changes to the code affect the outcomes&rdquo;. Our answer: We hand a six-year-old a short program that almost works, ask what it will do before it runs, and let the child change one block at a time. Saying the prediction aloud is the habit we are after.
- **Grade 2, C3.1**: Writing and running code, &ldquo;including code that involves sequential and concurrent events&rdquo;. Our answer: Concurrent means two things at once: a cat and a dog sprite crossing the stage together. In our Scratch classes the child times them so they meet in the middle, and works out why they did not.
- **Grade 2, teacher notes**: The ministry&rsquo;s own example: &ldquo;Students will develop code to move multiple objects from one location to another on a grid at the same time&rdquo;. Our answer: We use a squared grid on paper first, then the screen. Counting squares turns the code into arithmetic a Grade 2 child already knows.
- **Grade 2, teacher notes**: &ldquo;Changing the sequence of instructions in code can sometimes produce the same outcome and can sometimes produce a different outcome.&rdquo; Our answer: A favourite puzzle in our classes: swap two lines, then decide whether anything changed. Children learn that order sometimes matters and sometimes does not, and to test rather than guess.

Grades 3 to 7 keep building on this ladder, with repeating events, nesting, conditions, efficient code and subprograms, and our [guide to Ontario coding from Grade 1 to Grade 8](/ontario-coding-curriculum-grades-1-to-8) sets out each year. The point for a Scarborough parent is that coding at school is part of math, assessed alongside everything else, and squeezed into a strand that shares the year with number, geometry and data. Children who enjoy it usually want more of it than the timetable allows.

## What can a 9-year-old drop in to this week in Scarborough? Counting the City&rsquo;s own file

The City of Toronto publishes its recreation schedule as open data. Our older kids, around ages 10 to 12, join two of its tables, keep only Scarborough, and count the sessions a child of a given age can join. Every number below is our count, not the City&rsquo;s.

**Our count of drop-in sessions at locations whose District is Scarborough, from the City of Toronto file downloaded 22 October 2026 (sessions dated 15 October to 26 November 2026)**

| Child&rsquo;s age | Sessions open to that age | Scarborough locations offering one |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 639 | 22 |
| 7 | 1,052 | 23 |
| 8 | 1,052 | 23 |
| 9 | 1,070 | 23 |
| 10 | 1,100 | 23 |
| 11 | 1,125 | 24 |
| 12 | 1,131 | 24 |

The file is Registered Programs and Drop In Courses Offering, published by Parks, Forestry & Recreation and refreshed weekly. Its notes say a drop-in &ldquo;is a program offered within PFR facilities where registration is not required&rdquo;. Two of its tabs matter here: Drop-in, one row per session, and Locations, one row per place, with a District column. Our download held 7,039 drop-in rows at Scarborough locations, spread over 39 of them. Here is the script a 10-year-old can type, using nothing but the Python standard library:

```
import csv

district = {}
with open("locations.csv", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        district[row["Location ID"]] = row["District"]

age = 9
days = {}
with open("dropin.csv", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        if district.get(row["Location ID"]) != "Scarborough":
            continue
        low = int(row["Age Min"])
        high = 120 if row["Age Max"] == "None" else int(row["Age Max"])
        if age in range(low, high + 1):
            day = row["DayOftheWeek"]
            days[day] = days.get(day, 0) + 1

print(sum(days.values()), days)
```

### 1. Join the tables

A session row only gives a Location ID. The child builds a small dictionary from Locations, ID to District, then looks each session up in it. That is a join, the same idea a database uses, written in four lines.

### 2. Filter by age

Each row has Age Min and Age Max. A blank maximum means no upper limit, so the script treats it as 120. A session fits a 9-year-old when 9 sits inside the range, which Python checks with range.

### 3. Count by day

Every fitting row adds one to its weekday. For age 9 we got Saturday 191, Thursday 174, Friday 154, Sunday 150, Wednesday 144, Monday 137 and Tuesday 120: 1,070 in all, swim and skate making up most of them.

### The trap in the data dictionary

The City&rsquo;s field note for Age Min says &ldquo;minimum allowable age in months&rdquo;. The values tell a different story: ranges such as 0 to 5, 6 to 12 and 13 to 18, and a youth council open from 13 to 24. Those only make sense as years, so we read them as years and say so. Spotting that a label and its data disagree is a real skill, and children enjoy catching a grown-up file being wrong.

What the counts do not say matters as much. 273 of the sessions open to a 9-year-old name a caregiver or family in the title, so an adult has to come too. A count of sessions is not a count of places on the ice, and the file is a snapshot: change the download date and the numbers move. A good next step for the class is the after-school question: of the 1,070, how many start at 3 pm or later on a weekday? Our answer was 315. Data used under the Open Government Licence - Toronto. ## Should a Scarborough child learn to code when AI can write code?

Yes, and for the same reason children still learn to add when calculators exist.

The drop-in project above shows why. A tool can produce the script, but only a child who understands it notices that the age field says months and the numbers say years, or that a session needing a caregiver is not one a child can attend alone. Knowing what to doubt comes from having built the loop by hand.

Children under 13 in our classes do not use adult chatbots. They learn what AI is, how a model guesses from examples, and where it goes wrong, through age-appropriate tools and activities a teacher runs. Above all they practise thinking a problem through, step by step, and checking the answer rather than copying one.

Ontario put coding into Grade 1 math to teach step-by-step reasoning, and that reasoning is the part no tool can do on a child&rsquo;s behalf. The longer argument is in [is coding still worth learning in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
