Three big ideas

The first big idea says algebra allows us to generalize “relationships through abstract thinking.” The second says that, as with numbers, “operations extend to powers, radicals, and polynomials.” The third says quadratic relationships “are prevalent in the world around us.”

A fourth statement frames the trigonometry: “Trigonometry involves using proportional reasoning” to solve indirect measurement problems. The elaboration gives a lovely example: “calculating the width of a river using the distance between two points on one shore and an angle to a point on the other shore”. That is a sine law problem, and a very BC one.

Questions worth asking aloud

The sample inquiry questions point straight at test traps. “What is the connection between domain and extraneous roots?” is the radical-equation lesson in one sentence. “How do we tell if a mathematical solution is reasonable?” is the habit that catches them.

Two more make good revision prompts: “How is the cosine law related to the Pythagorean theorem?” and “When would we choose to represent a number with a radical rather than a rational exponent?” A student who can answer both in their own words understands the trigonometry and powers units.

What Grade 10 should leave behind

Pre-calculus 11 assumes a student can factor a trinomial, apply exponent laws with integer exponents, and work with lines and their equations fluently. When those are shaky, Grade 11 feels like learning two courses at once.

Our first lesson with a new Grade 11 student includes a short Grade 10 check: three factoring questions, three exponent questions, one line through two points. Whatever wobbles goes on the plan for the first month.

What Grade 12 will build on

Almost everything in Pre-calculus 11 returns in Pre-calculus 12: factoring becomes the factor theorem, rational expressions become rational functions with asymptotes, and the degrees-only unit circle becomes the radian one. Our Grade 12 math comparison shows where Pre-calculus 12 sits among the provinces’ courses.

Students choosing between Pre-calculus 11 and Foundations of Mathematics 11 should know that many UBC programs list Pre-Calculus 12 for BC applicants, Science among them. The Grade 11 choice keeps that route open or makes it longer.

Worked problem 1, ours

A quadratic in context: a launched ball

This problem is ours. It touches four Pre-calculus 11 skills in one story: completing the square, reading a vertex, solving a quadratic inequality by sign analysis, and restricting a domain to what makes sense.

The problem. A ball is launched upward from a platform. After t seconds it is h(t) = −5t2 + 20t + 1 metres above the ground. (a) Find the greatest height and when it happens. (b) For how long is the ball at least 16 m up? (c) When does it land, and what domain makes sense for h?

  1. Complete the square. Factor −5 from the t terms: −5(t2 − 4t) + 1. Half of −4 is −2, and (−2)2 = 4, so add and subtract 4 inside: −5[(t − 2)2 − 4] + 1 = −5(t − 2)2 + 21.
  2. Read the vertex. The vertex is (2, 21). The parabola opens down, so the greatest height is 21 m, at t = 2 s.
  3. Set up the inequality. h(t) ≥ 16 means −5t2 + 20t − 15 ≥ 0. Divide by −5 and flip the sign: t2 − 4t + 3 ≤ 0, that is (t − 1)(t − 3) ≤ 0.
  4. Use sign analysis. The zeros 1 and 3 split the line into three intervals. Testing t = 0, 2 and 4 gives positive, negative, positive for (t − 1)(t − 3). The product is at most 0 on the closed interval [1, 3].
  5. Answer in context. The ball is at least 16 m up from t = 1 s to t = 3 s, so for 2 seconds.
  6. Find the landing time. Solve h(t) = 0 with the quadratic formula: t = 2 ± √(21/5), so t ≈ 4.049 or t ≈ −0.049. Reject the negative root: time starts at launch. The sensible domain is 0 ≤ t ≤ about 4.049, and the range is 0 ≤ h ≤ 21.

The sign flip

Step 3 divides an inequality by a negative number, and the inequality sign reverses. Forgetting that turns the right answer, [1, 3], into its opposite: t ≤ 1 or t ≥ 3, which says the ball is high up before launch and after landing. Reading the answer in context catches it immediately.

That is the inquiry question “How do we tell if a mathematical solution is reasonable?” in action: the algebra produced an answer, and the story decides whether to trust it.

Why the domain matters

The formula h(t) is defined for every real t, but the situation is not. The curriculum lists “domain and range restrictions from problems in situational contexts” for exactly this reason. A negative time or a negative height has no meaning here.

Students who write the restricted domain before solving tend to reject the negative landing time without a second thought, and the marks for interpretation follow.

Worked problem 2, ours

A radical equation with an extraneous root, solved twice

The curriculum asks students to solve one-radical equations “algebraically and graphically” and to identify extraneous roots. This problem of ours does both, and uses a few lines of Python as the graph.

The problem. Solve √(2x + 9) = x + 3. State the domain restriction, find every candidate, and decide which candidates are real solutions.

  1. Restrict the domain first. The expression under the root cannot be negative: 2x + 9 ≥ 0, so x ≥ −4.5. A square root is never negative either, so the right side must satisfy x + 3 ≥ 0, that is x ≥ −3.
  2. Square both sides. 2x + 9 = (x + 3)2 = x2 + 6x + 9.
  3. Solve the quadratic. 0 = x2 + 4x = x(x + 4), so the candidates are x = 0 and x = −4.
  4. Check each candidate in the original equation. At x = 0: √9 = 3 and 0 + 3 = 3. Keep it. At x = −4: √1 = 1 but −4 + 3 = −1. Reject it.
  5. Explain the rejection. Squaring turned −1 into 1, so the squared equation accepted a value the original never could. The restriction x ≥ −3 from step 1 already ruled out −4.
  6. Confirm graphically. The program below compares the two sides at each candidate, then scans the domain for every place the graphs meet.

Domain and extraneous roots, connected

This is the curriculum’s inquiry question about domain and extraneous roots, answered with one example. Squaring is not reversible: it erases signs. Any value that makes the right side negative can sneak through the squared equation, and the domain restriction on the right side names exactly those values.

Students who write both restrictions in step 1 can often reject an extraneous root before they compute it, and they still check, because checking is quicker than losing marks.

Why the graph helps

Algebra produced two candidates and the graph showed one crossing. When the two disagree, the graph is telling you something real about the original equation. Graphing technology is named in the curriculum’s competencies for exploring relationships, and a short program does the same job.

Our scan uses steps of 0.5, which would miss two crossings very close together. For a test, the algebraic check is the proof; the scan is the sanity check.

Practice, ours

Twelve Pre-calculus 11 practice questions

Twelve questions of our own, not lifted from any classroom, district test or exam. They move through the year in order, powers first and triangles last. Try every one on paper before looking below.

  1. Rational exponents. Evaluate 27−2/3, and simplify (8x6)2/3.
  2. Radicals. Simplify √50 + 3√8 − √18.
  3. Ordering irrationals. Put in order from least to greatest, without a calculator first: 2√3, 3√2, √11, 3.3.
  4. Trinomial. Factor 6x2 − x − 15.
  5. Difference of squares. Factor 25x2 − 36y2.
  6. Rational expression. Simplify (x2 − 9)/(x2 + 5x + 6) ÷ (x − 3)/(x + 2), and state every non-permissible value.
  7. Rational equation. Solve x/(x − 3) = 3/(x − 3) + 2.
  8. Vertex form. Write y = 2x2 − 12x + 13 in vertex form. Give the vertex, axis of symmetry, range and x-intercepts.
  9. Inequality. Solve x2 − 2x − 15 > 0 and write the answer in interval notation.
  10. Sine law, ambiguous case. In triangle ABC, angle A = 35°, a = 7 and b = 11. How many triangles are possible? Solve each one.
  11. Cosine law. Two sides of a triangle are 8 cm and 11 cm with a 40° angle between them. Find the third side to two decimal places.
  12. Standard position. For an angle of 150° in standard position, give the reference angle and the exact values of its sine and cosine.

Answers, ours

Answers and the reasoning behind them

We confirmed every result by computer algebra (SymPy 1.14) or by running Python. Read the reasoning even when your number matches; it is what the marker looks for.

  1. 1/9 and 4x4. The cube root of 27 is 3, squared is 9, and the negative exponent gives the reciprocal. For the second, the cube root of 8 is 2 and squared is 4; x6 to the power 2/3 is x4.
  2. 8√2. √50 = 5√2, 3√8 = 6√2 and √18 = 3√2, so 5 + 6 − 3 = 8 of them.
  3. 3.3, √11, 2√3, 3√2. Square everything: 10.89, 11, 12 and 18. Squaring keeps the order for positive numbers.
  4. (2x + 3)(3x − 5). Find two numbers that multiply to 6 × (−15) = −90 and add to −1: −10 and 9. Split and group: 6x2 − 10x + 9x − 15.
  5. (5x − 6y)(5x + 6y). Both terms are perfect squares and they are subtracted.
  6. 1, with x ≠ −3, −2, 3. Factor: (x − 3)(x + 3)/[(x + 2)(x + 3)] × (x + 2)/(x − 3). Everything cancels. The excluded values come from every denominator, including the divisor’s numerator x − 3, which becomes a denominator when you flip it.
  7. No solution. Multiply by x − 3: x = 3 + 2(x − 3), so x = 3. But 3 is non-permissible, so the only candidate is extraneous.
  8. y = 2(x − 3)2 − 5. Vertex (3, −5), axis x = 3, range y ≥ −5, x-intercepts 3 ± √10/2, about 1.42 and 4.58.
  9. (−∞, −3) ∪ (5, ∞). Factor as (x − 5)(x + 3) > 0. Testing an interval each side and between the zeros shows positive outside, negative between. Round brackets, because the inequality is strict.
  10. Two triangles. sin B = 11 sin 35° / 7 ≈ 0.9013, so B ≈ 64.3° or 115.7°. Both leave room for C: 80.7° with c ≈ 12.04, or 29.3° with c ≈ 5.98. The code below does the same check.
  11. About 7.08 cm. c2 = 82 + 112 − 2(8)(11) cos 40° = 185 − 176 cos 40° ≈ 50.18. With a 90° angle the cosine term vanishes and the law becomes Pythagoras, which answers that inquiry question.
  12. Reference angle 30°; sin 150° = 1/2, cos 150° = −√3/2. 150° is in the second quadrant, where sine is positive and cosine negative. The values come from the 30-60-90 triangle.

A plan for the year, ours

How to get through Pre-calculus 11 without falling behind

Because each topic depends on the last, Pre-calculus 11 rewards steady work more than end-of-year cramming. This plan is ours; fit it to the order of your teen’s course outline.

  1. Run the Grade 10 check in the first two weeks. Factoring, exponent laws, lines. Fix gaps before radicals arrive.
  2. Learn the exponent and radical forms as one idea. Practise rewriting each radical as a power and back until it is automatic.
  3. Write restrictions before solving. For every radical or rational equation, write the domain restriction or non-permissible values first.
  4. Check every candidate root. Substitute into the original equation and write the check down, even when you are sure.
  5. Solve each quadratic two ways. One method you choose, one you would not, then compare. The curriculum lists five methods for a reason.
  6. Draw a sign chart for every inequality. Zeros, intervals, test values, then interval notation.
  7. Sketch every triangle. Label the given parts before deciding between the sine and cosine laws. For the sine law with two sides and a non-included angle, test the supplement every time.
  8. Keep a mistakes page. Log each error, then mark it as carelessness or as an idea that needs teaching again.
  9. Review one old unit each week. Ten minutes on a past topic stops it fading before the final.
  10. Before each unit test, do a mixed set. Our twelve questions above make a reasonable start.

Questions from BC families

Pre-calculus 11: questions families ask

What does Pre-calculus 11 cover in BC?

In BC's curriculum, Pre-calculus 11 includes the real number system, rational exponents, radicals and radical equations, polynomial factoring, rational expressions and equations, quadratic functions and equations, linear and quadratic inequalities with sign analysis and interval notation, and trigonometry with the sine and cosine laws, including ambiguous cases.

Do you grant Pre-calculus 11 credit?

No, we do not grant provincial credits. Our lessons run beside the school course. To earn Pre-calculus 11, a student enrols with a school that teaches and marks it: their own secondary, a district summer course, or an online school.

Is Pre-calculus 11 harder than Foundations of Math 11?

They are different rather than simply harder or easier. Pre-calculus 11 is heavier on algebra: radicals, rational expressions and quadratics. Foundations of Mathematics 11 includes logical reasoning, optimization, statistics, scale models and financial topics. The right choice depends on the courses a student will need in Grade 12.

Do I need Pre-calculus 11 for university?

Universities usually name Grade 12 courses. For BC applicants, UBC listed Pre-Calculus 12 for Science and several other programs on 22 October 2026, and Pre-calculus 12 builds on Pre-calculus 11. So Pre-calculus 11 is usually the route to those programs. Check each program's page.

What is the hardest part of Pre-calculus 11?

In our lessons, students most often struggle with rational expressions, because they need fluent factoring and careful non-permissible values, and with the ambiguous case of the sine law. Both improve quickly once factoring is secure and every triangle is sketched before solving.

What is an extraneous root?

It is a value that solves a transformed equation but not the original one. In Pre-calculus 11 it appears when squaring both sides of a radical equation or multiplying through a rational equation. Checking every candidate in the original equation finds it.

Does Pre-calculus 11 use radians?

The BC Pre-calculus 11 curriculum lists angles in standard position in degrees. Radians appear in Pre-calculus 12, whose curriculum lists angles in standard position in both radians and degrees.

How should my teen keep up through the year?

Fix Grade 10 gaps early, write domain restrictions before solving, check every root, solve each quadratic two ways, draw a sign chart for every inequality, sketch every triangle, keep a mistakes page, and review one old unit every week.

When are classes for BC families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. BC no longer changes its clocks, so a time agreed on Pacific time holds all year.

What does Pre-calculus 11 tutoring cost?

Your first class is free. After that the fee is monthly and covers eight live classes; there is no term contract, so you can stop when a month ends. The fees section shows today's group and private figures.
