# Computer science personal statement projects: something real to write about

Most searches for a computer science personal statement end at example statements, and copying from those is the one thing UCAS checks for. The useful question comes earlier: what has the student actually done with computing that they can describe, explain and defend? Cambridge's Department of Computer Science and Technology says a simple program run from the command line is a great example of independent work. This page sets out what UCAS, Imperial and Cambridge say about the three-question statement, gives six project briefs of ours sized for a sixth former, and works one through with real measurements. We mentor the project. The statement stays the student's own.

## What are the three UCAS personal statement questions?

UCAS replaced the single free-text statement with three questions that share one 4,000-character limit. Each answer needs at least 350 characters, and UCAS says admissions staff still read the three as a whole, so evidence should not be repeated across them.

**The three questions as UCAS words them, with the evidence UCAS suggests for each (read 31 October 2026)**

| # | UCAS question | Evidence UCAS mentions | Where a CS project fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Why do you want to study this course or subject? | Motivation, subject knowledge, research, super-curricular activities | The question the project set out to answer, and why it mattered to you |
| 2 | How have your qualifications and studies helped you to prepare for this course or subject? | Skills from formal study, EPQ, competitions such as UKMT, essays or projects on a technique | A project grown from A Level work, an EPQ, or an olympiad you sat |
| 3 | What else have you done to prepare outside of education, and why are these experiences useful? | Work experience, hobbies, online learning such as MOOCs, podcasts | A project built in your own time, and what it taught you |

### The numbers, and how flexible they are

UCAS says the 4,000-character limit, including spaces, is unchanged, and that it can be spread across the three answers in any way. Imperial gives the same rule for 2027 entry: at least 350 characters per section, 4,000 overall, with a built-in counter.

Our arithmetic: three minimum answers use only 1,050 characters, leaving 2,950 to place where the evidence is strongest. A strong project might take a third of the total on its own.

### No wrong box

UCAS says there is no wrong place for a piece of evidence and that applicants should not panic about whether something belongs in question 2 or 3. Cambridge adds that it has no preference for how a statement is split.

So a project built for an A Level class and then extended at home can sit in either answer. What matters is that it appears once, explained, rather than three times in passing.

## What do universities want to see in a computer science personal statement?

Academic engagement, explained. Cambridge says it reads every statement in full, gives it no formal score, and wants mostly academic detail; Imperial wants fewer skills with evidence rather than a long list. Both point away from activity lists and towards what the student thought.

**What two universities say about the statement, quoted from their own pages (read 31 October 2026)**

| University | What it says |
|---|---|
| Cambridge | "We will read your personal statement in full." and "We don't give personal statements a formal score." |
| Cambridge | Your statement "should focus mostly on academic detail throughout"; "Don't exaggerate." |
| Cambridge | Non-academic activities "should not take up more than 20% of the section that you mention them in" |
| Cambridge | "It is better to focus on a few activities in detail." and "Crucially, we are interested in your thoughts." |
| Cambridge | "The best personal statements show a pattern and logic in an applicant's academic engagement." |
| Imperial | One statement goes to all five choices; choose "quality over quantity" when describing skills, with evidence for each |
| Imperial | Work experience "isn't essential for most Imperial courses" |

### Cambridge Computer Science, specifically

The Department of Computer Science and Technology defines super-curricular work as "learning about the subject independently, beyond what you're taught in school". Its suggestions are deliberately modest: an app or website, a command-line program, something built with a Raspberry Pi, or an article on a theoretical topic you researched.

It also suggests learning a language the Cambridge course does not teach, naming Python as a popular choice, and lists puzzle sites such as Project Euler, Advent of Code and Codewars, plus the Bebras Computing Challenge and the Cipher Challenge.

### Mathematics is still the centre

The same department says in its FAQs that its primary focus is on Mathematics, that Further Mathematics is highly recommended, and that A Level Computer Science is valuable where a school offers it. Cambridge Computer Science also requires the TMUA, and Peterhouse and Trinity add the CSAT.

A project does not replace any of that. It gives the reader of a strong mathematical application a reason to believe the student will enjoy a degree that ends with a substantial project and a 10,000 to 12,000 word dissertation.

## What computer science projects are good for a personal statement?

Small, finished projects that start from a question and end with something measured or explained. The six briefs below are ours, sized for a sixth former working a few hours a week, and each matches one of the kinds of work Cambridge's department suggests. None needs anything beyond a laptop, apart from the Raspberry Pi in brief 4.

**Our six project briefs, with the question each one asks**

| # | Our brief | The question it answers | What to measure or explain |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Count primes two ways in Python: trial division and the sieve of Eratosthenes | Why is one method so much faster as N grows? | Steps taken for N = 104, 105, 106; the growth rate of each |
| 2 | A command-line Sudoku solver: plain backtracking, then with constraint propagation | How much search does a little reasoning save? | Number of guesses on the same twenty puzzles, before and after |
| 3 | A frequency-analysis breaker for Caesar and Vigen&egrave;re ciphers | How much ciphertext does frequency analysis need to work? | Success rate against message length |
| 4 | A Raspberry Pi logger that records room temperature every minute for a week | How noisy is a cheap sensor, and how do you smooth it? | Raw against moving-average readings; what the window size does |
| 5 | A small handwritten-digit classifier, then a study of its mistakes | Which digits does it confuse, and why those? | A confusion table; examples of 4 read as 9, and what they share |
| 6 | A researched article: why no program can decide whether every program halts | What does the proof actually show, and what does it not? | The argument in your own words, with one worked case |

### Start from a question

"I built a website" invites the reply "so what?". "I wanted to know why my prime counter slowed down so badly" invites a conversation. Every brief above is phrased as a question for that reason.

### Finish small

Cambridge's department says a project "doesn't have to be something complicated and polished". A finished command-line tool with one clear result beats an ambitious app abandoned at the login screen.

### Measure something

A number the student produced, such as steps taken, guesses saved or accuracy lost, turns a hobby into evidence. It is also the thing an interviewer is most likely to ask about.

The six briefs are ours, written on 31 October 2026. They follow the categories on the Cambridge Department of Computer Science and Technology super-curricular page (a program, a hardware build with a Raspberry Pi, a cryptography challenge, a researched article) and use public datasets and puzzles only. A student choosing one should still pick the question that genuinely interests them.

## What does a finished personal statement project look like?

Here is our brief 1 carried to the end, with numbers we measured ourselves on 31 October 2026. The point is not the primes; it is that the student ends with a result they can explain in a sentence and defend for ten minutes.

**Our prime-counting experiment: basic steps taken by each method (measured by us, 31 October 2026)**

| N | Primes up to N | Trial division steps | Sieve crossings-out | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10,000 | 1,229 | 117,527 | 16,981 | about 7 to 1 |
| 100,000 | 9,592 | 2,745,694 | 193,078 | about 14 to 1 |
| 1,000,000 | 78,498 | 67,740,404 | 2,122,048 | about 32 to 1 |

### What the numbers say

Each time N grows tenfold, trial division does roughly 23 to 25 times as much work, while the sieve does about 11 times as much. Trial division tests each number against divisors up to its square root, so its total grows faster than N; the sieve crosses out each composite a few times, growing only slightly faster than N.

That observation leads straight to the next question: what is the growth rate of each? A student who goes on to read about why the sieve's work is close to N log log N has moved from a coding exercise to the edge of number theory, which is the "pattern and logic" Cambridge says strong statements show.

### What the student writes down

Not a paragraph for UCAS. A log: the question, the first version, the surprise (trial division at a million took far longer than expected), the measurement, what they read next, and what they would try now, such as a segmented sieve to save memory.

Months later, the student writes their own sentences from that log. Because every claim in them came from something they did, there is nothing to exaggerate and nothing they cannot explain if asked.

1. **Write the question first.** One line, in the log, before any code.
2. **Build the simplest version that answers it.** For brief 1, that is two short functions and a counter.
3. **Measure, then check the measurement.** Both methods must agree on the prime count; ours give 78,498 below a million, the known value.
4. **Read one source about what you found.** Note its title and the one idea you took from it.
5. **Change one thing and measure again.** A second version, compared with the first, is a project; one version is an exercise.
6. **Stop and keep the log.** The code goes in a public repository with a short README; the log stays with the student.

The experiment, counts and ratios are ours, run in Python on 31 October 2026; the prime counts are standard values and the step counts depend on exactly how steps are counted. This is a model of a project, not text for a personal statement.

## Can someone else write or edit my personal statement?

No, and UCAS checks. Applicants now declare that the statement has not been copied or provided from another source, including AI software, and UCAS runs similarity checks on every statement. Imperial names the system: Copycatch. That is why we mentor projects and never touch the text.

**What UCAS and Imperial say about copied and AI-written statements (read 31 October 2026)**

| Source | What it says |
|---|---|
| UCAS | You "now have to declare that your personal statement hasn't been copied or provided from another source, including artificial intelligence software" |
| UCAS | The UCAS Verification Team checks "patterns of similarity in personal statements", and universities may be notified of similar elements |
| UCAS | Submitting AI-generated text as your own "could be considered cheating" and "Anything not in your words could get flagged as fraud" |
| UCAS | Acceptable uses of AI include ideas, structure and checking, not producing the text |
| Imperial | "UCAS runs all personal statements through its similarity detection system, Copycatch, to compare them with previous statements." |

## Four stages for a project worth writing about

Our suggested timing for a student applying in the autumn of Year 13. The project finishes before the writing starts, so the statement describes something complete.

**Our project timeline**

| When | Stage | Finished when |
|---|---|---|
| Year 12, spring term | 1. Choose a question | One line in a log, and a brief small enough to finish |
| Year 12, Easter to June | 2. Build the first version | It runs and answers the question roughly |
| Year 12, summer holiday | 3. Measure, read, improve | A second version compared with the first, and one source read |
| Year 13, September | 4. Stop and write, alone | The log is complete; the statement is drafted by the student and read by a teacher |

### If it is already September of Year 13

Pick the smallest brief, such as brief 1 or brief 3, and finish it in three or four weeks. A small finished project is honest; a large unfinished one is hard to write about.
Applicants with the 15 October deadline for Oxford or Cambridge have less time than others and should be strict about scope.

### If you are in Year 11 or early Year 12

Start with puzzles: the Cambridge department suggests Project Euler, Advent of Code and the Bebras challenge. They build fluency without the pressure of a project.
An EPQ in computing is another route, and UCAS lists the EPQ as evidence for question 2. The project must then follow the EPQ rules for the student's own work.

## What computer science applicants and parents ask

### What are the three UCAS personal statement questions?

Why do you want to study this course or subject; how have your qualifications and studies helped you to prepare; and what else have you done to prepare outside of education, and why are these experiences useful. UCAS says they share one 4,000-character limit.

### How long is the UCAS personal statement?

Up to 4,000 characters including spaces, across all three answers, with at least 350 characters for each. Imperial confirms the same structure for 2027 entry.

### What should I write about in a computer science personal statement?

Cambridge asks for mostly academic detail and says it is interested in your thoughts. A project you built, measured and can explain, or a topic you researched, gives you something specific to say in your own words.

### What can I do if I have no computer science experience?

Start small. Cambridge's Computer Science department suggests programming puzzles such as Project Euler and Advent of Code, and says a simple command-line program is a great example of independent work.

### Can I use ChatGPT to write my personal statement?

Not to produce the text. UCAS says applicants declare the statement was not copied or provided by another source, including AI software, and that anything not in your words could be flagged as fraud.

### Does UCAS check personal statements for copying?

Yes. UCAS says its Verification Team checks for patterns of similarity, and Imperial says every statement goes through the Copycatch similarity system. Copying from example statements is a real risk.

### Do extracurricular activities matter for computer science?

Subject-related ones matter most. Cambridge says non-academic activities should take up no more than 20% of the section they appear in, and that a few activities in detail beat a list.

### Will you write or check my personal statement?

No. We mentor the project itself and the computing and mathematics behind it. A school teacher is the right person to read a draft, and every word should be yours.

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.
