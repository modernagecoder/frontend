## An average describes nowhere

Eight Sligo places, one county figure, and the gap between them.

| Place | Average age | What stands out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| County Sligo as a whole | 40.7 | Up from 39.2 in 2016, against a national 38.8 |
| Sligo town, 20,608 people | 39.8 | Only 16.4% under 15, the smallest share of children of the towns checked |
| Coolaney, 1,155 people | 31.8 | 30.6% under 15, nearly double the town's share |
| Rosses Point, 883 people | 45.3 | 28.2% aged 65 or over |
| Enniscrone, 1,291 people | 45.3 | 26.8% aged 65 or over |
| The spread | 31.8 to 45.3 | Thirteen and a half years between two places in one county |

### What the average conceals

A single number for a county is a centre of gravity. It tells you nothing about how far apart the things being averaged are, and in Sligo they are very far apart indeed. Two places here differ by more than a decade of average age and both are inside the same small county.

### The counter-intuitive part

The biggest town has the smallest proportion of children. Where the people are and where the children are turn out to be two different maps, and only one of them is obvious from a population table.

### What a learner does with it

Rebuild the county from its parts, plot the eight places, calculate the range and the spread, and then write one sentence about what the county average is actually good for and what it is not.

### Why this is the most practical statistics lesson there is

Almost every decision made about a place is made from a summary figure. Average income, average age, average class size, average waiting time. In each case the summary is easy to produce and easy to quote, and in each case the thing that matters to an actual person is where in the spread they sit. A learner who has seen a thirteen year gap hiding behind one county average will, ever afterwards, ask to see the range. That single question is worth more in an argument than any amount of technique, and it is available to a twelve year old.

The extension is a real question rather than an exercise. If you were starting a class for twelve year olds in this county, and you only had the county average to go on, where would you put it? Then look at the town figures and answer again. The two answers are different, and understanding why is the point of the whole project.

### The towns

After Sligo town at 20,608 the county drops sharply: Tubbercurry 2,307, Strandhill 1,982, Collooney 1,797, Ballymote 1,711, Enniscrone 1,291, Coolaney 1,155 and Rosses Point 883.

### Density

Sligo town holds 1,649.6 people per square kilometre over 12.5 square kilometres, which is urban by Irish standards and unlike anywhere else in the county.

### Who lives here

Non-Irish citizens make up 9% of the county, led by UK citizens at 1,599 and Polish at 1,124, then Indian at 317 and German at 222.

Sligo has no Gaeltacht area. The statistics office defines the Gaeltacht as a set of electoral divisions in seven named counties and Sligo is not among them, so no part of this county is classified that way. That matters for a page like this only because it is the kind of detail people assume rather than check, and the check takes about a minute in the source itself.

## Ask how old County Sligo is and you will be told 40.7

It is the correct answer to the question asked, and it hides everything interesting.

A single figure is what a question about a county invites, and 40.7 years is exactly right. It is also true that two towns here sit four and a half years above it and one sits almost nine years below it. The answer is accurate and the picture it leaves in your head is wrong.

The fix is not a better tool. It is a better second question: what is the range, and which places sit at the ends of it. Anyone who has once plotted eight towns against a county average asks that automatically, because they have seen how much a single number can swallow.

That habit of asking for the shape rather than the summary is most of what separates somebody who uses data from somebody who is used by it. The longer argument is in [whether coding is still worth learning in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).

Small numbers, real consequence

Rosses Point has 883 people and Coolaney 1,155. In places that size there may be two or three children in a given school year with a serious interest in programming, and they may not be in the same year or the same school. That is not enough for a class in a room anywhere, at any price. Gathering those learners with others at the same level, from across the county and beyond it, is the only way the right group ever comes into existence.
