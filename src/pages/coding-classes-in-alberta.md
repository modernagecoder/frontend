## One credit each, and a prerequisite on almost every door

Read the course list as a graph rather than a catalogue and the shape of the problem appears.

Career and Technology Studies computing science is delivered as **1-credit courses**, and students usually aim to complete five of them in a semester. An Alberta High School Diploma requires a minimum of 100 credits, so a single computing science course is one per cent of a diploma, and it competes with fine arts, second languages and physical education for the same optional space.

The important part is not the price. It is the dependencies.

| Course | Prerequisite | Depth in the chain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CSE1110 Structured Programming 1 | None | 1 |
| CSE1120 Structured Programming 2 | CSE1110 | 2 |
| CSE2110 Procedural Programming 1 | CSE1120 | 3 |
| CSE2120 Data Structures 1 | CSE2110 | 4 |
| CSE3120 Object-oriented Programming 1 | CSE2110 | 4 |
| CSE3110 Iterative Algorithm 1 | CSE2120 | 5 |
| CSE3310 Recursive Algorithms 1 | CSE3110 and CSE3120 | 6 |

Six links, six semesters, nothing spare

If a prerequisite has to be finished before the next course starts, then the deepest material in the sequence sits six semesters from a standing start. Grade 10 to Grade 12 is six semesters. So a student beginning Structured Programming 1 in their first semester of Grade 10, and never taking a wrong turn, arrives at Recursive Algorithms 1 in their last. Anyone who starts a semester late, or who spends the first one on something with no prerequisite because it looked easier, does not get there at all.

Not every course sits on that chain, and that is worth knowing too. Client-side Scripting 1 and 2 and the CSE project courses have **no prerequisite**, so they are available immediately and are a sensible way to earn a credit or find out whether a student enjoys this. They are just not the door to the deep end. Structured Programming 1 is.

## The cheapest semester is the one you do not need

This is the only market where we would tell a Grade 8 parent to start now and mean it structurally rather than commercially.

Structured Programming 1 has no prerequisite, which means Alberta expects a student to arrive at it knowing nothing. That is fair and correct as policy. It is also a whole semester spent learning what a variable is, what a loop does, and why an error message is information rather than an insult.

A student who already knows those things does not skip the course. They spend it consolidating instead of surviving, and they arrive at Structured Programming 2 fluent rather than shaky. Over six links that difference compounds, because every course in a chain assumes the one before it landed properly.

### Grades 4 to 6

Ordering, counting, and predicting what a program will do before running it. No syntax, no deadline, and it is the groundwork the whole chain rests on.

### Grades 7 to 9

The target years. Blocks into typed code, and the specific goal of being able to write and debug a small program unaided before Grade 10 begins.

### Grade 10

Take Structured Programming 1 in the first semester if the deep end is the goal. Scripting and project courses are good, but they are not on the chain.

### Grades 11 and 12

Protect the diploma courses. If a learner is starting from nothing here, aim at scripting and a portfolio rather than at recursion.

Nothing here carries CTS credit, appears on an Alberta transcript or counts toward the 100. The claim is narrower and, we think, more useful: arrive at the first link already able to program. ## Six courses at five a semester is not two semesters

Every market page here works through one analytical trap. Alberta's is a computer science concept applied to the student's own timetable.

The learner is given the prerequisite table above and one question. If you can take five courses in a semester, how many semesters does it take to reach Recursive Algorithms 1?

Six courses. Five per semester. Almost everybody answers two.

The trap

Capacity does not beat dependency

You cannot take Structured Programming 2 in the same semester you take Structured Programming 1, because one is the prerequisite for the other. Nor the third, nor the fourth. The chain is a queue, and no amount of spare capacity shortens a queue. The answer is set by the *depth* of the longest path, which is six, not by dividing the count of courses by the seats available.

This is the critical path, and it is a real algorithm rather than a metaphor. The learner builds the graph as a dictionary of course to prerequisites, writes a function that returns the longest chain ending at any given course, and discovers that adding capacity changes nothing while removing one prerequisite changes everything. Then they run it on Object-oriented Programming 1, which sits at depth four rather than five, and see why Recursive Algorithms 1 needing both branches is what makes it expensive.

One more habit gets taught here, and it came out of researching this page. A widely repeated summary of Alberta's diploma rules gives a per-grade credit breakdown that adds up to 105 against a stated minimum of 100. The parts do not reconcile with the whole, which means somebody has misread something, possibly us. The correct response is not to pick the number you prefer. It is to publish neither and say why, which is what this page does.

The course codes and prerequisites are Alberta's, cited and linked above. The scheduling assumption, that a prerequisite must complete before the next course starts, is stated because it is an assumption rather than a rule we verified, and the learner is asked to test what happens to the answer if it is relaxed.

## What is left to learn if AI writes the code?

Look at what Alberta's chain actually builds toward. Data structures. Iteration. Object orientation. Recursion. Not one of those is a typing skill. They are ways of holding a problem in your head, and they are precisely what you need in order to look at generated code and say whether it is right.

Ask an AI how long it takes to reach Recursive Algorithms 1 at five courses a semester and there is a good chance you get two semesters, confidently, with the division shown. It is a reasonable-looking answer to a question that had a dependency structure hidden inside it. Nothing in the reply flags that a constraint was ignored, because the question did not mention one.

Noticing that a problem has a shape the arithmetic does not capture is the skill. It is learnable, it is what the project above trains, and it is worth more the faster machines produce fluent answers.

## Questions Alberta families ask before booking

Including the two against our own interest: no CTS credit, and no local office.

### Why does the order of Alberta CTS courses matter so much?

Because the courses are 1-credit units joined by prerequisites, so the sequence is a chain rather than a menu. Structured Programming 1 has no prerequisite. Structured Programming 2 needs it. Procedural Programming 1 needs that. Data Structures 1 needs Procedural Programming 1, Iterative Algorithm 1 needs Data Structures 1, and Recursive Algorithms 1 needs both Iterative Algorithm 1 and Object-oriented Programming 1. Take them out of order and a semester is spent without moving forward.

### Which Alberta computing science courses have no prerequisite?

On the listing we checked, Structured Programming 1, both Client-side Scripting 1 and 2, and the CSE project courses have no prerequisite. That matters for a student who wants a credit this semester rather than a place in the chain: scripting is a reasonable standing start, while Structured Programming 1 is the door to everything deeper.

### How many credits are these courses worth?

One credit each, and students usually aim to complete five of them in a semester. An Alberta High School Diploma requires a minimum of 100 credits, so each computing science course is a single per cent of a diploma and competes with fine arts, second languages and physical education for the same optional space. That is a real trade-off and worth knowing before it is made by accident.

### Is coding still worth learning now that AI writes it?

Alberta's own course list answers this. Look at what the chain builds toward: data structures, iteration, object orientation, recursion. Those are ways of thinking about a problem, not typing skills, and they are exactly what you need in order to judge whether generated code is correct. The syntax was never the scarce part.
