Coding summer camp for middle schoolers

Middle school is the awkward age for summer coding. Many famous computer science programs start at 16 or at tenth grade, while most kids camps are pitched at elementary students. A sixth, seventh or eighth grader needs something in between: real problem solving, a first typed language, and projects that feel grown up without being out of reach. This page shows where the age lines actually sit, how block coding turns into typed code, a small Python project a middle schooler can finish in one camp week, and the four-week plan our teachers use online.

The age gap

Why grades 6 to 8 fall between the big summer programs

Families who search for a middle school coding camp often find well-known names first, then discover their child is too young. Here is what three of those programs say on their own pages, quoted as written.

Age and grade lines for three well-known summer programs, as each page states it on 17 October 2026, with our note on the right
ProgramWhat its page saysOur note for middle schoolers
COSMOS (University of California)Open to "students completing grades 8-12"; "COSMOS is Limited to California High-School Students Only"An eighth grader in California can look ahead to it; elsewhere it does not apply
Carnegie Mellon CS Scholars"Be at least 16 years old by the program start date."A target for later high school, not for this summer
MIT Beaver Works Summer InstituteThe FAQ says "nor do we accept students below 10th grade"Same: a goal two or three summers away

What the gap means in practice

The research-style and residential programs are built for older teens who already code. A twelve year old is not behind; they are simply early, and the best use of their summer is to become the student those programs look for later.

That means learning to finish things: a small program that works, then a slightly bigger one, with the habits of testing and explaining built in from the start.

What to look for instead

A camp for this age should still feel serious. Students at eleven to fourteen notice quickly when material is pitched too young, and they lose interest just as fast when it is all lecture.

Look for typed code by the second week, a project finished each week, and a teacher who reads the student’s own code, not only a shared example.

Sources: COSMOS home page, Carnegie Mellon CS Scholars, BWSI FAQ, each checked on 17 October 2026. None of these programs is connected with us.

Blocks to text

How block coding turns into typed code

Most middle schoolers arrive with some block coding from school or from Scratch. The question is not whether blocks are “real” coding but how to make the jump to typing without losing the fun.

Three free block tools, described from their own pages (read 17 October 2026)
ToolWhat its makers sayWhere it leads
Scratch"200 million kids create on Scratch for free"Events, loops and variables, which carry straight into Python
Microsoft MakeCode"The blocks map directly to actual lines of code in a programming language."Switch the same project between blocks and code
MakeCode Arcade"MakeCode Arcade is a code editor to build Retro Arcade games for the browser"; its docs show games built in Blocks and games built in JavaScriptArcade games, then JavaScript
Code.org CS DiscoveriesA free curriculum for grades 6 to 10 using Game Lab, App Lab, AI Lab and Web LabWebsites, apps, games and physical computing

The MakeCode idea, in plain words

MakeCode says its aim is to introduce programming in a way that is "approachable and inviting", with blocks that correspond to lines of real code. A student can build a game in blocks, then open the code view and see the same program typed out.

That side-by-side view is the bridge. Students stop thinking of typed code as a different subject and start seeing it as the same instructions written faster.

When a student is ready to type

One signal we look for is frustration with dragging: a student who wants to change ten similar blocks at once is ready for a loop they can type.

Another sign is a project idea that blocks make clumsy, such as a quiz with many questions, a calculator, or a game that keeps score across rounds.

A summer is shorter than a semester, so a single unit is the right size: one unit, done well, with a finished project at the end.

A real project

One camp week, one finished program: a Python guessing game

This is the project we use in the second week for many middle schoolers. It is short enough to type in one lesson and rich enough to teach loops, conditions and a real computer science idea. Both programs are ours and run in standard Python 3.

The arithmetic, ours

Halving 100 numbers: 100, then 50, 25, 13, 7, 4, 2 and 1. That is at most 7 guesses. The reason is that 2 to the 7th power is 128, which is more than 100, while 2 to the 6th power is only 64.

For numbers from 1 to 1,000 the answer is 10 guesses, because 2 to the 10th power is 1,024. Ten times as many numbers needs only three more guesses, which surprises almost every student.

What the week teaches

Program 1 teaches the building blocks. Program 2 teaches a way of thinking: never guess at random when you can rule out half the possibilities at once.

That same halving idea appears later in searching sorted lists, in debugging (cut the program in half to find the broken line) and in competition problems in high school.

  1. Extension 1. Tell the player when they are within five of the secret number.
  2. Extension 2. Limit the player to 7 tries, and explain why 7 is fair.
  3. Extension 3. Keep the lowest score across several rounds.
  4. Extension 4. Make Program 2 catch a player who changes their number halfway.

Readiness

Is my middle schooler ready for typed code? A quick check

This is the checklist our teachers run in a first lesson. In our view, three or more yes answers point to starting in Python; fewer point to a stronger block project first.

  1. Typing. Can they type a full sentence without hunting for every key?
  2. Patience with errors. Will they read a message on the screen before asking for help?
  3. Blocks behind them. Have they finished at least one Scratch or MakeCode project of their own?
  4. Numbers. Are they comfortable with negative numbers and simple multiplication?
  5. An idea. Do they have something they want to make, even a silly one?

If the answer is mostly no

That is fine and common in grade 6. A summer of stronger block projects, with loops and variables used on purpose, sets up the switch for the fall.

The Scratch course on this page is built for exactly that.

If the answer is mostly yes

Start in Python straight away. The first week will feel fast, and that pace keeps this age group interested.

For a student who loves Roblox, Luau is another typed first language, and the game gives them an audience.

After the summer

A fall goal for middle schoolers: the Congressional App Challenge

The Congressional App Challenge is a national coding competition, run district by district, that is open to middle school students. Its 2026 rulebook gives a summer coder a clear next target.

A summer guessing game is not an app entry, but the habits are the same: finish, test with real people, explain your code. Four steps from block coder to independent programmer

The steps we see middle schoolers climb, usually one or two per summer.

Middle school coding steps (ours)
StepWhat it looks like
1. Block makerBuilds Scratch or MakeCode projects with help
2. Code readerCan follow a short typed program and predict what it prints
3. Code writerWrites a small program alone and fixes its own errors

For parents

Ask your child to read their code out loud to you. If they can explain each line, the learning is real.

Praise finished small programs more than ambitious unfinished ones.

For students

Error messages are clues, not insults. Read the last line first.

Keep every program you write in one folder. In a year it becomes your portfolio.

Questions from families

Coding summer camp for middle schoolers: common questions

What should a middle schooler learn at a coding summer camp?

How to finish small programs, and ideally the move from block coding to a first typed language such as Python. One finished project each week is a good sign the camp is working.

Is my seventh grader too young for well-known programs like CS Scholars or BWSI?

Usually yes for now. Carnegie Mellon's CS Scholars page asks for age 16 by the start date, and the BWSI FAQ says it does not accept students below 10th grade. They are good targets for later.

Can an eighth grader apply to COSMOS?

The COSMOS home page says it is for students completing grades 8 to 12 and is limited to California high school students only. Check the current page for the year you apply.

Is block coding enough at this age?

Blocks teach real ideas, and Microsoft says MakeCode blocks map directly to lines of code. In our view many middle schoolers are ready to start typing during a summer, and the first lesson is where we check.

What is Code.org CS Discoveries?

A free Code.org curriculum listed for grades 6 to 10. Its units can be taught alone, in collections of about a semester, or as a full year, using Game Lab, App Lab, AI Lab and Web Lab.

Which first typed language is right for grades 6 to 8?

Python for most students, because it reads almost like English. Luau inside Roblox Studio suits students who love Roblox. JavaScript suits eighth graders interested in websites.

How many hours a week does your summer plan take?

About four: two live lessons of an hour each and around two hours of practice. Over four weeks that is 16 hours, which suits this age better than long daily sessions.

When are your classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

Can middle schoolers enter coding competitions?

Yes. The 2026 Congressional App Challenge rulebook includes students enrolled in middle school, allows block-based languages, and allows teams of up to four students.

How is this different from your other summer camp pages?

Our kids and teens summer camp pages cover the full age ranges. This page is only about grades 6 to 8, the years between elementary block coding and high school programs.
