AI and Python classes for teens in Surrey, British Columbia

Where can a teenager in Surrey, BC learn Python and AI live, with a real teacher?

Modern Age Coders teaches Python and AI live online to teens aged 13 to 18 in Surrey, British Columbia, in a class of fifteen to twenty or one to one. Students usually start with Python for Teens, then choose AI and Machine Learning for Teens, Data Science for Teens or CCC preparation. Classes are booked on Pacific time, which in BC no longer changes, and the first class is free.

This page is about Surrey in British Columbia, the city south of the Fraser River, not the English county. A Surrey teenager has more computing on offer at school than the course names suggest: British Columbia files programming under two different subject areas, so a student can meet Computer Programming in one corner of the timetable and Computer Science in the mathematics department. Outside school, CEMC’s contests start with a 45-minute challenge that needs no coding at all. Below we set out what the BC curriculum says, quoting it, what permanent Pacific time means for a live class with teachers in India, how contests fit a Grade 9 or 10 student, which AI tools a teen may legally use, and an unsupervised learning project built on the City of Surrey’s own playground map.

Two homes for programming in BC schools: Applied Design and Mathematics

British Columbia lists programming courses in Applied Design, Skills, and Technologies (ADST) and, separately, Computer Science 11 and 12 in Mathematics. The quoted text is from curriculum.gov.bc.ca as we read it on 22 October 2026; the notes are ours.

Two lines in those documents describe our teaching better than we could. Computer Programming 11 defines computational thinking this way: “Key components are decomposition, patterns and generalizations, abstraction, and algorithmic thinking.” Computer Science 11 calls algorithms “sets of rules or instructions that precisely define a sequence of operations” and asks students, among its sample questions, “What makes one algorithm better than another algorithm?” That last question is the heart of the playground project further down: two runs of the same algorithm on the same data give different answers, and a student has to decide which one is better and why. Which BC courses a particular school offers, and in which grade, varies, so families should check the school’s own course list.

Grade 9 and 10

Students who enjoy building apps and tools often like the ADST courses; those who enjoy puzzles and proofs often like Computer Science 11 and 12. Many take both, and our lessons support either.

City of Surrey data project

Can a computer find Surrey’s communities on its own, from playground locations alone?

The City of Surrey maps every park playground it runs and labels each with a community such as Newton or Cloverdale. Our students hide those labels, let the k-means algorithm group the playgrounds by location, then compare its groups with the City’s. The clusters and counts here are our output; the community labels are the City’s.

Our k-means results on City of Surrey Park Playgrounds (167 playgrounds fetched 22 October 2026; k = 6 clusters; 20 random starts of 50 rounds each)
RunTotal squared distance to cluster centresPlaygrounds in a cluster led by their own communityCommunities leading the six clusters
Tightest of the 20 runs891 million square metres137 of 167Cloverdale, Guildford, Newton, South Surrey (twice), Whalley
Tightest run with six different leaders944 million square metres144 of 167Cloverdale, Fleetwood, Guildford, Newton, South Surrey, Whalley

This is the full program in standard-library Python. It downloads the playground outlines in the City’s own map grid, where positions are in metres, reduces each outline to a rough centre by averaging its corner points, and runs k-means twenty times from different random starting centres. When we ran it on 22 October 2026 it printed exactly the two rows in the table:import json, random, urllib.request from collections import Counter URL = ("https://services5.arcgis.com/YRpe0VKTJytZSSIB/arcgis/rest/services/" "Park%20Playgrounds/FeatureServer/0/query?where=1%3D1" "&outFields=COMMUNITY&f=json") spots = [] # centre of each playground outline, metres east and north (UTM zone 10N) for f in json.load(urllib.request.urlopen(URL))["features"]: ring = f["geometry"]["rings"][0] spots.append((sum(p[0] for p in ring) / len(ring), sum(p[1] for p in ring) / len(ring), f["attributes"]["COMMUNITY"])) def nearest(x, y, centres): gaps = [(x - cx) ** 2 + (y - cy) ** 2 for cx, cy in centres] return gaps.index(min(gaps)), min(gaps) def kmeans(k, seed): random.seed(seed) centres = [s[:2] for s in random.sample(spots, k)] for _ in range(50): groups = [[] for _ in range(k)] for s in spots: groups[nearest(s[0], s[1], centres)[0]].append(s) centres = [(sum(s[0] for s in g) / len(g), sum(s[1] for s in g) / len(g)) if g else centres[i] for i, g in enumerate(groups)] spread = sum(nearest(s[0], s[1], centres)[1] for s in spots) return spread, groups runs = sorted(kmeans(6, seed) for seed in range(1, 21)) def leaders(groups): return [Counter(s[2] for s in g).most_common(1)[0] for g in groups] def report(label, spread, groups): top = leaders(groups) print(label, "spread", round(spread / 1e6), "million m2;", sum(n for _, n in top), "of", len(spots), "match their cluster") print(" clusters led by", sorted(str(name) for name, _ in top)) report("tightest of 20 runs:", *runs[0]) six = next(r for r in runs if len({name for name, _ in leaders(r[1])}) == 6) report("tightest run with six different leaders:", *six)

1. No answers given

Unlike most school exercises, k-means is never told the right groups. It places six centres, gives each playground to the nearest one, moves each centre to the middle of its group, and repeats. The community names are used only afterwards, to judge the result.

2. Different starts, different answers

Twenty starts did not all agree. The run with the smallest total distance split South Surrey into two clusters and left Fleetwood without one of its own; a slightly looser run matched all six communities and placed 144 playgrounds with their own label.

3. Which one is better?

That is BC Computer Science 11’s own question, and here it has no single answer. The tightest run is better by the algorithm’s own measure; the six-community run is better at matching how the City describes itself. Students write down which they prefer, and why.

What the labels hide

Of the 167 playgrounds, 13 carry no community label and one is tagged City Centre. Newton has the most labelled playgrounds (38), then Cloverdale (32), Whalley (28), South Surrey (24), Guildford (18) and Fleetwood (13). Because South Surrey covers a large area with fewer playgrounds, a method that only cares about distance is tempted to cut it in two. Clusters reflect the data you give them, not the map in a resident’s head.

A note on the numbers: averaging an outline’s corners gives a rough centre, not a true centroid, which is close enough for clustering a city’s worth of small parks. The playground layer is published by the City of Surrey with an update frequency the City gives as monthly, so a later download may differ slightly. The City’s open data licence grants “a worldwide, royalty-free, perpetual, non-exclusive license to use the Information, including for commercial purposes”, and asks for the statement “Contains information licensed under the Open Government License”, with the City of Surrey named after it. Source: City of Surrey, Park Playgrounds layer. The City has not endorsed this page or our classes, and our clusters are not City boundaries.

Each rule comes from the organisation that owns the tool or runs the contest, read in its own document on 22 October 2026. The right-hand column is how we apply it.

The editor rule is the one Surrey families tend to miss. Many school laptops and free editors now finish lines of code automatically, and a student who has always worked that way can be startled to find the help gone on contest day. We start contest students with plain editors early. In project work the issue is different: the playground program is short enough that a chatbot could write it, but the interesting work is deciding whether a split South Surrey is a mistake or a finding. A teen who learns to make that call, and to explain it, is learning the part of AI work that tools cannot do for them.

Consent before ChatGPT

OpenAI asks for parental consent from 13 to 18. We let the family decide, list the lessons where the tool might appear, and make sure a teen without it misses nothing.

Some tools wait until 18

Anthropic’s terms set 18 as the minimum age, so Claude stays out of teen classes; our adult agentic coding course is closed to under-18s for the same reason.

Assignments remain theirs

We explain, question and give practice. We do not complete marked school work, and we would rather see a shorter program a student can defend than a longer one they cannot.

Pacific time, now permanent

Surrey keeps one clock all year now: what that means for live classes

The BC government announced that “B.C. will adopt permanent, year-round daylight saving time after clocks shift forward an hour on Sunday, March 8, 2026”, and that “Pacific time will be set seven hours behind co-ordinated universal time (UTC-7)”. So Surrey does not fall back on 1 November 2026, while Ontario and Quebec do.

Steady with India

Because India and BC both stay put, the gap is twelve and a half hours all year. A Surrey class time never shifts against its teacher, which makes long-term timetables simple.

The difference from Ontario is what moves: two hours in winter, three in summer. Anything a Surrey teen joins on Eastern time, such as a national webinar, needs checking after 1 November.

Judgement over tools

Why a Surrey teen should learn to judge a model before trusting an AI tool

Because k-means gave two different answers on the same playgrounds, and only a person can say which one matters.

Unsupervised learning is a good place to see the limits of automation. The algorithm optimises one thing, distance, and is perfectly happy to split a community in two. A student who has watched that happen understands that every model optimises something specific, and that the something is chosen by a person.

Our teaching follows from that. Students state the question first, write and run the steps, predict what should happen, and argue with what does. AI tools, where a family allows them, arrive later as a second opinion to be tested. Teens who work this way are the ones who notice when a confident answer is wrong.

The CCC enforces the same idea from the other side: no assistant in the editor, so the reasoning has to be the student’s. The longer argument is in why learning to code still pays off in 2026.

Questions from Surrey, BC families

What parents and teens in Surrey, British Columbia ask us most.

Is this page about Surrey in British Columbia?

Yes. It is for families in Surrey, BC, on Pacific time and in the BC school system, not Surrey in England. Classes are live online from home.

Do you have a classroom in Surrey?

No. Every lesson is live online. Teens join a class of fifteen to twenty or have a private teacher.

Does your course count as BC Computer Programming 11?

No. We teach alongside school courses and do not grant provincial credits. The credit for Computer Programming 11, 12 or Computer Science 11 comes from the school.

What should a Grade 9 student in Surrey start with?

Python for Teens in most cases, plus the Beaver Computing Challenge in November if they want a first contest. The free class confirms the level.

Does my teenager need my consent to use ChatGPT?

Yes. OpenAI's help centre says children aged 13 to 18 need parental consent, and that ChatGPT is not meant for children under 13. Our lessons work without it.

Is Claude used in your teen classes?

No. Anthropic's terms say users must be at least 18, so no student under 18 uses Claude with us.

Is editor autocomplete allowed in the CCC?

No. The CCC grader rules say AI-based tools within an IDE cannot be used, so we practise for contests with those features off.

Did BC stop changing the clocks?

Yes. The BC government says the province adopted permanent daylight saving time after 8 March 2026, set at UTC-7, so Surrey does not fall back on 1 November 2026.

When do classes run for Surrey students?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Every time is confirmed in Pacific time.

Where does the playground project data come from?

From the City of Surrey's published Park Playgrounds layer, used under the City's open data licence. Students download it and run the clustering themselves; the City has not endorsed our courses.
