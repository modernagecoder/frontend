Coding and AI classes in Neuss

AI is not one subject. For an eight year old it is learning that a computer can be taught from examples; for a fifteen year old it is building and testing a small model; for an adult it can mean putting AI to work in a job without trusting it blindly. This page sets out what we teach at each of those ages in Neuss, what the KMK and North Rhine-Westphalia have said about AI and Informatik, which popular AI tools allow which ages, and a first machine-learning model that learns to tell Neuss street trees apart by their size.

Why code comes before AI

A learner who has never written a loop can still type a question into a chatbot, but cannot tell whether the answer is right. We teach the code first so that, later, AI is something they can open up and check.

This is also how we keep our promise to teach learners to think, not to copy: in our classes nobody is asked to paste an answer they cannot explain.

What adults usually want

Adults in Neuss tend to come with a concrete aim: to automate a weekly report, to understand the models their company now uses, or to move into a data role. Their path starts with Python and moves to machine learning quickly, at their own speed, often one to one.

We give no career, salary or immigration advice; we teach the skills and show the work.

School and policy

What do the KMK and North Rhine-Westphalia say about AI and Informatik?

Two short answers: the KMK has added AI to its framework for digital education, and NRW teaches compulsory Informatik in grades 5 and 6.

The national picture

The Kultusministerkonferenz set out its strategy for education in a digital world in 2016. In 2026 it adopted an updated competence framework that adds AI-related competences to those areas. Since 2024 it has also had a recommendation for education administrations on handling AI in school learning.

In plain terms: schools are expected to teach about AI, but the framework describes competences, not a fixed timetable of AI lessons.

Neuss is in NRW

The Gesellschaft für Informatik reports that NRW teaches Informatik as a compulsory subject in grades 5 and 6 at all general school types. In the Qualifikationsphase it can be a three-hour Grundkurs or a five-hour Leistungskurs.

The written Informatik Abitur in NRW is set centrally and solved without a computer, so planning and tracing code by hand matter as much as typing it.

A real Neuss project

Can a computer learn to tell a Neuss plane tree from a birch by its size?

The City of Neuss publishes its register of street and park trees as open data with no restrictions. That is enough to build a first machine-learning model, and to make the most common beginner mistake on purpose.

The data

The register, Stadt Neuss: Baumbestand, is listed on GovData with a CC0 licence. Our download on 3 November 2026 has 72,648 trees, each with its kind of tree, height in metres, crown width in metres and trunk girth in centimetres. Ahorn, maple, is the most common, with 19,910 trees.

We keep three kinds that look quite different to a person: Platane (plane tree), Birke (birch) and Rosskastanie (horse chestnut), and only trees with all three sizes filled in and above zero. That leaves 2,960 trees in neuss_trees.csv.

The idea

The model is a nearest-neighbour model, one of the oldest ideas in machine learning. To guess a new tree, find the five known trees whose sizes are closest to it and take the kind most of them share.

To test it fairly, one tree in five, chosen by its ID number, is hidden from the model and used only for the test. The model must beat the lazy strategy of always guessing the most common kind.

Sixty-seven per cent against a lazy 39 is a real result, but there is a flaw in how "closest" is measured. The distance adds up differences in height, crown and girth as plain numbers. Height is in metres and differs between trees by a few units; girth is in centimetres and differs by a hundred or more. So a 10 cm difference in girth counts as much as a 10 m difference in height, and the model is really listening to the trunk alone.

The fix is to put the three measurements on the same scale before measuring distance. Version 2 works out how much each measurement usually varies in the training trees (its standard deviation) and divides by it, so that "one unit" means "a typical difference" for all three. It is the same three trees and the same five neighbours; only the ruler changes.

Girth varies about fifteen times more than height in these numbers, which is why it drowned out the other two. After scaling, the model gets 70 per cent right. That is a modest gain, and the honest part of the lesson is looking at where it came from, kind by kind, rather than trusting one number.

Data: Stadt Neuss, Baumbestand, licence CC0, downloaded 3 November 2026. The selection, split, model and counts are our own. All three kinds improve with scaling, but the horse chestnut stays hard: size alone gets just over half of them right, a sign that the model needs other information, such as leaf shape, that the register does not hold.

  1. Children, 8 to 12. Measure five family members' height in metres and shoe length in centimetres. Who is "closest" to whom if you just subtract the numbers? Does that seem fair?
  2. Teenagers. Run Version 1, then remove girth from the size tuple altogether and predict whether the score rises or falls before you run it.
  3. Teenagers, further. Change k from 5 to 1 and to 25. Which kind of tree gains, and which loses?
  4. Adults. Replace the standard deviation with the range (largest minus smallest). Girth in this file runs from 3 cm to 565 cm; explain why a single extreme trunk can stretch the range and make it a poorer ruler.

An AI contest

Is there a national AI competition a Neuss pupil can enter?

Yes: the Bundeswettbewerb Künstliche Intelligenz. We are not connected to it and describe it from its own pages.

What it asks for

The BWKI is run by the Tübingen AI Center for pupils in Sekundarstufe I and II. A project must use machine-learning methods, and an entry needs written documentation, code that runs and a short video pitch. Projects may be handed in in German or in English.

That last point matters for a learner who studies with us: a project built and explained in English can be entered as it is.

The 2026 calendar

For 2026, registration opened on 1 March 2026 and projects had to be handed in by 20 September 2026. The final takes place in Tübingen on 13 November 2026.

The next round follows the same pattern, so a Neuss teenager who starts a model this winter has months to improve it before the 2027 deadlines are announced.

We never write a competition project for a learner. We teach the methods, ask the questions a jury would ask, and leave the project in the learner's own hands. The tree model above is the kind of starting point that can grow into one, with better data and a fair test.

Neuss learners ask

Coding and AI classes in Neuss: questions and answers

Are the classes in German?

No. Classes are live and in English only. The mentor adjusts speed and wording to the learner, gets used to their accent quickly and explains again in simpler words when needed.

Is AI too early for a child of 8?

Not if it is taught the right way. Children of 8 to 12 learn how a computer can learn from examples through games and sorting tasks, without personal chatbot accounts, alongside ordinary coding in Scratch or Python.

Will my teenager use ChatGPT in class?

Only within the tool's own terms, which set a minimum of 13 and require a parent's permission under 18. Most teen AI work in our classes is code the learner writes and tests, such as the tree model on this page.

Do Neuss schools teach AI?

The KMK added AI-related competences to its digital education framework in 2026, and NRW teaches compulsory Informatik in grades 5 and 6. There is no fixed AI timetable; our classes teach alongside school, and the school stays in charge of marks.

When are classes held in Neuss time?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Pupils tend to choose afternoons, adults early evenings or Saturdays.

Where are the mentors?

In India, teaching live over video. India is four and a half hours ahead of Neuss in winter and three and a half hours ahead in summer.

I am an adult with no coding background. Can I start with AI?

Yes, through Python. Adults usually spend the first weeks on Python basics and data, then move to machine learning. Many choose one to one so the pace is entirely their own.

How large are the groups?

A batch has fifteen to twenty learners at a similar level. If you prefer, one-to-one lessons give you two private classes a week at agreed times.

How do the fees work?

You pay monthly. Fees are shown in euro at the European Central Bank reference rate and taken in US dollars, which your bank converts, so your statement may show a slightly different amount. The first class is free.

Can a project from class be entered in the BWKI?

A learner's own project can be, if it uses machine learning and meets the rules. The BWKI accepts projects in German or English. We teach the methods but never build the entry for the learner.
