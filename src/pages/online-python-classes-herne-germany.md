## What happens in a live online Python class?

People in Herne often ask us whether "online" means a course of videos. It does not. Here is one ordinary hour, step by step.

1. **Minutes 0 to 5: the warm-up.** The mentor opens the video call, greets each learner by name and puts a two-line puzzle on the shared screen. Nobody has to speak first; anyone can type a guess in the chat.
2. **Minutes 5 to 20: one new idea.** The mentor writes a short program live, explains one new thing (a loop, a dictionary, reading a file) and runs it. Then the program is broken on purpose, and the group says what will go wrong before it does.
3. **Minutes 20 to 45: the learner types.** Each learner writes their own version in their own editor and shares the screen when stuck. The mentor reads the code line by line with them, asks what they expected and lets them find the mistake.
4. **Minutes 45 to 55: show and explain.** Two or three learners show a working program and say in their own words why it works. Explaining is where most of the learning settles.
5. **Minutes 55 to 60: what comes next.** A small task for the days before the next class, and for younger learners a short note to parents on what was built.

### English, at your speed

Lessons are taught in English only. The mentor slows down, rephrases and checks understanding, and quickly learns how each learner speaks. A Herne pupil whose English is still growing practises it for real every class, around code that looks the same in any language.

### Thinking before typing

We teach learners to plan a program in plain words first and to predict its output before running it. Copying a finished answer, from a classmate or from an AI tool, is never the goal; being able to explain every line is.

### What you need at home

A laptop or desktop computer, a stable connection and a free Python editor that the mentor helps you install in the first class. A tablet is fine for watching but awkward for typing, so we suggest a keyboard from the start.

## How many Herne pit shafts were working at the same time?

Herne grew on coal. The city publishes its list of historic colliery shafts as open data, free to reuse, and the years each one worked. That is enough for a proper Python question with a surprising answer and a lesson about order.

### The data

The dataset is called Historische Zechenstandorte and comes from the City of Herne's geodata service, listed on GovData under the licence Datenlizenz Deutschland Zero 2.0. We downloaded it on 3 November 2026: 80 shaft sites, each with a name such as Shamrock 4 or Mont Cenis 2 "Carl", a year it started working and a year it stopped.

One site, Mont Cenis 5, has no years at all. The other 79 have both. Sixteen names end in Wetterschacht: shafts that carried fresh air into the mine rather than coal out of it.

### The question

In which year were the most shafts at work at the same time, and how many? A natural guess is the 1950s, the decade people often picture as the height of coal. The program disagrees.

We turn the GeoJSON into a small file, herne_shafts.csv, with three columns: name, opened, closed. Version 1 does the obvious thing: for every year from 1850 to 2000 it checks every shaft.

**Version 1: check every shaft in every year** (Python)
```python
import csv

shafts = []
with open("herne_shafts.csv", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        if row["opened"] and row["closed"]:
            shafts.append((row["name"], int(row["opened"]), int(row["closed"])))

print("Shafts with both years:", len(shafts))

working = {}
for year in range(1850, 2001):
    working[year] = sum(1 for _, a, b in shafts if a <= year <= b)

peak = max(working.values())
peak_years = [y for y in working if working[y] == peak]
print("Most shafts at work at once:", peak)
print("In the years:", peak_years[0], "to", peak_years[-1])
```
It prints Shafts with both years: 79, Most shafts at work at once: 75 and In the years: 1930 to 1931.

The peak is 1930 and 1931, with 75 of the 79 shafts working, not the 1950s. By 1950 the count had already fallen to 68. Version 1 is correct but wasteful: it makes 151 passes over the list, one per year, and most years nothing changes. A professional would ask a different question: when does the count change at all?

The count changes only when a shaft opens or closes. So Version 2 turns each shaft into two events: plus one in the year it opened, and minus one in the year after it closed, because a shaft that closed in 1967 was still working during 1967. Sort the events, walk through them once with a running total, and keep the highest value seen.

**Version 2: sort the events and sweep once** (Python)
```python
import csv

events = []
with open("herne_shafts.csv", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        if row["opened"] and row["closed"]:
            events.append((int(row["opened"]), +1))
            events.append((int(row["closed"]) + 1, -1))

events.sort()
running = 0
peak = 0
for year, change in events:
    running += change
    if running > peak:
        peak, peak_year = running, year

print("Events to sort:", len(events))
print("Peak:", peak, "first reached in", peak_year)
print("Still working after the last event:", running)
```
It prints Events to sort: 158, Peak: 75 first reached in 1930 and Still working after the last event: 0.

The answers agree, which is the first thing to check whenever you replace a slow program with a clever one. The hidden detail is the sort. Python sorts the pairs by year first and then by the second number, so in any year a closing event, minus one, comes before an opening event, plus one. Had the openings come first, the running total would briefly count a shaft that had already stopped, and in a different dataset the peak could be one too high. Deciding how ties sort is part of the program, not a detail.
