# Online maths circles in the UK

A maths circle is a small group that meets regularly to work on problems with no syllabus and no test, often staying with one question for several weeks. In the UK, circles and circle-like clubs are run by universities and specialist maths schools, usually free and often with limited places. ## What is a maths circle?

A circle is a community and a pace, not a course. The same pupils meet week after week, a problem may stay on the table for a month, and nobody is graded.

### Three things that make a circle

Continuity. The Axiom page puts it plainly: "Axiom is a long-term programme and students who join in year 7 will be guaranteed a place on the programme for their entire school journey." A circle builds over years.

Open problems. Questions are chosen because they lead somewhere, not because they appear on a paper. Peers. Pupils talk to each other more than to the leader, and the leader asks rather than tells.

### How it differs from a class or a club

A tuition class moves through a syllabus and checks progress. A school maths club often runs puzzles that end in a session. A masterclass series is a short, intense block. A circle is slow on purpose: ideas get time to go wrong and come back.

King's says the pupils who most enjoy its circles "are high-attainers who love problem-solving and imaginative, creative thinking", which is a description of temperament as much as attainment.

## What does one problem look like across three circle sessions?

Here is a circle arc we wrote and run, for ages 11 to 15. It is ours, not taken from any programme above. The problem fits on one line; the thinking fills three weeks.

**The question.** A post office sells only 3p and 5p stamps. Which postage amounts can you not make exactly?

### Week 1: explore

Pupils list amounts from 1p upwards. 3, 5, 6, 8, 9 and 10 work; 1, 2, 4 and 7 do not. From 8 onwards every amount works, because 8, 9 and 10 do, and adding a 3p stamp reaches the next three. The largest impossible amount is 7.

### Week 2: conjecture

Change the stamps to 4p and 7p and the largest impossible amount is 17. With 2p and 5p it is 3. Someone notices that 3 times 5 minus 3 minus 5 is 7, 4 times 7 minus 4 minus 7 is 17, and 2 times 5 minus 2 minus 5 is 3. The circle has a conjecture.

### Week 3: test and explain

Does it always work? Try 4p and 6p: every odd amount is impossible, so there is no largest one. The rule needs stamps with no common factor. The session ends on why the formula holds for such pairs, written in pupils' own words.

**Our check: the largest impossible postage for two stamp values** (Python)
```python
def largest_impossible(a, b, limit=200):
    can = [False] * (limit + 1)
    can[0] = True
    for n in range(1, limit + 1):
        can[n] = (n >= a and can[n - a]) or (n >= b and can[n - b])
    missing = [n for n in range(limit + 1) if not can[n]]
    return missing[-1] if missing else None

for a, b in [(3, 5), (4, 7), (2, 5), (5, 8)]:
    print(a, b, largest_impossible(a, b), a * b - a - b)
```

Run it and each line prints two equal numbers: 7, 17, 3 and 27. The code does not prove the rule, and a good circle says so out loud; it only makes the conjecture worth proving. That distinction, between evidence and proof, is the most valuable thing a circle teaches, and it takes weeks rather than minutes to sink in.

The problem framing, the three-week plan, the code and the outputs are ours. The underlying result is a classical one in number theory, often called the Frobenius coin problem for two values.

## Circle problems to start at home

Five problems we wrote, each one able to fill a session. Answers and the key idea follow each; resist reading them for at least a quarter of an hour.

### A. Locker doors (ages 10 and up)

A corridor has 100 closed lockers. Pupil 1 opens all of them; pupil 2 toggles every second; pupil 3 every third; and so on to pupil 100. Which stay open? Exactly the square numbers, 1, 4, 9 up to 100: only squares have an odd number of factors.

### B. Staircase sums (ages 11 and up)

Which numbers are a sum of two or more consecutive whole numbers? 9 is 4 plus 5, and 10 is 1 plus 2 plus 3 plus 4. Test up to 32. The ones that fail are 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32: the powers of two.

### C. Knight's reach (ages 11 and up)

On an endless chessboard, what is the fewest knight moves to reach the square one step to the right? Three. Each knight move changes the square colour, so an odd number is needed, and one move cannot do it.

### D. Cutting a cake (ages 12 and up)

Three cuts across a round cake: what is the most pieces you can make? Seven. With n straight cuts the maximum is 1 plus n times n plus 1 over 2, so 2 cuts give 4 and 4 cuts give 11.

The circle conversation is why each new cut adds one more piece than the cut before it.

### E. Digit reversal (ages 12 and up)

Take any three-digit number with different first and last digits, reverse it, and subtract the smaller from the larger: 742 minus 247 is 495. The answer is always a multiple of 99.

Write the number as 100a plus 10b plus c, and the difference is 99 times the gap between a and c.

All five problems and explanations are written by Modern Age Coders and are not taken from Axiom, NRICH, STEP Support or any other programme.

## How do you tell a good online maths circle from a tuition class with a new name?

Ask five questions. A real circle, free or paid, should answer yes to most of them.

1. Does one problem ever last more than one session?
2. Do pupils speak to each other during the session, not only to the leader?
3. Is there no test, no grade and no homework sheet of similar questions?
4. Are pupils asked to write explanations, and does someone read them?
5. Does the same group meet for months, so pupils know each other's ideas?

## What families ask about maths circles

### What is a maths circle?

A small group that meets regularly to work on rich problems with no syllabus and no grades, often staying on one problem for weeks. King's College London Mathematics School describes its Axiom circles as students coming together to grapple with puzzles and learn to think like mathematicians.

### Are there maths circles in the UK?

Yes. The Axiom circles run by King's College London Mathematics School are a long-term programme from Year 7, partly online. University outreach such as the Oxford Online Maths Club and Cambridge's STEP Support Programme works in a similar spirit for older pupils.

### Can my child join a maths circle online?

Some free programmes run online, but most have an age range or priority rules. The Oxford Online Maths Club needs no registration and suits sixth formers best. Our own weekly circle runs online for ages 10 to 18 and is open to any family.

### Is the Oxford Online Maths Club free?

Yes. Oxford says it is free, needs no registration and is recorded. It streams on Thursdays at 5pm UK time, and from June to December it hosts the Oxford Maths Admissions Test Livestream, which now focuses on the TMUA.

### Who gets a place on Axiom maths circles?

King's says a pupil who joins in Year 7 keeps a place for their whole school journey. Places are prioritised for students who meet the widening-participation criteria the school publishes on its Axiom page, so read that page before applying.

### What age should a child start a maths circle?

Many circles start around age 10 or 11, the Year 6 to Year 7 point, once a child can explain their thinking in words. Younger children do well with reasoning and puzzle lessons first.

### Is a maths circle the same as olympiad training?

No. Olympiad training works towards a paper on a fixed date. A circle has no paper at all, although circle pupils often enjoy olympiads because they are used to long, unfamiliar problems.

### Is STEP Support a circle?

Not quite. The STEP Support Programme is free online modules, designed to be studied weekly over about six months from Year 12, with hints and full solutions. Working through it with friends gives it some of a circle's feel.

### Are you connected with Oxford, Cambridge or King's?

No. We run our own classes and answer to no university or school. We describe these programmes from their own pages so families can find them, and we have no link with any of them.

### When does your circle meet?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Every time is given in UK time.
