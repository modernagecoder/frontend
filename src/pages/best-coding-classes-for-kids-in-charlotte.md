## How many Charlotte bus stops have a shelter? Percent of what?

The City of Charlotte publishes every active CATS bus stop on its open data portal, with a column for shelters, benches and more. We asked the server to count them on 18 October 2026 and turned the answers into a lesson about denominators.

**CATS bus stops with a shelter or a bench (our counts and percentages from the City of Charlotte Bus Stops layer, 18 October 2026)**

| Group of stops | How many stops | With a shelter | With a bench or a shelter |
|---|---|---|---|
| All active stops | 2,849 | 299, or 10.5% | 637, or 22.4% |
| Stops marked as a transfer point | 433 | 103, or 23.8% | 180, or 41.6% |

### 1. Count the whole

First question: how many stops are there? The server answered 2,849. That number is the denominator, the bottom of every fraction that follows, and children write it down before anything else.

### 2. Filter a group

Next, keep only the stops whose Shelter column says Shelter. There are 299. Divide by 2,849 and you get about 10.5%, roughly one stop in ten.

### 3. Change the bottom

Now keep only transfer points, where riders change buses: 433 stops, 103 of them sheltered. Same city, same shelters, yet the share jumps to 23.8% because the bottom of the fraction changed.

### The sentence we want every child to be able to say

Saying "23.8% of stops have shelters" would be wrong; the true sentence is "23.8% of transfer-point stops have shelters". Children who learn to name the group behind a percentage at ten are hard to fool with a chart at fifteen.

Younger children do the same lesson with paper slips: one slip per stop in a small sample, sorted into piles, counted, then written as a fraction. Older ones write it in Python with a list of dictionaries and a filter, and then try a bonus question: 14 stops have both a bench and a shelter, so why does "bench or shelter" come to 637 and not 299 plus 352? Working out that the 14 would otherwise be counted twice is a real piece of logic.

All of these numbers are ours, worked out from the city&rsquo;s layer on 18 October 2026; the city notes that the stop file is updated over time, so a later count may differ. The City of Charlotte publishes the layer under a Creative Commons Attribution NonCommercial ShareAlike licence. We quote only a handful of totals with credit to the city, we do not copy or resell the data, and students download it themselves from the city&rsquo;s portal.

## Should a Charlotte kid learn to code when AI can write code?

Yes, for the same reason children still learn arithmetic in the age of calculators.

An AI chatbot could tell a child that 23.8% of stops have shelters, and the child would have no way to see the missing words "of transfer points". Children who have filtered the list themselves know exactly which group the number describes, because they chose it.

Our youngest students do not use adult AI tools at all. Older children learn, in a supervised class, what AI is, how it makes mistakes and why a person must check its answers, with tools whose own terms allow their age.

Coding gives a child the power to ask a precise question and check the answer; that is the skill AI makes more valuable, not less. The longer argument is in [is coding still worth learning in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
