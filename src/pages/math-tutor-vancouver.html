Math tutor in Vancouver

Where does a Vancouver student find a live math tutor?

Modern Age Coders is an online math tutor for Vancouver students, teaching live with a real teacher from Grade 1 up to Pre-calculus 12 and Calculus 12, either one to one or in a group of fifteen to twenty at the same level. Senior students usually take Western Canada Grade 12 Math: Pre-calculus 12 and Math 30-1; contest fans take Waterloo Math Contests. Lesson one is free.

British Columbia splits its math courses earlier than many parents realise. After Mathematics 9, a Vancouver student chooses between Foundations of Mathematics and Pre-calculus 10 and Workplace Mathematics 10, and by Grade 11 the Foundations and Pre-calculus routes have separated. UBC’s requirements data lists Pre-Calculus 12 for Science, Applied Science (Engineering) and Commerce, so the choice made at fifteen shows up again at seventeen. This page lays out that pathway using the province’s own course descriptions, teaches one statistics lesson on the City of Vancouver’s public tree records, works a problem from scratch, then covers contests and how to judge tutoring prices.

Two features of that list matter more than any single topic. First, Workplace Mathematics 10 sits beside the shared Grade 10 course, and Foundations 11 and Pre-calculus 11 run on separate tracks, so a family whose child moves off the Pre-calculus route should ask the school early what that means for Grade 12. Second, Statistics 12 exists as its own course in BC; its content list names the five-number summary, Chebyshev’s inequality, the 68-95-99.7 rule and correlation and causation, and it says outright that software should be used.

What UBC asks for

UBC’s own requirements data lists Pre-Calculus 12 under the Grade 12 requirements for Science, for Applied Science (Engineering) and for Commerce at the UBC Sauder School of Business. Our UBC Computer Science page covers the computing side.

Grade 9 is not a warm-up

Mathematics 9 already covers operations with rational numbers, exponent laws, polynomials, multi-step linear equations and two-variable linear relations. Students who are shaky on negative fractions here tend to stall when slope arrives in Grade 10.

Calculus is optional, not extra

Calculus 12 is a separate course from Pre-calculus 12. In the UBC requirements data we saved it appears once, as recommended for one program, and not at all for Science or Engineering. A student taking both in one year carries two heavy courses, which is where one-to-one help earns its keep.

What our lessons are, and are not

We teach alongside the course a Vancouver student is taking at school, and we do not grant BC credits; marks and credits for every course from Mathematics 9 to Calculus 12 come from the school. We are not connected with the BC Ministry of Education and Child Care, UBC, the City of Vancouver or any Vancouver school district.

Vancouver data lesson

A street tree 66 metres wide? Finding outliers in Vancouver’s public trees file

The City of Vancouver publishes its public tree inventory as open data. Statistics 12 students summarise the trunk diameters, set a fence for outliers and find out what one impossible number does to each statistic. Every figure here is ours.

The file is called Public trees. The City describes it as a listing of public trees on boulevards and public trees in parks, in the City of Vancouver, and warns that private trees are not included in the inventory and that it may be several years between updates for some attributes. Our export on 23 October 2026 held 184,268 rows, each with a genus, a species, a height in metres, a trunk diameter in centimetres and one of the city’s 22 local areas.

The maximum is the first thing a careful student questions. A trunk 6,673 cm across is a tree more than 66 metres wide; the record, asset 68485, is a pin oak in Mount Pleasant, and the next largest tree in the file measures 779.0 cm. One extra digit typed at entry would explain it, but the file cannot tell us, so we flag it rather than fix it. What the value does is instructive: drop it, and the mean moves only from 32.48 to 32.45 cm, because one value among 183,791 has little pull on an average. The maximum and the range, though, are ruined by it.

The Python behind the table reads the export, keeps positive diameters, finds the quartiles and the fence, and counts what lies beyond it. Only the standard library is needed:import csv, statistics with open("public-trees.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as f: rows = list(csv.DictReader(f, delimiter=";")) sizes = sorted(float(r["diameter_cm"]) for r in rows if float(r["diameter_cm"]) > 0) q1, median, q3 = statistics.quantiles(sizes, n=4, method="inclusive") fence = q3 + 1.5 * (q3 - q1) print("five-number summary:", sizes[0], q1, median, q3, sizes[-1]) print("upper fence:", round(fence, 2)) print("trees beyond it:", sum(1 for s in sizes if s > fence)) print("mean:", round(statistics.mean(sizes), 2))

Outside the fence is not wrong

Our script finds 3,925 trees above the 98.95 cm fence. Almost all are simply big old trees, which Vancouver has plenty of. The 1.5 IQR rule marks values worth a second look, not values to delete, and students must say that in words.

Zeros are missing data

There are 477 rows with a diameter of 0 and 3 with a height of 0. A tree with no trunk is not a tiny tree; it is a measurement nobody recorded. Leaving zeros in would drag the first quartile down for no good reason.

Heights come in steps

The file holds only 84 distinct heights, and the eight commonest cover 93.7 percent of rows: 4.6, 7.6, 10.7, 13.7, 16.8 and 19.8 m lead. The steps are about 3 m apart, which we read as ten-foot bands converted to metres; that reading is ours, not the City’s.

Correlation, before and after cleaning

Across 183,788 trees with both measurements above zero, our correlation between diameter and height is r = 0.651. Keep only trees inside the fence and it rises to 0.755. For maples alone it is 0.807; for the Prunus genus, cherries and plums, 0.636. Thicker trees tend to be taller, but the height steps blur the pattern, and nothing here says diameter causes height: both grow with age, the lurking variable Statistics 12 warns about.

A follow-up session compares genera. Our medians are 22.9 cm for maples (Acer, the commonest of 171 genus labels, with 41,976 trees), 33.0 cm for Prunus and 36.8 cm for lindens (Tilia). A worked problem, ours

Worked problem: what one huge value does to a mean and a median

The nine diameters below are invented by us, not taken from the file, so the arithmetic can be done by hand. The method is the one the Vancouver data needs.

Question. A student measures nine trunks on one block, in centimetres: 8, 12, 15, 21, 25, 30, 38, 46 and 240. (a) Find the five-number summary and the interquartile range. (b) Use the 1.5 IQR rule to decide whether 240 is an outlier. (c) Compare the mean and the median with and without it, and say which you would report.

Step 1, order and split. The list is already in order. With nine values the median is the fifth, 25. Using the method our Python uses here (the median of each half, counting the middle value in both), Q1 is 15 and Q3 is 38. So the summary is 8, 15, 25, 38, 240, and the IQR is 38 minus 15, which is 23.

Step 2, build the fences. 1.5 times 23 is 34.5. The upper fence is 38 plus 34.5, or 72.5, and the lower fence is 15 minus 34.5, which is negative and so catches nothing. 240 is far beyond 72.5, so it is an outlier by this rule. Whether it is an error is a separate question, answered by going back to the tree.

Step 3, compare centres. With all nine values the mean is 435 divided by 9, about 48.33, nearly twice the median. Without 240 the mean is 195 divided by 8, about 24.38, and the median of the remaining eight is the average of 21 and 25, which is 23. The median barely moved; the mean halved. Report the median, and mention the outlier in a sentence.

Step 4, contrast with the city file. In our nine-tree example one outlier doubles the mean. In the Vancouver file a far wilder value moved the mean by three hundredths of a centimetre. The difference is the sample size: one value among nine carries a ninth of the weight; one among 183,791 carries almost none. That contrast is a Statistics 12 idea in one line.

Try it yourself. (1) Replace 240 with 60 and redo the fence test. (2) Find the standard deviation of the eight values without 240 and a z-score for 46, the Foundations 11 way. (3) Explain why a box plot shows the outlier clearly while a mean alone hides it. We go through answers in the free lesson.

Math and AI

Why a Vancouver student still has to understand the statistics

An AI tool will summarise a data file instantly. Whether the summary is honest is a human decision.

Ask a chatbot for the largest street tree in Vancouver and it may well report a trunk 66 metres wide without blinking. It has no reason to doubt the file. A student who has computed a fence, compared the maximum with the next value and thought about how a trunk is measured knows to stop and check.

That is why our teachers have students estimate before computing and write one sentence about what each result means. Teenagers meet AI tools only within the age limits we keep to, and treat them as a checker for finished work rather than a producer of it. Graded school assignments stay the student’s own work.

BC’s Grade 9 course asks students to spot misleading statistics; that habit is exactly what an AI summary needs from its reader. The longer argument is in why reasoning still matters when AI can answer.
