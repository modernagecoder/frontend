Dordrecht today

A whole island in one municipality, a fifth of it water

The national register held 122,991 people on 1 January 2026, in 57,807 households averaging 2.08 people, on 77.56 square kilometres of land inside 99.47 of territory. That leaves 2,191 hectares of water, nearly 22 percent of the municipality, which is what a city built where three rivers meet looks like on a map. In 1995 the count was 114,089, so Dordrecht has gained 8,902 people in thirty-one years, a rise of 7.8 percent, among the slowest in this series.

A working city, not a student one

18,375 residents are under fifteen and 13,765 are between fifteen and twenty-five; 33,357 are between twenty-five and forty-five, 32,218 between forty-five and sixty-five, and 25,276 are sixty-five or over. 23,817 households hold one person, 41 percent. Only 804 students are counted at institutions here, while 2,370 residents are in applied higher education and 1,070 at university level.

Thirty-six primary schools, eleven secondary

36 primary establishments teach 10,534 pupils and 11 secondary establishments teach 7,208. The whole municipality is one woonplaats divided into 14 wijken and 133 buurten, so nothing is far, but the island has one road and rail crossing to the north and the traffic behaves accordingly.

The oldest city of Holland

Its entry says so directly, and dates the confirmation of city rights by Count Willem the First to 1220, after earlier charters granted around 1195 and 1205. Note that the claim is about Holland rather than about the Netherlands, which is a different competition and one this cluster settles elsewhere.

An island by accident, and a synod

The municipality covers the entire Island of Dordrecht, the Eiland van Dordrecht, about 9,000 hectares. The island exists because of the St Elizabeth floods, the Sint-Elisabethsvloed of 1421 and a second in 1424, which put the city on water it had not been on before. In 1618 and 1619 the Synod of Dordrecht met here, setting the Remonstrants against the Counter-Remonstrants and commissioning the translation that became the Statenbijbel of 1637.

Signature project

Two thousand, a hundred thousand, or a dozen: build the habit of tracing a number to its source

One night in 1421, three figures separated by nearly four orders of magnitude, and a lesson about what repetition does and does not prove.

1. Line the three up

About a dozen deaths in the Grote Waard, from modern analysis. Two thousand, carried in the traditional account and hedged in its own source with the words as they say. A hundred thousand, from legend. The largest is about eight thousand times the smallest, and all three describe the same night.

2. Ask how each one is supported

The learner separates the question of what a figure says from the question of how it is known. One rests on reasoning about the settlements and the ground; one rests on a chain of retellings whose earliest link the source itself flags as hearsay; one rests on nothing at all except being memorable. The three are not three estimates, they are three different kinds of claim.

3. Count the sources, not the mentions

A figure repeated in a hundred books may descend from one manuscript, and search results measure how often a claim has been copied rather than how well it is attested. The learner writes the rule down: before believing a number, find the earliest thing that says it, and count how many independent things say it after that.

Repetition is not evidence, and a search result is a count of copies

Numbers travel better than the reasons for them. A figure written once in a chronicle gets copied into a history, then into a schoolbook, then into a thousand pages, and at every step the hedge that came with it falls away a little more, until it is quoted flatly by people who have never seen the original and reasonably assume somebody else has. The defences are unglamorous. Find the earliest source that states the figure. Ask whether later sources are independent of it or descended from it. Keep the hedge that the original carried, because as they say is part of the claim rather than decoration. And treat a very round, very large number about a very old event as a hypothesis about human memory rather than a measurement. Every figure in the table above is quoted from the entries named in the evidence for this page, including the hedges, and none is preferred here.

Coding and AI

An AI will tell you two thousand people drowned in 1421. Why should a child in Dordrecht learn to ask who counted?

Because that figure is everywhere, and being everywhere is exactly what a model measures.

A system trained on text learns which claims appear most often, and the most often repeated version of this one is two thousand. That is not a flaw in the training so much as an accurate reflection of the corpus: the number really is repeated more than any other. What the corpus does not encode is that the repetitions descend from a small number of tellings, that the source carrying the figure hedges it, or that a modern reading of the same evidence lands three orders of magnitude lower.

So the learner builds the trace instead of accepting the total. The work is unglamorous, mostly reading and record-keeping, and it produces the single most useful question anybody can carry into professional life: how many independent sources are there, really. A fourteen-year-old in Dubbeldam who has followed one number back to a single manuscript will follow a market-size figure back to a slide at twenty-four. Tools will keep reporting the consensus. Noticing that a consensus can be one source copied a thousand times is the part a person does.

The case for a Dordrecht child learning to code in 2026 is therefore nothing to do with medieval floods. It is that the most confidently held numbers are often the least examined, that tracing one to its origin is a skill rather than a talent, and that this city keeps an unusually clear example of it in its own history. The longer argument is in the argument for a child writing code in an age of AI.

The city, its island, its records, the teaching and the terms.

How many people really died in the St Elizabeth flood?

Nobody knows, and the honest answer is to give all three published figures with their support. The entry on the flood dates it to the night of 18 to 19 November 1421, says about thirty villages were made uninhabitable, records that two thousand people are said to have drowned, notes that legend inflated the toll to a hundred thousand, and reports that modern analysis puts the deaths in the Grote Waard at about a dozen. Tracing a famous number back to what actually supports it is the project on this page.

Is Dordrecht the oldest city in the Netherlands?

It calls itself the oldest city of Holland, which is a narrower claim, and its entry dates the confirmation of its city rights by Count Willem the First to 1220 after earlier charters around 1195 and 1205. The separate argument about the oldest city of the Netherlands is set out on the Nijmegen page in this series.

Is the whole municipality really an island?

Yes. The municipality covers the entire Island of Dordrecht, about 9,000 hectares, and just under 22 percent of its 99.47 square kilometres is water. The island exists because of the St Elizabeth floods of 1421 and 1424.

Is any of this taught in Dutch?

It is not. All teaching happens in English. School vocabulary is the exception and stays Dutch, so a teacher says groep, havo, vwo or profielkeuze rather than reaching for a translation. There is no Dutch edition of this course.

What time are classes for a family in Dordrecht?

Summer here puts three and a half hours between the two sides and winter puts four and a half, all of the movement coming from the Dutch clock. What works in practice is the stretch after school and before bedtime, plus any hour of Saturday or Sunday. The recurring slot is fixed at the free lesson and then stays.

Why are there so few students in Dordrecht?

Because there is very little to study here. Only 804 students are counted at institutions in the municipality, while 2,370 residents are registered in applied higher education and 1,070 at university level.
