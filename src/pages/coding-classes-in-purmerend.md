## A market town, an Amsterdam growth town, and since 2022 the Beemster too

The national register held 96,182 people on 1 January 2026, in 43,664 households averaging 2.16 people, on 93.61 square kilometres of land inside 96.63 of territory: a published 1,025 residents to the square kilometre. In 1995 the count was 73,084. The statistics office has recalculated the years before the 2022 merger to the present boundaries, so that figure already includes the Beemster, and the gain of 23,098 people, 32 percent, is on the same ground throughout.

### A market town since the fifteenth century

From 1434 Purmerend is described as a town in official documents, and in 1484 it received market rights. The town has called itself Marktstad for many years, and the Purmerends Museum stands in the old centre. The encyclopaedia names seven wijken for the town: Centrum, Overwhere, Wheermolen, Purmer-Noord, De Gors, Purmer-Zuid and Weidevenne.

### Growth for Amsterdam

As a groeikern from the 1960s Purmerend grew at great speed: after 1960 the population rose from about 10,000 to about 80,000. The encyclopaedia puts it plainly: the town lies conveniently for Amsterdam, where many residents, often former residents of the capital, work.

### Families, and an older quarter

15,586 of the 43,664 households have children and 15,744 hold one person. 14,241 residents are under fifteen, 10,401 are aged fifteen to twenty-five, 24,402 twenty-five to forty-five and 25,988 forty-five to sixty-five, while 21,150 are sixty-five or over, 22 percent.

**The Beemster, dry since 19 May 1612** The Beemster became dry land on 19 May 1612, with Jan Adriaanszoon Leeghwater in an advisory role, and in 1999 the polder was placed on the UNESCO World Heritage List. Within it lie four villages: Middenbeemster, Noordbeemster, Westbeemster and Zuidoostbeemster. The former municipality of Beemster covered 72.08 square kilometres, 2.80 of them water. On 1 January 2022 it was dissolved and merged with Purmerend, after a council election for the new municipality on 24 November 2021 at which the turnout was 32.4 percent.

## Who answered, and who was only counted

Every result from a vote, a survey or a feedback button is a result about the people who took part. The first job is to know how many that was.

### 1. Count who was asked

Everyone entitled to vote in the new municipality was asked in November 2021. That number is the base against which every answer has to be read, and without it any result is a fraction with no bottom.

### 2. Count who answered

The turnout was 32.4 percent. About two in every three people who could have taken part did not, and nothing in the result says whether they would have answered the same way. A response rate is the first number to print, not a footnote.

### 3. Say whose answer it is

Write the result as the choice of those who voted, not of Purmerend. The same discipline applies to the map and the headcount: a figure about the land is about the land, and a figure about residents is about residents.

**The same gap between who was asked and who answered**

| Where it happens | Who was asked | Who answered | The usual mistake |
|---|---|---|---|
| A customer survey | every customer on the list | the few who replied | reporting the replies as what customers think |
| App store reviews | every person who installed the app | the handful who wrote a review | reading the average star rating as general satisfaction |
| Usage data | every user of a product | those who left tracking switched on | treating the tracked users as all users |
| Exit questionnaires | everyone who left | the ones who bothered to fill it in | assuming the unhappy ones are represented |
| The first council election of the merged Purmerend | everyone entitled to vote | 32.4 percent of them | describing the result as what Purmerend wanted |

### A response rate belongs next to every result

The people who answer a question are rarely a random sample of the people who were asked. They may care more, have more time, feel more strongly, or simply be the kind of person who answers things, and each of those can move the result in a direction that the result itself cannot reveal. That is why survey researchers weight their answers, why careful product teams compare the users who respond with the users who do not on everything they know about both, and why a good report prints its response rate beside its headline. Machine learning inherits the same problem through its data: a model trained on the users who agreed to share data learns about those users, and a model trained on reviews learns about reviewers. Purmerend shows the idea with published figures and no drama. Its first joint council was chosen on a turnout of 32.4 percent, which is a fact about participation and implies nothing about the absent two thirds. Its territory is three quarters polder and its population nine tenths town, which is a fact about the difference between land and people. In each case the error is the same small word: saying Purmerend when the figure is about a particular part of it.

## Five habits for any result that came from volunteers

Polls, surveys, reviews, feedback forms, opt-in data and elections all report on the people who took part. Practised on a town whose council, map and headcount each tell a different story.

**Reporting a result for the people it really describes**

| Stage | Rule | What it prevents | What it gives you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Record the base | Store how many were asked as well as how many answered | Results with no denominator | A response rate you can print |
| Print the rate | Put the response rate next to every headline figure | A third passed off as the whole | Readers who can judge the result |
| Compare the groups | Check responders against non-responders on anything known about both | Unseen differences between them | A sense of which way the bias runs |
| Name the subject | Say whether a figure is about land, residents or participants | A map read as a headcount | Statements about the right group |
| Keep the silence | Treat non-response as missing, never as agreement | Two thirds counted as a yes | Honest uncertainty |

## An AI will tell you what Purmerend decided. Why should a teenager in Weidevenne ask how many people took part?

Because the first council of the merged town was chosen on a turnout of 32.4 percent.

Ask a language model what Purmerend wanted when it merged with the Beemster, and it will answer in the voice of the town, because that is how news reports are written and how it learned to summarise them. It will not usually say that about two in three people entitled to vote did not, or that nobody knows what they would have chosen. The same model, asked about a product, will summarise its reviews as what customers think, and the reviews came from the small share of customers who write reviews. The answer is fluent in both cases, and in both cases it describes the answerers as if they were everyone.

A learner who has written a simulation in which the people who feel strongly are more likely to reply, and watched the survey drift away from the truth as fewer people answered, carries that picture into every dataset afterwards. Machine learning depends on it, since models are built from whatever data happened to be collected, and the people who opt in, click, rate or reply are almost never a fair picture of the rest. Models will keep speaking for everyone. Asking who actually took part stays a human job.

The case for a child in Purmerend learning to code in 2026 is therefore not the commute to Amsterdam. It is that anyone who has counted the people who did not answer learns never to confuse a response with a population again. The longer argument is in [why a child should still learn to code in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
