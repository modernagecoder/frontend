## When are the July school holidays in Peru?

In 2026, from Monday 27 July to Friday 7 August, the third block of what MINEDU calls "management weeks". Teaching stopped on Friday 24 July and restarted on Monday 10 August.

**The middle of the 2026 school year, from MINEDU's Tabla 1**

| Block | Weeks | 2026 dates |
|---|---|---|
| Second block of teaching weeks | 9 | 25 May to 24 July |
| Third block of management weeks (the mid-year break) | 2 | 27 July to 7 August |
| Third block of teaching weeks | 9 | 10 August to 9 October |

Source: MINEDU, Resolución Ministerial 501-2025-MINEDU, Norma Técnica para el Año Escolar 2026, Tabla 1 "Distribución y fechas para la calendarización 2026". The scanned, signed page was read by eye on 9 November 2026. The full 2026 year has 36 teaching weeks and 8 management weeks.

### What "management weeks" means

MINEDU's norm defines management weeks as time for the school's staff to plan, follow up and evaluate, working together or alone. Teaching weeks are the ones set aside for developing students' competencies. So for pupils, the two management weeks in July and August are the mid-year holidays.

The same table places one-week management breaks in May and October, and two-week blocks before teaching starts in March and after it ends in December. July's is the only two-week pause that falls between two teaching blocks.

### Why your school's dates may differ

The norm lets regional education offices (DRE) and local ones (UGEL) set a different distribution of teaching and management weeks for climate or social reasons, and schools may organise the year in two-month or three-month terms. Private schools often publish their own calendar.

Adult education follows its own dates: the norm splits Educación Básica Alternativa into a first period from 13 March to 26 July and a second from 10 August to 20 December. Always check your own school's calendar first.

**What about July 2027?** MINEDU had not published the 2027 school-year norm when we checked on 9 November 2026: a search of gob.pe for its exact title found nothing, against 26 results for the 2026 norm. We will not guess the 2027 dates. When the norm appears, its Tabla 1 will show them, as it did for 2026.

Because two quiet weeks are long enough to begin and short enough to keep going. A summer start has its own advantages, which our page on Peru's January to March break covers; July has different ones.

In the break there is no homework competing for the evening. A child can take the first, slowest steps of coding, where most frustration lives, without also juggling school deadlines.

Classes do not end on 7 August. A learner who has finished a first project in the break returns to school with a habit already formed, and the weekly classes simply carry on in the second half of the year.

## How do you count a practice streak? A Python lesson with a hidden bug

A streak is the number of days in a row a goal is met. Teenagers love counting them, and writing a streak counter teaches one of the classic loop bugs: a result that only gets recorded when something ends, so the last run is never recorded at all.

### The task

We give the learner a list of twelve numbers: invented minutes of coding practice on each calendar day of a twelve-day break, Monday to the Friday of the following week. The goal is 20 minutes. What is the longest run of days in a row at or above the goal?

Looking at the list by hand, the answer is 4: the last four days are 30, 25, 45 and 20. Most first programs look like Version 1, and Version 1 says 3.

### Why Version 1 is wrong

Version 1 only updates longest when a run breaks, inside the else. The final run of four never breaks, because the list ends first, so it is never compared with longest. The program reports the earlier run of three.

The fix is to compare after every day, which is what Version 2 does in one line. It is shorter as well as correct, which is often the sign of a better idea rather than a patch.

**Version 1: records a streak only when it breaks** (Python)
```python
# minutes of coding practice on each day of the break (an invented log)
log = [30, 25, 0, 20, 40, 35, 0, 15, 30, 25, 45, 20]
GOAL = 20

longest = 0
run = 0
for minutes in log:
    if minutes >= GOAL:
        run += 1
    else:
        longest = max(longest, run)   # a streak just ended: record it
        run = 0
print("Longest streak:", longest, "days")
```
It prints Longest streak: 3 days, which is wrong for this log.

**Version 2: checks after every day** (Python)
```python
longest = 0
run = 0
for minutes in log:
    run = run + 1 if minutes >= GOAL else 0
    longest = max(longest, run)       # check after every day, not only at a break
print("Longest streak:", longest, "days")
print("Days at goal:", sum(m >= GOAL for m in log), "of", len(log))
```
Run after Version 1, it prints Longest streak: 4 days and Days at goal: 9 of 12.

The habit we want to teach is to **test the case where the answer sits at the very end**. The bug hides in any loop that saves its result only at a boundary: a run of wins, a sequence of rising temperatures, a stretch of days online. Learners then write their own test: a log where every day meets the goal. Version 1 prints 0 for it, which is plainly absurd, and nobody forgets the lesson after that.

1. **Ages 6 to 8, no screen.** Colour a square on a paper calendar for every day of the break with some practice. Count the longest line of coloured squares. Did you count the line that touches the edge?
2. **Ages 9 to 12, Scratch.** Make a sprite that counts how many times in a row you click within two seconds, and shows the longest run. Check what happens if you stop on a good run.
3. **Ages 13 to 17, Python.** Predict what Version 1 prints for [25, 25, 25], then run it. Rewrite it so the bug cannot happen.
4. **16 and up.** Change Version 2 so it also prints which day the longest streak started. Decide what to print if two streaks tie, and write it down as a comment.

## What does a child need at home for online classes in the break?

A computer, a reliable connection and a quiet corner. INEI's figures for 2026 suggest the computer, not the internet, is the item more families lack.

### What INEI measured

INEI's household survey for the second quarter of 2026 found that 36.4% of Peruvian households had at least one computer. Internet use was lower among children aged 6 to 11, at 68.2%, than among young people aged 12 to 18, at 92.5%.

In other words, many teenagers are already online every day; younger children are less often, and fewer homes have a computer than have a phone.

### What that means for coding

Coding needs a keyboard and a screen big enough to see code and a game side by side. A phone can join a video call, but it is not enough to type programs. A shared family laptop is fine if the child can have it for the class hour.

For the youngest learners, a headset with a microphone helps them hear the mentor's English clearly, especially in a lively holiday house. Check the setup in the free first class, before the break begins.

Source: INEI, Estadísticas de las Tecnologías de Información y Comunicación en los Hogares, II Trimestre 2026, read on 8 November 2026. INEI writes decimals with a comma (36,4%); we use a point.

When suits your family. Peru keeps UTC minus 5 in every month with no clock changes, and India keeps UTC plus 5:30, so India is ten and a half hours ahead whether it is July or January. These are clock conversions, not class times.

Source: IANA time zone database, America/Lima (UTC minus 5; Peru's last summer-time rule dates from 1994) and Asia/Kolkata (UTC plus 5:30), with INACAL's Peru clock matching on 8 November 2026. We worked out the conversions.
