ICD2O explained: the Grade 10 digital technology course, and what happened to ICS2O

Search for the Grade 10 computer course in Ontario and two codes come back: ICD2O and ICS2O. The current Ontario curriculum website lists one of them, ICD2O, Digital Technology and Innovations in the Changing World, issued in 2023. The other, ICS2O, Introduction to Computer Studies, is the Grade 10 course in the 2008 curriculum document. This page reads ICD2O from the ministry’s own data, sets it beside ICS2O expectation by expectation, and builds one small project at its level. We teach coding beside the school course and do not grant Ontario credits, so the ICD2O credit itself comes from the school.

The description, in the ministry’s words

The record opens with an ambitious sentence: “This course helps students develop cutting-edge digital technology and computer programming skills that will support them in contributing to and leading the global economic, scientific and societal innovations of tomorrow.”

It names artificial intelligence and cybersecurity as topics, then ends on judgement rather than skill: “Using critical thinking skills with a focus on digital citizenship, students will investigate the appropriate use and development of the digital technologies that they encounter every day, as well as the benefits and limitations of these technologies.”

What open means

Open is one of the three course types in Ontario computer studies. The 2008 document defines open courses as courses that broaden students’ knowledge and interests, and adds: “They are not designed with the specific requirements of universities, colleges, or the workplace in mind.”

In practice, an open Grade 10 course is for every student, whatever their plans. A student heading for university computer science and a student who simply wants to understand the technology around them can sit in the same ICD2O class.

Three strands, ten overall expectations

The three ICD2O strands and what each one asks for

ICD2O has three strands and ten overall expectations. Each expectation has a short title of its own on the curriculum website, which makes the course easy to map. Strand A works differently from the other two, and that is worth understanding first.

Strand A runs through the others

Strand A, Computational Thinking and Making Connections, is not taught as a separate unit. The website says its learning happens in the context of Strands B and C, and “it should be assessed and evaluated within these contexts”. So the planning, the ethics and the career links come up inside the hardware lessons and the programming projects.

Two of its specific expectations stand out for a Grade 10 course. Students “investigate how to identify and address bias involving digital technology”, and they “analyze accessibility issues involving digital technology, and identify measures that can improve accessibility”. The project further down is built on the second.

Strand B is everyday technology

Strand B is about the devices and software students already use. It asks them to “use file management techniques, including those related to local and cloud storage, to organize, edit, and share files”, and to “apply safe and effective security practices, including practices to protect their privacy”.

Its last overall expectation, B4, is where artificial intelligence and automation sit. Students look at what the systems can do and at their limits, which is a thinking skill as much as a technical one.

What the comparison shows

Both are Grade 10 open courses with no prerequisite, and both teach beginning programming. The programming core is similar: variables, decisions, loops, errors and documentation appear in each.

The differences are in the world around the code. ICD2O adds artificial intelligence and automation, cloud storage, cybersecurity as a strand of its own, bias, and programs that work with outside data and libraries. ICS2O spent more of its time on the computer itself: hardware components, operating systems and home networking.

What the sources do not say

We did not find a sentence in either source saying that ICD2O replaces ICS2O, or giving a date when one stopped and the other began. The website lists ICD2O and not ICS2O; the 2008 document describes ICS2O. That is all either says.

If a school course calendar still lists ICS2O, the school is the right place to ask which curriculum the course is taught from. For a student, the practical point is the same either way: either course is a gentle first step before Grade 11 computer studies.

Strand C, close up

Strand C is where ICD2O students write code. Its three overall expectations break into specific ones that read like a checklist for a first programming course. Here is the checklist, with our note on what each looks like in a lesson.

Which language?

The website’s expectations do not name a programming language, and classes use different ones. Python suits a course like this because a beginner can read it almost like English, though a teacher may choose a block-based tool or another text language.

We teach ICD2O students in Python unless their class uses something else. The ideas in strand C carry straight into Grade 11, where ICS3U and ICS3C students work in a text language.

Where Grade 10 students get stuck

Not on syntax, usually. They get stuck on the step before: turning a vague task into exact steps. That is why we start every project with C1.2 thinking, the everyday algorithm written in plain words, and only then open the editor.

The second sticking point is errors. A message such as TypeError looks like a wall to a beginner. Read slowly, it says exactly which line went wrong and why, and learning to read it is a skill the curriculum names directly.

A Grade 10 project, ours

One ICD2O-level project: a colour contrast checker in Python

This project is ours, written for this page at the level of ICD2O. It joins three parts of the course: accessibility from strand A, data and a library from strand C, and a modification task at the end. It answers a real question: can people with low vision read this text on this background?

  1. Describe the everyday algorithm first. To judge whether text is readable, compare how bright the text colour is with how bright the background is. The bigger the difference, the easier it is to read.
  2. Find a trustworthy rule. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, WCAG 2.1, published by the W3C, define brightness as relative luminance and give the contrast ratio formula, “(L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05)”, where L1 is the lighter colour.
  3. Break it into small functions. One turns a single colour channel into linear light, one works out the luminance of a colour, and one compares two colours. Each does one job and can be tested alone.
  4. Read the data with a library. The colour pairs are a small CSV table, read with Python’s csv module. Using an existing module instead of splitting text by hand is what expectation C3.4 asks for.
  5. Decide with nested conditions. WCAG 2.1 asks for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for body text and at least 3:1 for large text. An if inside an else turns the ratio into a verdict.
  6. Test with cases you can check. Black on white must give 21, the highest ratio possible. A colour against itself would give 1. If those two come out wrong, nothing else can be trusted.
The checker’s results (ours, Python 3.13; thresholds from WCAG 2.1)
Text colourBackgroundContrast ratioVerdict
#000000 (black)#FFFFFF (white)21.0Passes for body text
#777777 (mid grey)#FFFFFF (white)4.48Large text only
#30520C (moss green)#FBF8F2 (paper)8.47Passes for body text
#FFFF00 (yellow)#FFFFFF (white)1.07Fails

The modification. WCAG 2.1 also has an enhanced level, which asks for “a contrast ratio of at least 7:1”. Changing the program to report four levels instead of three is a C3.2 task: modify an existing program so it completes a different task. The cleanest change replaces the nested if with one chain of elif tests, checked from the strictest level down.

What the project teaches

Mid grey on white, 4.48, misses the body text line by a hair. A student who rounds to one decimal place too early would print 4.5 and wrongly pass it, which is a real lesson about when to round: at the end, never in the middle.

The order of the elif tests also matters. Checking 3 before 7 would label black on white as large text only, because 21 is also at least 3. Strictest first is a rule worth remembering.

Why accessibility is a good first project

It has a right answer that can be checked, it uses real arithmetic, and it matters to real people. ICD2O asks students to analyze accessibility issues and identify measures that can improve accessibility, and this program is one such measure.

It also reuses well. Expectation C3.5 asks students to explain their program with “considerations for reuse by others”, and a classmate building a website can call the contrast function without reading how it works.

AI, automation and security

Artificial intelligence and cybersecurity in ICD2O

The ICD2O record names artificial intelligence and cybersecurity in its description, and strand B gives each its own expectation. Here is what the course asks, and how we handle the same topics in class.

What AI can and cannot do

Expectation B4.1 asks students to “investigate current innovations, including automation and artificial intelligence systems, and assess the impacts of these technologies on everyday life”. The assessing is the point.

Bias

Strand A asks students to identify and address bias involving digital technology. A Grade 10 student can already ask what data a system learned from, and who might be missing from it.

Safe practices

B3 covers safe data and security habits: strong passwords, care with links, privacy settings. These are habits, so they are practised, not memorised.

Using AI without letting it think for you

Our teachers explain how AI systems work, in plain terms, and we let students see where they go wrong. What we do not do is let a chatbot write a student’s program. A Grade 10 student who copies working code learns nothing that strand C can assess.

AI chat tools set their own age rules in their terms of use, so families should read those terms before a teen signs up for one. We do not ask students to sign up for any.

A cybersecurity habit to teach at home

If a message asks for a password through a link, do not use the link. Open the real site yourself, by typing its address or using a saved bookmark, and check there. That one habit protects against a very common trick, and it is practice question 8 below.

It also links to strand C: once a student can write a program that checks input, they understand why a website that does not check its input is a security risk.

Practice, ours

Ten ICD2O practice questions, with answers

We wrote these ten for this page; none is from a school, a board or a ministry document. Questions 1 to 6 are short Python programs to read before running, and 7 to 10 ask you to explain or plan. Answers follow the questions.

Question 7 · an everyday algorithm. Write numbered steps for borrowing a book at a self-serve library kiosk. Include at least one decision (an if) and one repeat (a loop).

Question 8 · safe practice. A message that looks like it comes from your school asks you to confirm your password through a link. List three things to do, in order.

Question 9 · benefits and limitations. A school library starts using an automated system that recommends books from what a student borrowed before. Give one benefit and one limitation.

Question 10 · modify a program. Change the contrast checker above so it reports four levels, including the WCAG enhanced level of 7:1. Which test must come first?

Answers, ours

Answers to the ten questions

Each answer explains the reasoning, which is what a teacher marks. All six programs were run in Python 3.13.

  1. 19, then 40. Powers come first, so 2 ** 2 is 4; then multiplication, 3 * 4 is 12; then 7 + 12 is 19. Brackets change the order: (7 + 3) is 10, times 4 is 40. This is the order of operations expectation C1.4 describes.
  2. 42 str, then 6. With text, + joins the two pieces, so "4" + "2" is the text 42, and its type is str. After int() turns each into a number, + adds them. Same symbol, different job depending on the type of data.
  3. Extreme cold warning. -12 is below 0, and also below -10, so the inner if runs. For -5 it prints Below freezing. For exactly 0 it prints Above freezing, which is not quite right: water freezes at 0. Changing the first test to temp <= 0 fixes it, and finding that edge is the real skill.
  4. 8 W g WINNIPEG. len counts the letters, index 0 is the first letter, index -1 counts from the end, and upper() returns a capitalised copy. The original word is unchanged.
  5. 2. The loop looks at each answer once and adds 1 when it is b. There are two b answers. The program counts how many answers match one choice, like marking a quiz against one correct option.
  6. TypeError: can only concatenate str (not "int") to str. Python will not join text and a number with +. Turn the number into text first, with str(score), or use an f-string: f"Score: {score}". Both print Score: 7.
  7. One good answer: 1. Scan your library card. 2. If the card is not accepted, ask staff and stop. 3. Scan a book. 4. Repeat step 3 until every book is scanned. 5. Choose a receipt option. 6. Take your card and books. The if is step 2 and the loop is step 4.
  8. Do not use the link; check another way; report it. Open the school site yourself by typing the address or using a bookmark, and see if any message is waiting there. Never type a password into a page reached from a message. Then tell a teacher or the school’s technology staff, so others are warned.
  9. Benefit: students find books close to what they already enjoy, quickly. Limitation: it can keep suggesting the same kind of book, so a student sees less variety, and it knows nothing about what a student has read outside the library.
  10. The 7:1 test must come first. See the four-level verdict code in the project. Checking from the strictest level down means each ratio stops at the highest level it meets; checking 3 first would stop every passing ratio at large text only.

After Grade 10

After ICD2O: Grade 11 courses and the diploma

ICD2O is an open course, and it is not a prerequisite for anything in the course table: ICS3U and ICS3C list no prerequisite at all. So what does it lead to? Mostly, to readiness.

Why take ICD2O if nothing requires it

Because Grade 11 computer studies moves fast. The first unit of ICS3U or ICS3C assumes nothing, but by the middle of the course students are writing loops over arrays and functions with parameters. A student who met variables, conditions and loops in Grade 10 has room to think about the new ideas instead of the syntax.

And because ICD2O covers things the Grade 11 courses do not dwell on, such as artificial intelligence, cloud storage and bias. Those are useful to every student, whatever they study next.

ICD2O and the diploma

Ontario’s graduation requirements page lists computer studies as one of the subjects in the STEM-related course group for students who started Grade 9 in 2024 or later, and in Group 3 for students who started in 2023 or earlier. ICD2O is a computer studies course on the curriculum website’s list.

That page also requires a credit in technological education for the 2024 cohort. Technological education is a separate subject with its own courses, and we found no source saying that ICD2O counts toward it, so ask the school before planning around that.

Questions about Grade 10

ICD2O and ICS2O: questions families ask

What is ICD2O?

ICD2O is Digital Technology and Innovations in the Changing World, a Grade 10 open computer studies course listed on the current Ontario curriculum website with the issue year 2023. It has three strands: Computational Thinking and Making Connections; Hardware, Software, and Innovations; and Programming.

What happened to ICS2O?

ICS2O, Introduction to Computer Studies, is the Grade 10 open course in the 2008 Computer Studies curriculum document. The current Ontario curriculum website lists ICD2O as its Grade 10 computer studies course and does not list ICS2O. Neither source we read says that one replaced the other.

What is the Grade 10 computer course in Ontario?

On the current Ontario curriculum website, the Grade 10 computer studies course is ICD2O, Digital Technology and Innovations in the Changing World. It is an open course with no prerequisite. Ask your school which Grade 10 courses it offers in a given year.

Does ICD2O have a prerequisite?

No. The course record on the Ontario curriculum website lists no prerequisite for ICD2O.

Do I need ICD2O to take ICS3U?

No. ICS3U and ICS3C list no prerequisite. ICD2O is still a useful first step, because Grade 11 computer studies moves quickly once arrays and functions arrive.

Does ICD2O include coding?

Yes. Strand C, Programming, asks students to explain programming concepts and algorithms, write simple programs with conditions and loops, interpret errors, write subprograms, use libraries and modify existing programs.

Does ICD2O teach artificial intelligence?

It asks students to investigate current and emerging innovations, including automation and artificial intelligence, and to assess their benefits and limitations. It also includes cybersecurity and data practices as an overall expectation of their own.

Which programming language does ICD2O use?

The expectations do not name a language, so it depends on the class. We teach in Python unless the student's class uses something else.

Does ICD2O count for the diploma?

Ontario's graduation requirements page lists computer studies in the STEM-related course group for students who started Grade 9 in 2024 or later. We found no source saying ICD2O counts as the technological education credit, so check with the school.

Do you grant the ICD2O credit?

No. We teach coding beside the school course and do not grant Ontario credits. The credit comes from the school or credit provider that teaches and evaluates ICD2O.

When are your classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Times are set in your own Ontario time zone.

How much do classes cost?

The first class is free. After that, families pay a monthly fee and can stop when any month ends. The fees section on this page shows the current figure.
