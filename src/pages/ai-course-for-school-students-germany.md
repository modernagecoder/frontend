## Do pupils in Germany learn AI at school?

Pupils meet AI as a topic in many subjects, but building a model is computer science, and how many pupils take computer science in the upper school differs enormously between Länder.

### Using AI is now in the framework

In 2026 the KMK, the conference of the Länder education ministers, adopted an update of its competence framework Bildung in der digitalen Welt that adds competences related to artificial intelligence. That covers how pupils use, question and judge AI across their subjects.

It does not mean that a pupil writes code. A history lesson that asks whether a chatbot answer is reliable is part of that framework, and so is a German lesson on AI-written text.

### Building AI depends on Informatik

The Gesellschaft für Informatik counts, in its Informatik-Monitor 2026/27, how many pupils take Informatik in the Qualifikationsphase of the Gymnasium upper school. Across Germany the average is about 15 percent.

## Which school maths do you need to understand AI?

Far less new maths than people fear. The three areas of the German Abitur maths exam are the same three areas that machine learning is built from.

The IQB, the Länder institute that coordinates the shared Abitur task pools, describes the structure of the maths exam in its own paper on the pool tasks: a Part A without aids and a Part B where aids are allowed, and both parts contain tasks on each of Analysis, Analytische Geometrie/Lineare Algebra and Stochastik. Shared pools for maths have existed since the 2017 exam year. A pupil in the Oberstufe therefore spends two years on precisely the topics our course leans on, usually without hearing what they are for outside school.

**The three Abitur maths areas and the AI idea each one explains (our mapping)**

| Abitur area | What the pupil does at school | What it does in machine learning |
|---|---|---|
| Analysis | Derivatives, slopes, finding a minimum | Gradient descent: walk downhill on the error until it is as small as it gets |
| Analytische Geometrie / Lineare Algebra | Vectors, lines, planes, dot products | Every input becomes a vector; a simple model is a weighted sum, a dot product |
| Stochastik | Probability, expected value, distributions | Judging a model: how often is it right, and how sure should we be? |

Source for the three areas: IQB, Beschreibung der Struktur der Aufgaben für die Abiturprüfung im Fach Mathematik, and the IQB Abitur page on the shared pools. The right-hand column is our own teaching map, not an IQB statement.

### For pupils in Klasse 7 to 10

A younger pupil has not met derivatives yet, and does not need to. We start with what they know: a line through points, an average, a percentage. The idea that a model guesses, measures how wrong it is and adjusts can be shown with a slope and a ruler long before the word derivative appears.

When the derivative arrives in Klasse 10 or 11, it has an obvious use already waiting.

### For pupils in the Oberstufe

An Oberstufe pupil can follow every line of the example below on paper. The two partial derivatives in it are a small step beyond the school course, and we take that step slowly, in English, with the German words beside the English ones where it helps: Ableitung, Steigung, Tiefpunkt.

Pupils often tell us that their maths lessons make more sense once they have watched a model use them.

## How does a model learn a rule nobody told it?

Give a program pairs of temperatures in Celsius and Fahrenheit and nothing else. It starts by guessing zero for everything, and by following the slope of its own error it finds the rule on its own. How fast it gets there depends on one number: the step size.

### What the program does

The model is a straight line, prediction = w times Celsius + b. At the start w and b are both zero, so every prediction is zero and the average squared error, the loss, is 3,290.7.

At each step the program works out the two partial derivatives of the loss, which say in which direction w and b should move to make the error smaller, and moves them a little that way. The size of that move is the learning rate.

### Why this is the heart of AI

Large language models and image recognisers have billions of numbers instead of two, but they are trained by the same idea: measure the error, take its derivative, step downhill, repeat.

Every engineer who trains a model has to choose a step size, and a bad choice wastes days or ruins the run. A pupil can see both failures here in a second.

**Version 1: the same model, three step sizes, 5,000 steps each** (Python)
```python
# Celsius readings and the Fahrenheit values that go with them (our own example data)
celsius = [-10, -3, 0, 4, 9, 15, 21, 28, 34]
fahrenheit = [c * 9 / 5 + 32 for c in celsius]
n = len(celsius)

def loss(w, b):
    return sum((w * c + b - f) ** 2 for c, f in zip(celsius, fahrenheit)) / n

def step(w, b, rate):
    # the two partial derivatives of the loss, straight from Oberstufe Analysis
    dw = sum(2 * (w * c + b - f) * c for c, f in zip(celsius, fahrenheit)) / n
    db = sum(2 * (w * c + b - f) for c, f in zip(celsius, fahrenheit)) / n
    return w - rate * dw, b - rate * db

for rate in [0.0001, 0.001, 0.003]:
    w, b = 0.0, 0.0
    for _ in range(5000):
        w, b = step(w, b, rate)
    print("rate", rate, " w =", round(w, 3), " b =", round(b, 3), " loss =", f"{loss(w, b):.3g}")
```
It prints rate 0.0001 w = 2.399 b = 14.837 loss = 183, then rate 0.001 w = 1.802 b = 31.935 loss = 0.00259 and rate 0.003 w = 1.8 b = 32.0 loss = 4.18e-14.

The true rule is Fahrenheit = 1.8 times Celsius + 32, and nobody typed it in. With the smallest step the model is still far off after 5,000 steps: it has found a slope near 2.4 and an offset near 15, a line that is roughly right in the middle of the data and wrong at the ends. With a step ten times larger it is almost there. With 0.003 it has found 1.8 and 32 to the last digit shown. So is a bigger step always better? Version 2 answers that.

**Version 2: one step size that works, one that is slightly too big** (Python)
```python
for rate in [0.003, 0.005]:
    w, b = 0.0, 0.0
    history = []
    for _ in range(6):
        w, b = step(w, b, rate)
        history.append(f"{loss(w, b):.0f}")
    print("rate", rate, " loss after steps 1 to 6:", ", ".join(history))
```
Run after Version 1. It prints rate 0.003 loss after steps 1 to 6: 2672, 2195, 1827, 1543, 1324, 1154 and rate 0.005 loss after steps 1 to 6: 12669, 55174, 247728, 1119912, 5070438, 22964118.

**How the step size changes the training (our calculation, starting loss 3,290.7)**

| Learning rate | Steps until the loss is under 0.01 | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0001 | 44,584 | Learns, but very slowly |
| 0.001 | 4,456 | Learns steadily |
| 0.003 | 1,484 | Learns fastest of the three |
| 0.005 | Never | Each step overshoots; the loss grows about four to five times per step |

The data, the code and every number in this section are our own, run with Python 3 on 3 November 2026. The temperatures are a teaching example, not measurements.

Between 0.003 and 0.005 lies a cliff. Our calculation puts the edge at about 0.0032: beyond that, every step jumps across the valley of the error and lands higher up the other side, and the next step jumps further. The edge is set by how steeply the loss curves, and the Celsius values up to 34 make it curve steeply in the w direction. That is why engineers rescale their inputs before training, and why a pupil who understands a second derivative understands a real training problem. We work through that argument with Oberstufe pupils on paper after they have watched it happen on screen.

1. **Predict first.** Before running Version 1, write down which of the three rates you expect to win. Then run it.
2. **Find the cliff.** Try 0.0031, 0.0032 and 0.0033 in Version 2 and describe what changes in the six losses.
3. **Rescale.** Divide every Celsius value by 10 before training, and adjust the final w. How large can the rate be now?
4. **Going further.** Add a little random noise to the Fahrenheit values. The model can no longer reach zero loss. What does it reach instead, and why?

## Which AI competitions can school students in Germany enter?

Three established routes accept a pupil's own AI work. They differ in age, in what they want to see and in how you enter.

**Routes for a school student's AI project in Germany, from the organisers' own pages**

| Route | Who can enter | What is handed in | Dates and entry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bundeswettbewerb Künstliche Intelligenz (BWKI), Tübingen AI Center | Pupils in Sekundarstufe I and II, alone or in a team | Written documentation, runnable code and a video pitch; German or English | 2026 round: registration from 1 March 2026, projects due 20 September 2026, final 13 November 2026 |
| Jugend forscht, field Mathematik/Informatik | Ages 15 to 21; Jugend forscht junior up to 14, from Klasse 4 | A written research report, then a stand at the contest | Online registration by 30 November; the site already lists the Bundeswettbewerb 2027 |
| Dr. Hans Riegel-Fachpreise | Pupils shortly before the Abitur or Matura | A final paper with its focus in subjects including Mathematik or Informatik | Run separately by each partner university for its region |

Sources: bw-ki.de (Teilnahmebedingungen and the 2026 timeline); jugend-forscht.de (age groups, the timeline at a glance, the Mathematik/Informatik field); hans-riegel-fachpreise.com (competition page and checklist). Read on 3 November 2026.

### What each one rewards

The BWKI wants a machine-learning method at the centre and says so in its conditions. Jugend forscht is a research competition: the question and how carefully it is answered matter more than the size of the model. The Riegel prizes reward a written school paper, such as a Facharbeit or seminar paper, judged by a university; the organisers say about 300 prize winners are honoured each year.

The BWKI also runs an award for schools, KI-Schule des Jahres, whose new round started on 1 August 2026.

### What we do and do not do

We are not connected with the Tübingen AI Center, Jugend forscht, the Dr. Hans Riegel-Stiftung or any school, and we never enter work on a pupil's behalf. The project and the entry are the pupil's own.

What we teach is the craft underneath: a precise question, data the pupil is allowed to use, a model they can explain line by line, an honest test, and a write-up a jury can follow. A pupil who can explain the step-size table above to a stranger is well on the way.

## AI course for school students in Germany: questions

### Are the classes in German?

No. All classes are live and in English only, with a mentor based in India who matches the pupil's pace and accent. Python itself uses English keywords, so the code looks the same whatever language a pupil thinks in.

### Is my child's English good enough?

Usually yes. Most of the lesson is code on a shared screen, and the mentor speaks slowly, repeats in simpler words and waits for answers. Pupils at German schools often find their English improves as a side effect.

### What age is the course for?

For pupils aged 13 to 19, from about Klasse 7 to the Abitur year. Younger children can start with our AI literacy course for ages 8 to 14.

### Does my child need Informatik at school?

No. According to the Informatik-Monitor 2026/27, only about 15 percent of upper-school pupils across Germany take Informatik in the Qualifikationsphase. The course starts from Python basics for anyone who has not programmed.

### Does the course help with the Abitur?

It uses the same maths, Analysis, geometry and Stochastik, and many pupils say school maths makes more sense afterwards. It does not replace school and awards no German grade or qualification; the school course is the one that counts.

### Can my child enter the BWKI or Jugend forscht?

Yes, if they meet the organisers' conditions. The BWKI accepts projects in German or English from Sekundarstufe I and II, and Jugend forscht has a Mathematik/Informatik field for ages 15 to 21, with a junior section up to 14. We are not connected with either and do not enter work for pupils.

### Will my child use ChatGPT or Claude?

Not as part of the course: pupils write their models in Python. Anthropic's terms put Claude at 18 or over, so we never use it with anyone under 18. Rules for other tools are quoted in our AI tools age guide.

### When are the classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. In German time that usually means after school or on weekend mornings.

### How are fees paid?

### Group or one to one?

A group of fifteen to twenty pupils suits a steady pace and costs less. One to one suits a pupil with a competition deadline, exam weeks to plan around or a project of their own.
