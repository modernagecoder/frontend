# WA GATE ASET maths practice

In Western Australia, academic entry to the Gifted and Talented Secondary Selective Entrance programs begins in Year 5, with testing in Year 6 and placement from Year 7. Academic applicants sit one test: the Academic Selective Entrance Test, or ASET. Its number-based part, Quantitative Reasoning, is 35 multiple-choice questions in 35 minutes. This page explains it from the department's ASET Handbook, quotes the department's view on coaching, and practises a kind of reasoning that suits it well: growing matchstick patterns, where the job is to find the rule. There is no program-choice or admissions advice here, and no promise of a result.

## Not a school maths test, by design

The handbook is explicit about what this test is and is not.

**From the ASET Handbook** "This test assesses quantitative reasoning ability, which is very different to a test of school achievement in mathematics or science." It checks how well a student can use mathematical and scientific reasoning to solve problems, with content drawn from many sources and "very different to what is taught in school".

The handbook asks students to look at information carefully, find patterns and relationships, solve problems, and spot similarities and differences. Rough working is allowed in the question booklet and on the back of the answer booklet, but only shaded answers are marked.

On its testing page the department also states: "We do not endorse or recommend any form of private professional coaching or tutoring". It suggests reading the handbook and trying the Practice ASET so students know what to expect.

We take that seriously. A test designed to identify potential rather than acquired knowledge is not something to drill. What does help is a child who enjoys spotting patterns, explains rules in words, and checks a rule against every case.

## Matchstick growing patterns

Shapes made of matchsticks grow one step at a time. The challenge is to find how many sticks each step adds, write a rule, and use the rule for steps far too large to draw.

### Count the first few

Draw steps 1, 2 and 3 and count the sticks. Write the counts in a row.

### Find what is added

The difference between counts tells you how many sticks each new piece needs, after the first piece is built.

### Write the rule, then test it

A rule such as 3 times the step, plus 1, must work for every step you drew, not just one.

**Original matchstick patterns with rules**

| Pattern | First counts | Rule for step n | Question and answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squares joined in a row | 4, 7, 10 | 3n + 1 | Step 10 needs 31 sticks |
| Triangles joined in a row, alternately up and down | 3, 5, 7 | 2n + 1 | Step 20 needs 41 sticks |
| Squares in a row: which step uses exactly 100 sticks? | 4, 7, 10 | 3n + 1 = 100 | Step 33 |
| Hexagons joined in a row | 6, 11, 16 | 5n + 1 | Step 8 needs 41 sticks |
| A pattern starting 5, 9, 13 | 5, 9, 13 | 4n + 1 | Step 15 needs 61 sticks |
| Square grids: 1 by 1, 2 by 2, 3 by 3 | 4, 12, 24 | 2n(n + 1) | A 3 by 3 grid needs 24 sticks |

The first pattern explains why so many rules end in plus 1. The first square needs 4 sticks, but every square after it shares a side with the one before, so it adds only 3. Four sticks is really 1 plus 3, which is where 3n + 1 comes from. A student who can explain the plus 1 has understood the pattern, not just matched numbers.

The last pattern is different, and that is the point. The counts 4, 12, 24 do not go up by the same amount each time, so no rule of the form "times something, plus 1" fits. Counting horizontal and vertical sticks separately in a 3 by 3 grid gives 4 rows of 3 and 4 columns of 3, which is 12 plus 12. Checking a rule on every step catches the moment a pattern stops being simple.

## Abstract Reasoning: 35 questions in 20 minutes

The fourth ASET test is faster still, and the handbook describes it in terms that link closely to pattern work.

### What the handbook says

Abstract Reasoning assesses the ability to think about ideas and patterns that are not always obvious, to recognise connections, make reasoned choices and decide whether they make sense. It has 35 multiple-choice questions to complete in 20 minutes.

The handbook names a question type called Next in Sequence, in which a student identifies the rule for a sequence and chooses the option that most logically and simply comes next.

### Why pattern habits transfer

Finding what changes from one step to the next, writing it as a rule and testing that rule on every step is the same habit whether the steps are matchstick shapes or abstract figures.

Twenty minutes for 35 questions is a little over half a minute each, so a student who has practised looking for one change at a time, rather than everything at once, will feel far calmer.

Try the hexagon pattern from the table with this in mind. One hexagon needs 6 sticks. Each new hexagon shares one side with its neighbour, so it adds 5. Step 8 therefore needs 6 plus 7 lots of 5, which is 41, and the rule 5n + 1 says the same thing in a single line. Whether a sequence is made of numbers, sticks or shapes, the question is always: what is added or changed each time, and what was there at the start?

## Pencil, ovals and careful alignment

The ASET is answered on paper, and the handbook spends real care on the answer booklet.

### What the handbook describes

Students receive one answer booklet for all four tests. Each multiple-choice question has four small ovals labelled A, B, C and D, coloured in with a pencil because scanners read pencil marks only.

Students may answer in any order, but the handbook recommends working through questions in order, because that reduces the risk of misaligning answers.

### Habits worth building

Check the question number against the row before colouring an oval, every time.

Use the question booklet for working; markers only see the shaded answers.

If stuck, choose the most sensible option and move on, since wrong answers cost nothing.

## Four stages for young pattern spotters

These develop through puzzles, discussion and good teaching over years.

**From counting to rules**

| Stage | The student can | Secure when |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Continue | Extend a pattern by drawing | Draws step 4 of the square pattern correctly |
| 2. Difference | See what each step adds | Explains why each square adds 3 sticks |
| 3. Rule | Write a rule for step n | Finds step 20 of the triangle pattern without drawing |
| 4. Reverse and test | Work backwards and check rules | Finds which step uses 100 sticks and tests the grid pattern |

## Western Australian families ask

### What is the ASET?

The Academic Selective Entrance Test used by the WA Department of Education for Gifted and Talented Secondary Selective Entrance programs. Academic applicants sit the ASET only.

### When do students sit the ASET?

The department says selection starts in Year 5, with testing in Year 6 and placement from Year 7. For the 2028 intake, the Year 6 ASET in Perth metro, Bunbury and Albany is on Saturday 6 March 2027.

### How long is the Quantitative Reasoning test?

35 minutes for 35 multiple-choice questions, according to the ASET Handbook.

### Is Quantitative Reasoning a maths test?

The handbook says it is very different to a test of school achievement in mathematics or science; it assesses mathematical and scientific reasoning with content unlike what is taught in school.

### Are marks taken off for wrong answers?

No. The handbook says each question is worth one point and no points are deducted for incorrect answers.

### What is a matchstick growing pattern?

A sequence of shapes made from matchsticks, where each step adds pieces. Students find how many sticks each step adds and write a rule, such as 3n + 1, for any step.

The department says it does not endorse or recommend private professional coaching or tutoring, and suggests the ASET Handbook and the Practice ASET instead.
