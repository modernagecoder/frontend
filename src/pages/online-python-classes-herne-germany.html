Live, not recorded

What happens in a live online Python class?

People in Herne often ask us whether "online" means a course of videos. It does not. Here is one ordinary hour, step by step.

  1. Minutes 0 to 5: the warm-up. The mentor opens the video call, greets each learner by name and puts a two-line puzzle on the shared screen. Nobody has to speak first; anyone can type a guess in the chat.
  2. Minutes 5 to 20: one new idea. The mentor writes a short program live, explains one new thing (a loop, a dictionary, reading a file) and runs it. Then the program is broken on purpose, and the group says what will go wrong before it does.
  3. Minutes 20 to 45: the learner types. Each learner writes their own version in their own editor and shares the screen when stuck. The mentor reads the code line by line with them, asks what they expected and lets them find the mistake.
  4. Minutes 45 to 55: show and explain. Two or three learners show a working program and say in their own words why it works. Explaining is where most of the learning settles.
  5. Minutes 55 to 60: what comes next. A small task for the days before the next class, and for younger learners a short note to parents on what was built.

English, at your speed

Lessons are taught in English only. The mentor slows down, rephrases and checks understanding, and quickly learns how each learner speaks. A Herne pupil whose English is still growing practises it for real every class, around code that looks the same in any language.

Thinking before typing

We teach learners to plan a program in plain words first and to predict its output before running it. Copying a finished answer, from a classmate or from an AI tool, is never the goal; being able to explain every line is.

What you need at home

A laptop or desktop computer, a stable connection and a free Python editor that the mentor helps you install in the first class. A tablet is fine for watching but awkward for typing, so we suggest a keyboard from the start.

The mentors work from India over a live video link, so the slots further down land in Herne afternoons and at weekends. In a group the same mentor stays with the batch; one-to-one learners keep the same mentor too, so the person reading your code next month already knows how you think.

Why typed Python, not blocks

For learners of 9 and over we start with typed Python rather than block coding. Typing is slower at first, but every error message is a small lesson in reading carefully, and the habit of reading carefully is what separates someone who can code from someone who can follow a tutorial.

Younger children who are not yet comfortable on a keyboard usually do better with a Scratch course first; the free first class shows which suits.

Why a mentor, not only exercises

Most adults who try Python alone stop at the same place: the first program that works but that they cannot explain. A mentor who asks "what will this print?" before you press Run turns that point into the start of real understanding.

For teenagers the same questions are the ones that matter in Informatik at school, where the written Abitur in NRW is done without a computer.

A real Herne project

How many Herne pit shafts were working at the same time?

Herne grew on coal. The city publishes its list of historic colliery shafts as open data, free to reuse, and the years each one worked. That is enough for a proper Python question with a surprising answer and a lesson about order.

The data

The dataset is called Historische Zechenstandorte and comes from the City of Herne's geodata service, listed on GovData under the licence Datenlizenz Deutschland Zero 2.0. We downloaded it on 3 November 2026: 80 shaft sites, each with a name such as Shamrock 4 or Mont Cenis 2 "Carl", a year it started working and a year it stopped.

One site, Mont Cenis 5, has no years at all. The other 79 have both. Sixteen names end in Wetterschacht: shafts that carried fresh air into the mine rather than coal out of it.

The question

In which year were the most shafts at work at the same time, and how many? A natural guess is the 1950s, the decade people often picture as the height of coal. The program disagrees.

We turn the GeoJSON into a small file, herne_shafts.csv, with three columns: name, opened, closed. Version 1 does the obvious thing: for every year from 1850 to 2000 it checks every shaft.

The peak is 1930 and 1931, with 75 of the 79 shafts working, not the 1950s. By 1950 the count had already fallen to 68. Version 1 is correct but wasteful: it makes 151 passes over the list, one per year, and most years nothing changes. A professional would ask a different question: when does the count change at all?

The count changes only when a shaft opens or closes. So Version 2 turns each shaft into two events: plus one in the year it opened, and minus one in the year after it closed, because a shaft that closed in 1967 was still working during 1967. Sort the events, walk through them once with a running total, and keep the highest value seen.

The answers agree, which is the first thing to check whenever you replace a slow program with a clever one. The hidden detail is the sort. Python sorts the pairs by year first and then by the second number, so in any year a closing event, minus one, comes before an opening event, plus one. Had the openings come first, the running total would briefly count a shaft that had already stopped, and in a different dataset the peak could be one too high. Deciding how ties sort is part of the program, not a detail.

  1. Ages 9 to 11. Take ten shafts from the table on paper, draw each as a bar on a timeline, and count the bars over 1930. Then over 1970.
  2. Ages 12 to 15. Run Version 1, then change it to print the year with the most closures. Predict the answer from the source note before you run it.
  3. Ages 16 and up. Swap the minus one and plus one so openings sort first, and find out whether the peak changes on this data. Then build a tiny test file where it does.
  4. Adults. Time both versions on a list of 80,000 made-up shafts and explain the difference in terms of how many steps each one takes.

When do online Python classes run in Herne time?

Herne keeps German time, which changes twice a year; India does not. The table shows what that means for a class time.

Example Herne times and the mentor's clock in India

Monday 14:4519:1518:15
Thursday 16:1520:4519:45
Sunday 10:1514:4513:45

German law (the Einheitenzeitgesetz) sets MEZ at UTC+1 for winter and MESZ at UTC+2 for summer. The clocks went back on 25 October 2026 and go forward again on 28 March 2027. Indian clocks stay at UTC+5:30 all year: a gap of four and a half hours to Herne in winter, three and a half in summer. The rows are examples only; no batch time is fixed here.

When you book, the times you see are already in your own clock. Adults in Herne often prefer an early evening or a weekend morning; school pupils usually take a slot after lessons end.

Online Python classes in Herne: questions and answers

Are the classes in German?

No. We teach only in English, always live. Your mentor keeps a clear, steady speed, gets used to your accent and puts a hard sentence another way when needed. Python keywords are English words anyway, so the code itself does not change.

Is an online Python class just a set of videos?

No. Each class is a live video call with a mentor who watches you write code, answers questions as they come up and asks you to explain your program. Nothing replaces the live lesson.

What age can someone in Herne start Python with you?

Children usually start typed Python from about 9. Younger children often begin with Scratch first. There is no upper age limit: many of our Python learners are adults.

Does Python help with Informatik at a Herne school?

It can. NRW teaches compulsory Informatik in grades 5 and 6, and later as an elective or Oberstufe course. Our classes teach alongside school, and the school course and its teacher remain what counts for grades.

When are classes held in Herne time?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Pupils often choose an afternoon slot, adults an early evening or a weekend morning.

Where are the mentors based?

In India. They teach you live over video. Indian clocks run four and a half hours ahead of Herne in winter and three and a half in summer, so a Herne afternoon is an Indian evening.

What computer do I need?

A laptop or desktop with a keyboard, a stable internet connection and a free Python editor. The mentor helps you install it in the first class.

How big are the groups?

A batch holds fifteen to twenty learners at a similar level. Prefer a mentor of your own? Then choose one to one: two private lessons each week, at hours you pick.

How are fees charged?

Once a month. This page lists the fee in euro using the reference rate of the European Central Bank; the charge is made in US dollars and converted by your own bank, so a small difference on the statement is normal. Your first lesson costs nothing.

Do you use AI tools to write the code?

Learners write their own code. We teach how to think through a problem and explain every line, not how to copy an answer from a person or a chatbot. Adults later learn to check AI-written code with tests.
