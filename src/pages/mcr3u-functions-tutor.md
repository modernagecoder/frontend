## What Grade 11 Functions covers

### Small phrases that change what is tested

The first specific expectation asks students to &ldquo;explain the meaning of the term function&rdquo;, not just use one. Expect a test question that shows a relation as a table or a mapping diagram and asks for a reason, in words, why it is or is not a function.

Inverses are tied to an idea students already have. The curriculum asks them to &ldquo;relate the process of determining the inverse of a function to their understanding of reverse processes&rdquo;: undoing each operation in reverse order. Our first worked problem uses exactly that framing.

### Algebra that runs through every unit

Strand A&rsquo;s algebra expectations carry a footnote: the skills &ldquo;are to be introduced as needed, and applied and consolidated, as appropriate, in solving problems throughout the course.&rdquo; In practice, factoring and rational expressions are not one unit that ends; they are tested all year.

Rational expressions come with a specific demand: students simplify them and &ldquo;state the restrictions on the variable values&rdquo;. A correct simplification with the restrictions missing loses marks on almost every Ontario test we see.

### Why the course exists

According to the programme overview, Functions &ldquo;builds on the concepts and skills developed in the Grade 9 and 10 academic mathematics courses&rdquo; and prepares students for Grade 12 courses leading to university programs, &ldquo;including science, engineering, social sciences, liberal arts, and education.&rdquo;

That range is the point. MCR3U is not only for future engineers; it is the general-purpose university math course of Grade 11.

### Prerequisite and credit

The course page gives &ldquo;Prerequisite: Principles of Mathematics, Grade 10, Academic&rdquo;, which is MPM2D, and the ministry&rsquo;s current online record carries the same prerequisite and the year 2007.

Functions is a one-credit course. Ontario&rsquo;s diploma rules ask for three math credits, one of them in Grade 11 or 12, so for many students MCR3U is also the course that completes the math requirement for graduation.

## Where Grade 11 Functions students lose marks

These are patterns from our own MCR3U classes, written as a table so a student can check their last test against it. Each comes with the habit that fixes it.

**Frequent MCR3U errors and the fix for each (ours)**

| Topic | What goes wrong | The habit that fixes it |
|---|---|---|
| Function notation | Reading f&minus;1(x) as 1/f(x) | Say &ldquo;f inverse&rdquo; aloud; the reciprocal is written 1/f(x) |
| Domain and range | Giving the domain of a quadratic as its x-intercepts | Ask which inputs are allowed, not where the graph crosses an axis |
| Transformations | Applying a horizontal stretch and shift to y = f(2x &minus; 6) without factoring | Factor first: f(2(x &minus; 3)), so the shift is 3, not 6 |
| Quadratics | Completing the square and losing the factor in front | Factor the leading coefficient out of both x terms before adding anything |
| Rational expressions | Cancelling terms instead of factors | Only whole factors cancel; write restrictions before cancelling |
| Exponents | Treating 82/3 as 8 &times; 2/3 | Root first, then power: the cube root of 8 is 2, squared is 4 |
| Sequences | Mixing up the recursive and the general term | Recursive uses the previous term; general uses n |
| Sine law | Giving one triangle when two exist | Compare a with b sin A every time the angle is not between the given sides |

### The jump from Grade 10

Grade 10 students work with relations: graphs and equations, mostly of lines and parabolas. Grade 11 asks them to step back and talk about the object itself: what a function is, what its domain is, what its inverse does. Students who were strong at Grade 10 calculation can stumble here because the questions now ask for explanation.

We spend the first weeks making students say things in sentences: &ldquo;this is a function because each input has exactly one output&rdquo;. It feels slow, and it pays back through the whole year.

### Why trigonometry lands at the end

Trigonometry is strand D, the last of the four in the curriculum, and a school that follows that order reaches it right before the exam. It is also the strand with the most new vocabulary: reference angles, the CAST rule, the ambiguous case, period, amplitude, phase shift. Students meet it tired and with the least time to practise.

Starting trigonometry practice early, even ten minutes a week, changes the final exam more than any other single adjustment we suggest.

## An inverse function and an ambiguous-case triangle, worked out

Two problems we wrote, both at MCR3U test level: one from the first strand, one from the last. Each step states the reason for the step. Computer algebra confirmed the results.

Problem 1, an inverse. Let f(x) = 2(x &minus; 3)2 + 1 with domain x &ge; 3. Find f&minus;1(x), state its domain and range, and check your answer.

1. Ask why the domain is restricted. A full parabola fails the horizontal line test: two inputs share each output, so its inverse would not be a function. Keeping only x &ge; 3, the right half from the vertex, makes f one-to-one.
2. List what f does, in order. Subtract 3, square, multiply by 2, add 1. The inverse must undo these in reverse order: subtract 1, divide by 2, take the square root, add 3.
3. Write that as a formula. f&minus;1(x) = 3 + &radic;((x &minus; 1)/2). Only the positive root is used, because the outputs of the inverse are the inputs of f, and those are all at least 3.
4. Swap domain and range. f has domain x &ge; 3 and range y &ge; 1, because the vertex is (3, 1) and the parabola opens up. So f&minus;1 has domain x &ge; 1 and range y &ge; 3.
5. Check with a number. f(5) = 2(2)2 + 1 = 9. Then f&minus;1(9) = 3 + &radic;(8/2) = 3 + 2 = 5. The inverse returns the original input.
6. Check with the graph. The inverse is the reflection of f in the line y = x. The point (5, 9) on f becomes (9, 5) on the inverse, and the vertex (3, 1) becomes the starting point (1, 3).

The swap-x-and-y method gives the same answer, but students who use it mechanically often keep the &plusmn; sign and never decide between roots. The reverse-process method forces that decision, because it asks what the original inputs were.

Problem 2, the ambiguous case. In triangle ABC, angle A = 40&deg;, side a = 8 cm (opposite A) and side b = 10 cm. Find every possible value of angle B and side c, to two decimal places.

1. Notice the warning sign. The given angle is not between the two given sides. That is the side-side-angle situation, where zero, one or two triangles can exist.
2. Count the triangles before solving. The height from C to the line AB is b sin A = 10 sin 40&deg;, about 6.43 cm. Side a is 8 cm: longer than the height but shorter than b. So a can swing to two positions, and there are two triangles.
3. Use the sine law for B. sin B / 10 = sin 40&deg; / 8, so sin B = 10 sin 40&deg; / 8, about 0.8035.
4. Find both angles with that sine. The calculator gives B = 53.46&deg;. Sine is also positive in the second quadrant, so B = 180&deg; &minus; 53.46&deg; = 126.54&deg; is the other candidate. Both are allowed because 40&deg; + 126.54&deg; is still less than 180&deg;.
5. Finish each triangle. If B = 53.46&deg;, then C = 86.54&deg; and c = 8 sin C / sin 40&deg;, about 12.42 cm. If B = 126.54&deg;, then C = 13.46&deg; and c is about 2.90 cm.
6. Report both. The answer is two triangles: B is about 53.46&deg; with c about 12.42 cm, or B is about 126.54&deg; with c about 2.90 cm.

**How many triangles? The side-side-angle test when angle A is acute (ours)**

| If side a is | Triangles | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| shorter than b sin A | None | a cannot reach the third side |
| equal to b sin A | One (a right triangle) | a just touches the third side |
| between b sin A and b | Two | a can lean two ways, as in problem 2 |
| at least as long as b | One | the second position would put the angle on the wrong side |

### The common thread

In both problems the risky moment is a choice between two answers: the positive or negative root in the first, the acute or obtuse angle in the second. A student who knows where that choice hides stops losing marks to it.

The checks also match. A number in, a number back out for the inverse; the angle sum and the height comparison for the triangle. A check is part of the solution, not decoration.

### How to practise these

For inverses, practise with linear, quadratic and simple square root functions, always writing the domain restriction first. For the ambiguous case, practise the height comparison on its own until it is automatic, then add the solving.

## Twelve MCR3U practice questions

We wrote these twelve questions for this page, three per strand, and checked each with a computer. They are not from any textbook, school or board. Use degrees throughout, as MCR3U does.

1. Functions. The relation {(1, 4), (2, 5), (3, 4), (1, 6)} is given. Is it a function? Explain in one sentence.
2. Domain and range. State the domain and range of y = &minus;(x + 1)2 + 4.
3. Inverses. Find the inverse of f(x) = 3x &minus; 5 and check it with one number.
4. Quadratics. Write f(x) = &minus;2x2 + 12x &minus; 7 in vertex form and give its maximum value.
5. Number of zeros. How many zeros does 3x2 &minus; 4x + 2 have? Use the discriminant.
6. Rational expressions. Simplify (x2 &minus; 9)/(x2 + x &minus; 6) &times; (x &minus; 2)/(x + 3) and state the restrictions.
7. Rational exponents. Evaluate 272/3 &times; 16&minus;1/2 without a calculator.
8. Exponential models. A table shows x = 0, 1, 2, 3 and y = 5, 15, 45, 135. Write the function and find y when x = 6.
9. Recursive sequences. t1 = 4 and tn = 2tn&minus;1 &minus; 3. Write the first five terms.
10. Series. Find the sum of the first 20 terms of 7 + 11 + 15 + &hellip;
11. Exact values. Find the exact value of sin 120&deg; &times; cos 315&deg; &times; tan 225&deg;.
12. Sinusoidal functions. For y = 2 sin(3(x &minus; 30&deg;)) &minus; 1, state the amplitude, period, phase shift and range.

## Answers, each with its reason

The reasons are the part to study. An MCR3U marker looks for them, and they are what transfers to the next question.

1. Not a function. The input 1 is paired with two different outputs, 4 and 6. Repeated outputs (4 appears twice) are fine; repeated inputs with different outputs are not.
2. Domain: all real numbers. Range: y &le; 4. Any x can be squared. The parabola opens down from its vertex (&minus;1, 4), so 4 is the largest output.
3. f&minus;1(x) = (x + 5)/3. Undo in reverse: add 5, then divide by 3. Check: f(2) = 1, and f&minus;1(1) = 6/3 = 2.
4. f(x) = &minus;2(x &minus; 3)2 + 11, maximum 11. Factor &minus;2 from the x terms: &minus;2(x2 &minus; 6x) &minus; 7. Add and subtract 9 inside: &minus;2((x &minus; 3)2 &minus; 9) &minus; 7 = &minus;2(x &minus; 3)2 + 18 &minus; 7.
5. None. The discriminant is (&minus;4)2 &minus; 4(3)(2) = 16 &minus; 24 = &minus;8. A negative discriminant means the parabola never meets the x-axis.
6. (x &minus; 3)/(x + 3), with x &ne; &minus;3 and x &ne; 2. Factor: (x &minus; 3)(x + 3) over (x + 3)(x &minus; 2), times (x &minus; 2) over (x + 3). One (x + 3) and the (x &minus; 2) cancel. The restrictions come from every original denominator, including the factors that cancelled.
7. 9/4. 272/3: the cube root of 27 is 3, squared is 9. 16&minus;1/2: the square root of 16 is 4, and the negative exponent makes it 1/4. 9 &times; 1/4 = 9/4.
8. y = 5(3)x; when x = 6, y = 3645. Each y is 3 times the last, a constant ratio, so the function is exponential with base 3 and starting value 5. 36 = 729 and 5 &times; 729 = 3645.
9. 4, 5, 7, 11, 19. Double the previous term and subtract 3 each time: 2(4) &minus; 3 = 5, 2(5) &minus; 3 = 7, and so on.
10. 900. The common difference is 4, so t20 = 7 + 19(4) = 83. The sum is 20(7 + 83)/2 = 900.
11. &radic;6/4. sin 120&deg; = &radic;3/2 (second quadrant, positive). cos 315&deg; = &radic;2/2 (fourth quadrant, positive). tan 225&deg; = 1 (third quadrant, positive). The product is &radic;6/4.
12. Amplitude 2, period 120&deg;, phase shift 30&deg; to the right, range from &minus;3 to 1. The period is 360&deg; divided by 3. The centre line is y = &minus;1, so the graph reaches 2 above and 2 below it.

## MCR3U: questions families ask

### What is MCR3U?

MCR3U is Functions, Grade 11, University Preparation, in Ontario's 2007 mathematics curriculum for Grades 11 and 12. Its four strands cover characteristics of functions, exponential functions, discrete functions such as sequences and series, and trigonometric functions.

### Do you grant the MCR3U credit?

No. We teach alongside the student's school course and do not grant Ontario credits. The credit comes from the school or program that teaches and evaluates MCR3U. Our classes give the explanations and practice around it.

### What is the prerequisite for MCR3U?

Principles of Mathematics, Grade 10, Academic, which is MPM2D. That is the prerequisite printed on the MCR3U page of the 2007 curriculum, and the ministry's current online record shows the same.

### Which Grade 12 courses need MCR3U?

Advanced Functions (MHF4U) lists Functions, Grade 11, or the college course Mathematics for College Technology as its prerequisite. Data Management (MDM4U) and Mathematics for College Technology (MCT4C) both accept Functions. Calculus and Vectors needs Advanced Functions, so it depends on MCR3U too.

### Is MCR3U hard?

It is a real step up from Grade 10, mostly because it asks students to explain, not just calculate: what a function is, why an inverse needs a restricted domain, why a triangle has two solutions. Those are skills that improve with practice. We never promise a particular mark.

### Does MCR3U count toward the diploma?

Yes. MCR3U is one credit, and the Ontario graduation requirements include three math credits with at least one in Grade 11 or 12. For many students, MCR3U is the course that meets that Grade 11 or 12 requirement.

### What should my teen review for the MCR3U final exam?

All four strands, with extra time on trigonometry, because it is the last strand in the curriculum's order. Our checklist on this page lists ten skills to test without notes, from inverses and rational expressions to the ambiguous case and sinusoidal graphs.

### Can you help a student retaking MCR3U?

Yes. A retake is a good time for a second teacher, because the lost marks from the first attempt show exactly where to start. We work alongside whatever school or summer program is delivering the course.

### When do classes take place?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Most Ontario families book in Eastern time, and the clocks change on 1 November 2026 and 14 March 2027.
