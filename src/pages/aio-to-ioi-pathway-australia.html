AIO to IOI pathway in Australia

For a small number of Australian students each year, the Australian Informatics Olympiad is the first step of a much longer road. The Australian Maths Trust invites strong performers into its olympiad programs, a sequence of invitational contests, mentoring and camps from which Australia's team for the International Olympiad in Informatics is chosen. Very few students reach the end, and nobody can promise a place. What families can do is understand the steps, the calendar and the rules, so that a talented student knows what is coming. This page sets those out from the Trust's own pages and 2026 calendar, then teaches the skill that separates the later stages from the AIO: working out whether an algorithm is fast enough before writing it.

Students who do well in the Australian Informatics Olympiad may be invited into the Australian Maths Trust's olympiad programs. Every stage after the AIO is by invitation.

The steps

From one contest to an international team

The Trust describes each step on its own pages. This is the order they appear in, with the Trust's wording.

Two points are easy to miss. First, every step after the AIO is by invitation; there is no way to enter the AIIO, FARIO or the selection school directly. Second, the Trust uses several contests and the camp together, rather than a single exam, to choose the team.

The pathway also includes the Asian Pacific Informatics Olympiad, which appears in the Trust's 2026 calendar, and, for girls, the European Girls Olympiad in Informatics with its own mentor program.

The AMT and Australian Science Innovations "do not permit students to participate in more than one Olympiad program" in a year. A student invited into both maths and informatics, or into a science olympiad, has to choose one subject for that year.

The Trust also notes that the programs are supported by an Australian Government grant for mathematics and informatics olympiads. Invitations, selection and team announcements are entirely the Trust's decisions.

Read with the AIO's 2026 date of 27 August, the cycle is clear. A student does well in the AIO in late August, may be invited to the School of Excellence in December, then sits the AIIO in February and FARIO in March of the following year, before the selection school and APIO in autumn.

That means the real preparation for the invitational stages happens in the months after the AIO, not before it. Students who stop practising once the AIO is over are the ones who find February hardest.

The calendar is published year by year, and the 2027 version had not appeared when we checked. Dates and venues change, and the Trust's calendar is the only reliable source for them.

Several stages are sat at school or by correspondence rather than at a central venue. Families should expect a busy run from February to May for an invited student, alongside normal schoolwork.

Who can be selected

Eligibility for Australia's IOI and EGOI teams

The Trust publishes eligibility criteria, valid from 1 October 2025. They are short and worth reading in full on the Trust's site.

International rules

Students must "meet all IOI regulations as stated in Section S2 of the IOI Regulations"; for EGOI, the EGOI regulations apply as well.

An Australian school

The criteria require that a student "be attending an Australian school".

Time to take part

Students must "have the capacity to participate in all Olympiad activities", which is why the calendar above matters.

The criteria also note situations the Trust considers individually, and ask students in them to contact the Trust directly. We do not advise on eligibility; the published criteria and the Trust's staff are the authority, and anyone unsure should ask the Trust before relying on an invitation.

Source: AMT, IOI and EGOI Eligibility Criteria (PDF), read on 7 October 2026.

The skill the later stages demand

An operations budget: is my algorithm fast enough?

The AIO already marks efficiency. In the invitational contests, almost every problem has an obvious slow solution and a hidden fast one. Strong students estimate the running time before writing a single line.

The idea is simple. Count roughly how many basic steps an algorithm takes for the largest input, then compare that with how many steps the computer can do in the time allowed. If the count is far too big, no amount of tidying the code will save it; a different algorithm is needed.

To make that concrete we timed a plain Python loop on our own laptop. It managed about 28 million simple steps per second. Faster languages such as C++ do many more, and contest machines differ, so treat the numbers below as an illustration of scale rather than a promise about any judge.

The table shows why the jump matters. For 1,000 items, even an algorithm that compares every pair finishes almost instantly. For 100,000 items the same pairwise approach takes about 353 seconds, roughly six minutes. For a million items it takes about 35,000 seconds, nearly ten hours, while a sort-based approach still finishes in under a second.

Olympiad problems are built around exactly this gap. The skill is to read the input limits in a problem statement, make this estimate in your head, and only then choose the method.

Keeping it healthy

The pathway is long and selective. Students who enjoy problem solving for its own sake cope best with not being invited, which is the most common outcome.

Schoolwork and sleep come first; olympiad work should fit around them.

Questions about the pathway

What families of olympiad students ask

How does a student get from the AIO to the IOI?

Students who do well in the AIO may be invited into the AMT's olympiad programs. From there, invitational contests such as the AIIO and FARIO, the Informatics Selection School and APIO lead to selection of Australia's IOI team.

What is the AIIO?

The Australian Invitational Informatics Olympiad, a February contest for invited Australian and New Zealand students. The AMT says around 40 students are invited each year.

What is FARIO?

The French-Australian Regional Informatics Olympiad, held remotely between students from France, Australia, New Zealand and Belgium since 2004. The AMT calls it one of the main competitions used to select the IOI team.

Can a student enter the invitational contests directly?

No. Every stage after the AIO is by invitation from the AMT.

Who is eligible for the Australian IOI team?

The AMT's criteria require students to meet the IOI regulations in Section S2, attend an Australian school, and be able to take part in all olympiad activities.

Can a student do the maths and informatics olympiads in the same year?

The AMT and Australian Science Innovations do not permit participation in more than one olympiad program in a year, so students choose one subject.

What is an operations budget?

A quick estimate of how many basic steps an algorithm needs for the largest input, compared with what a computer can do in the time allowed. It shows before coding whether an approach can possibly be fast enough.

Where was the IOI in 2026?

The AMT's 2026 calendar lists the IOI as 9 to 16 August in Tashkent, Uzbekistan.

Can you get my child selected?

No one can promise that. We teach algorithms and contest skills; invitations and selection are decided by the AMT.

Are you connected with the AMT?

No. We are an independent online school, and the facts here come from the AMT's published pages.
