AQA A level Maths 7357 help

AQA A level Mathematics, specification code 7357, is sat at the end of Year 13 as three two-hour papers. What sets it apart from the other boards is how those papers are put together: pure maths is not confined to one or two papers but runs through all three, with mechanics added on Paper 2 and statistics on Paper 3. This page sets out exactly what each paper can test, how the marks are weighted, the large data set, the calculator AQA requires, and how we teach 7357 live online.

The short answer

AQA A level Mathematics 7357 is a linear A level sat at the end of Year 13 as three written papers, each 2 hours, 100 marks and a third of the grade. Paper 1 tests pure maths. Paper 2 can test any Paper 1 content plus vectors and mechanics; Paper 3 can test any Paper 1 content plus statistics. A calculator is used on all three. Grades run from A* to E. The three papers

What is on each AQA A level Maths paper?

Paper 1 is pure. Papers 2 and 3 are each pure plus one applied area. All three are sat at the end of the course.

AQA describes 7357 as linear, with every exam sat when the course ends. The specification lists nineteen content areas lettered A to S, and assigns them to papers in a particular way: Paper 1 draws on A to I, the pure topics; Paper 2 on "Any content from Paper 1" plus J and P to S; Paper 3 on "Any content from Paper 1" plus K to O. Every paper offers "A mix of question styles, from short, single-mark questions to multi-step problems."

Why the pairing matters

On Paper 2 a mechanics question can need integration to find displacement from velocity, or a trigonometric identity to resolve forces. On Paper 3 a statistics question can sit next to a pure question on logarithms or proof.

So a student cannot revise mechanics for Paper 2 and statistics for Paper 3 and leave pure behind. Pure is examined three times over.

Where vectors live

Vectors, area J, is listed with Paper 2 rather than Paper 1. That is a small detail with a practical consequence: vector work tends to appear alongside mechanics, where it describes position, velocity and force.

Students who learn vectors only as geometry can find the mechanics setting unfamiliar, so we teach both together.

How marks work

How is AQA A level Maths 7357 graded?

Three papers of 100 raw marks, added straight together into a total out of 300, then graded A* to E.

AQA scales each paper by a factor of one, so the raw marks are the scaled marks: "Total scaled mark: 300". Grade boundaries are set on that total. The qualification is "graded on a six-point scale: A*, A, B, C, D and E", and a student below E is recorded as U and "will not receive a qualification certificate."

The distinction is in the paper structure above. Half of every paper rewards technique, which is why accurate algebra and calculus remain the foundation, and the other half rewards turning words and contexts into maths and explaining the result.

Large data set

What is the AQA A level Maths large data set?

A real data set published only on AQA's website, which Paper 3 questions assume students have worked with.

Every A level Maths board must require students to "become familiar with one or more specific large data set(s) in advance of the final assessment", and AQA notes that "This requirement is common to all exam boards." AQA's first data set was an extract of the government's Family Food data, used for exams in 2018 and 2019. The specification then says: "For exams from 2020 there is a new dataset. This replacement dataset is also only available on the AQA website."

  1. Download the current data set from AQA's 7357 resources. The specification says it is only available there, so textbook extracts may be out of date.
  2. Learn the contexts: what each variable means, its units, and where values are missing or unusual. Questions can assume this background without explaining it.
  3. Explore it with a spreadsheet, as the content requires: summary statistics, charts and comparisons between groups.
  4. Practise analysing a subset with the calculator alone. AQA expects students to analyse "a subset or features of the data" in exactly that way.
  5. Connect each feature of the data to a topic in K to O: sampling methods, outliers and cleaning, correlation, distributions and hypothesis tests.

We set a short data-set task most weeks in Year 13, so the context is familiar long before the exam. Familiarity is the skill being rewarded: a student who has used the data set can read a question about it quickly, while one who has not spends minutes decoding the setting.

Calculator

Which calculator do you need for AQA A level Maths?

One with an iterative function, statistical distributions and an inverse normal function. AQA names all three.

The specification says: "A calculator is required for use in all assessments in this specification." It then sets a minimum: "an iterative function", "the ability to compute summary statistics and access probabilities from standard statistical distributions", and "an inverse normal function." Which models are permitted in the exam room is governed by the JCQ document Instructions for conducting examinations.

What each required function is for, in our words

Students who buy a calculator in Year 12 sometimes discover in Year 13 that it cannot do inverse normal calculations. Checking against AQA's list on day one avoids that. We also teach the working that must be written alongside a calculator result: the distribution and its parameters, the probability being found, and the conclusion in context.

Worked examples, ours

Three 7357 style problems, one for each paper type

We wrote these to show how a Paper 3 statistics question and a Paper 2 mechanics question still lean on pure maths.

Our first problem, Paper 3 style. The masses of a type of apple, X grams, are modelled as X ~ N(50, 82). (a) Find P(X < 62). (b) The heaviest 10 per cent are sold separately. Find the lowest mass in that group.

Part (a)

Standardise: z = (62 − 50) ÷ 8 = 1.5. The calculator's normal distribution function gives P(X < 62) = 0.9332 to 4 decimal places.

Writing the distribution, the value standardised and the probability statement earns method credit even if a key is pressed wrongly.

Part (b)

We want k with P(X > k) = 0.1, so P(X < k) = 0.9. The inverse normal function gives z = 1.2816, so k = 50 + 8 × 1.2816 = 60.25 grams.

This is the step AQA's required inverse normal function exists for. Without it, a student must work backwards from tables they will not be given.

Our second problem, Paper 3 style. A spinner is suspected of landing on red more often than half the time. In 20 spins it lands on red 14 times. Test at the 5 per cent level.

  1. Let X be the number of reds in 20 spins, so X ~ B(20, p). H0: p = 0.5 and H1: p > 0.5, a one-tailed test.
  2. Under H0, P(X ≥ 15) = 0.0207 and P(X ≥ 14) = 0.0577. The critical region is X ≥ 15, since 0.0577 is above 5 per cent.
  3. 14 is not in the critical region, so there is insufficient evidence at the 5 per cent level to say the spinner favours red.
  4. The conclusion is written in context, about the spinner, not just "accept H0". AO2 marks reward that sentence.

Our third problem, Paper 2 style. A uniform beam AB is 4 m long with mass 20 kg. It rests horizontally on supports at A and at C, where AC = 3 m. Find the reaction at each support, taking g = 9.8 m s−2.

Moments about A

The weight 20g acts at the midpoint, 2 m from A. Taking moments about A removes the unknown reaction there: RC × 3 = 20g × 2, so RC = 40g/3, about 130.7 N.

Resolving vertically

RA + RC = 20g, so RA = 20g − 40g/3 = 20g/3, about 65.3 N. Keeping g as a symbol until the last line avoids rounding twice.

Questions about 7357

What students and parents ask about AQA A level Maths

How many papers are in AQA A level Maths?

Three. Each is a 2-hour written exam with 100 marks, worth a third of the A level. All three are sat at the end of the course, because 7357 is linear.

What is on AQA A level Maths Paper 2?

Any pure content from Paper 1, plus vectors and the mechanics areas: quantities and units, kinematics, forces and Newton's laws, and moments.

What is on AQA A level Maths Paper 3?

Any pure content from Paper 1, plus statistics: sampling, data presentation and interpretation, probability, statistical distributions and hypothesis testing.

Is there a non-calculator paper in AQA A level Maths?

No. AQA says a calculator is required for all assessments in 7357. It must have an iterative function, statistical distributions and an inverse normal function.

What is the AQA large data set?

A real data set AQA publishes on its website, which students must know before the exam. The first was an extract of the Family Food data for 2018 and 2019 exams, and a replacement has been used since 2020.

How is AQA A level Maths graded?

The three papers are added to a total out of 300, and grades A* to E are awarded on that total. A student below E receives a U and no certificate.

Can I resit AQA A level Maths?

Yes. AQA says students can re-sit the qualification as many times as they wish within its shelf life. Because it is linear, a resit means all three papers again.

Is AQA A level Maths harder than Edexcel?

The assessment objectives are set by Ofqual and are the same for every board. The difference is structure: AQA spreads pure across all three papers, while Edexcel keeps statistics and mechanics together on Paper 3.

When are your 7357 classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Times are given in UK time.

Are you linked with AQA?

No. Modern Age Coders is an independent teaching company with no connection to AQA. Every practice question on this page was written by us.
