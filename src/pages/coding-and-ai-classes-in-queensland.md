## How far along the Gold Coast line is each station?

OpenStreetMap's route for the line from Roma Street to Varsity Lakes, every platform projected onto the track. Our measurements, 7 October 2026.

| Station | Along the track (km) | Straight line (km) | Track per straight km |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| South Bank | 2.684 | 1.878 | 1.429 |
| Boggo Road | 4.239 | 3.287 | 1.290 |
| Altandi | 17.873 | 13.792 | 1.296 |
| Loganlea | 31.249 | 25.634 | 1.219 |
| Beenleigh | 40.205 | 33.409 | 1.203 |
| Coomera | 59.441 | 52.033 | 1.142 |
| Helensvale | 68.229 | 60.104 | 1.135 |
| Robina | 85.264 | 76.032 | 1.121 |
| Varsity Lakes | 89.013 | 79.555 | 1.119 |

### 1. One line from many pieces

The route arrives as more than two hundred separate track pieces. Learners join them end to end into a single line of 1,434 points measuring 89.1 km, and check that no piece was reversed or skipped.

### 2. Drop each platform onto it

A platform sits beside the track, not on it. For every short segment the code finds the closest point, then keeps the closest segment overall. Platform centres land between 0 and 6.4 m from the line.

### 3. Read the distance

The distance along the line to that dropped point is the chainage. Near Brisbane the track bends with the river and the ratio to the straight line is about 1.4; by the Gold Coast it settles near 1.12.

### The shortcut that drifts

Snapping each platform to the nearest stored point on the line, instead of projecting onto a segment, looks almost the same and takes one line of code. On this route it moves Coomera by 73.9 m and the platform OpenStreetMap labels Hope Island by 64.6 m, because stored points are sparse on straight stretches. Learners measure both methods and see exactly where the shortcut fails.

Every figure in this section is our calculation from OpenStreetMap data, which volunteers maintain and which can be incomplete. Track geometry also differs from official railway chainage, which starts at its own datum; our zero is the Roma Street platform.

Geometry in code

## Five habits for any program that measures along a route

Practised on a railway, then used on hiking tracks, delivery runs, pipelines and any data recorded "so many kilometres along".

| Habit | What it looks like | What it prevents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Check the joins | Confirm each piece starts where the last one ended | A line that doubles back on itself |
| Project, do not snap | Find the closest point on each segment, not the closest vertex | Errors of tens of metres on straight stretches |
| Use a sensible projection | Convert degrees to metres with a local scale | Distances stretched or squeezed by latitude |
| Report the offset too | Store how far each point sat from the line | Silently attaching a point to the wrong track |
| Say where zero is | Name the starting point of every chainage | Two reports that disagree by a fixed amount |

The second habit matters most. A line stored as points is only accurate between them if you treat the gaps as straight segments, and the closest vertex can be a long way from the closest point. Once learners have seen 73.9 m appear from that one shortcut, they test every geometric function against a second method.

## An AI tool will happily write a distance function. Why should a Queensland student test it?

Because a function can return believable kilometres and still measure the wrong thing.

Ask an assistant how far Helensvale is from Roma Street and you may get the straight-line figure, the timetable figure or a guess, with no hint which. Ask it to write code that places stations on a route and it may snap each one to the nearest stored point, because that is shorter to write. The output looks tidy. The error only appears when someone compares it with a careful projection and finds tens of metres missing.

A student who has measured the Gold Coast line both ways knows what to ask of any generated answer involving distance: along what, from where, and by which method? Those questions apply to maps, sensors, logistics and every model that turns positions into numbers, and they separate someone who uses AI output from someone who can vouch for it.

That is why a Queensland student should still learn to code in 2026: not to beat an AI tool at typing, but to know when its kilometres are the right ones. The longer argument is in [why teenagers should still learn to code in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).

## Queensland coding class questions

What Queensland families ask us first.

### How many people live in Queensland?

The 2021 Census counted 5,156,138 people in Queensland, with a median age of 38, and 2,526,238 of them in Greater Brisbane.

### What is chainage?

Chainage is a distance measured along a route such as a railway, road or pipeline from a fixed starting point, rather than straight across the map. Engineers use it to say exactly where something sits on a line.

### What is the Gold Coast line project?

Learners rebuild the line from Roma Street to Varsity Lakes from OpenStreetMap, project each platform onto the track and measure its chainage. Varsity Lakes comes out at 89.013 km along the track against 79.555 km in a straight line.

### Does Queensland have daylight saving?

No. The IANA time zone database records Queensland's last daylight saving ending in March 1992, so the state stays on the same time all year, four and a half hours ahead of India.
