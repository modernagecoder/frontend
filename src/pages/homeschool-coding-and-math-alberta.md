## Alberta home education: the two kinds of program

The province's home education page, read on 22 October 2026, opens with a definition: "Home education is a parent-directed approach to educating a student in Grades 1 to 12 at home or elsewhere in which they are responsible for making all education decisions." It then says "A parent or guardian may choose 1 of 2 home education programs".

**Alberta's two home education programs, quoted from alberta.ca/home-education (read 22 October 2026)**

|  | Supervised by school authority (funded) | Not supervised by school authority (notification only, non-funded) |
|---|---|---|
| Who you notify | "Each year, parents have the responsibility to notify their supervising school authority of their intent to home educate their child" | "Each year, parents have the responsibility to notify the Minister of Education of their intent to home educate their child." |
| The program plan | Parents "must develop a home education program that enables the student to achieve appropriate learning outcomes." | Parents must develop such a program "but are not required to submit this program plan to the Minister of Education." |
| Teacher evaluations | The associate board or school must arrange for certificated teachers "to conduct at least two formal evaluations of each student each school year", including "a review of the student portfolio". | No school authority evaluation is described for this type. |
| Funding, as the page states it | "Students are not eligible for funding if a home education program is accepted after September 29." | "Parents choosing to home educate their children without the supervision of a school authority are not eligible to receive any Alberta Education funding." |
| Timing advice on the page | A parent "should consider looking for a supervising school authority in the spring before the upcoming school year." | The page gives a notification form and an online option. |

## What the Home Education Regulation asks of every parent

Alberta Regulation 89/2019, in the King's Printer edition current as of September 1, 2025, applies to both kinds of program. A few of its sections decide how a coding and math program should be recorded.

**Home Education Regulation, AR 89/2019, quoted from the King's Printer PDF (read 22 October 2026)**

| Section | What it says | What it means for coding and math (ours) |
|---|---|---|
| 3(2) | "A home education program must enable a student to achieve applicable outcomes." | Pick outcomes first, then classes and projects that reach them. |
| 3(4) | "A home education program may follow the Alberta Programs of Study." | Following the provincial math program is a choice, not a requirement. |
| 4(1)(a) | A parent "must conduct an evaluation of the progress of the student at regular intervals and maintain a record of the methods and dates of those evaluations". | A monthly check, written down with its date and how it was done. |
| 4(1)(b) | A parent "must maintain dated samples of student work and a general record of the student's activities". | Date every problem set and save every finished program. |
| 6 | A student whose program "follows the Alberta Programs of Study is eligible to receive high school credits" on "the recommendation of a school principal", if the student meets the course standards and learning outcomes. | Credits come through a school principal, never through a tutoring provider. |

### If you do not follow the Programs of Study

The regulation's Schedule sets learning outcomes for programs that do not follow the provincial program. It opens with "a solid core program including language arts, mathematics, science and social studies".

Three of its items fit coding and math directly: students should "use mathematics to solve problems in business, science and daily life situations", "demonstrate critical and creative thinking skills in problem solving and decision making", and "demonstrate competence in using information technologies".

### If you do follow them

Alberta has been renewing its curriculum. The province lists a new K to 6 mathematics curriculum as implemented under 2022/2023, and says its draft Grades 7 to 9 curriculum integrates "artificial intelligence, computer science" and other topics ahead of mandatory implementation in September 2027.

Families who follow the provincial program should check which version applies to their child's grade each year, because the junior high change lands in September 2027.

Sources: [Home Education Regulation, AR 89/2019 (King's Printer)](https://kings-printer.alberta.ca/documents/Regs/2019_089.pdf) and [Alberta, curriculum: how we got here](https://www.alberta.ca/curriculum-how-we-got-here), read 22 October 2026. Families must check the current official text.

## A four-week coding and math cycle that produces dated samples

Because section 4 asks for regular evaluation and dated samples, we plan home-education coding and math in four-week cycles, each ending with a recorded check. This is our own plan for a student in about Grade 8 or 9, with two of our live classes a week.

**One four-week cycle (our sample plan)**

| Week | Math focus | Coding focus | Dated sample to keep |
|---|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | A new idea, for example linear relations, built from tables and graphs | Write a short program that prints a table of values | The first worked page and the program file |
| Week 2 | Practice in mixed problems, including one from daily life | Change the program to test a second rule and compare | A practice set, self-checked, dated |
| Week 3 | An applied project that needs the idea, such as the Garden Bed Planner below | Build the project program in class, then extend it at home | The project plan, code and output |
| Week 4 | Review and a short written check, about forty minutes | Explain the project code to a parent, line by line | The check, marked, with the method and date recorded |

### Evaluation, recorded

Week 4 gives the "methods and dates" record that section 4(1)(a) mentions, in one line per cycle.

### Samples, dated

Every week leaves at least one dated item, which builds the portfolio that supervised programs review.

### About ten cycles a year

Ten four-week cycles fit a school year with room for breaks and a summer pause.

## Project: the Garden Bed Planner, square rows or staggered rows

We wrote this project for home-educated students in Grades 8 to 10. It answers a real gardening question with geometry and a short Python program, and the answer turns out to be "it depends", which is the point. It also speaks to the Schedule's "daily life situations" item and to using information technology.

The question. Seedlings must be 30 cm apart and at least 15 cm from the edge of a raised bed. Is it better to plant in square rows, or in staggered rows where every second row shifts half a space along? In staggered rows the rows can sit closer together, 30 times the square root of 3 divided by 2, about 26 cm, because each plant sits in the gap between two plants of the next row and stays 30 cm from both.

**bed.py · our program** (python)
```python
# Our Garden Bed Planner: square rows or staggered rows? (Grades 8 to 10 math with code)
import math

def count(length, width, spacing, margin, staggered):
    usable_l = length - 2 * margin          # plants stay margin cm from every edge
    usable_w = width - 2 * margin
    gap = spacing * math.sqrt(3) / 2 if staggered else spacing   # distance between rows
    rows = math.floor(usable_w / gap) + 1
    plan = []
    for r in range(rows):
        shift = spacing / 2 if (staggered and r % 2 == 1) else 0
        per_row = math.floor((usable_l - shift) / spacing) + 1
        plan.append(per_row)
    return plan

for length, width in [(240, 120), (225, 160)]:
    square = count(length, width, 30, 15, staggered=False)
    stagger = count(length, width, 30, 15, staggered=True)
    print(f"Bed {length} x {width} cm: square {sum(square)} plants {square}, "
          f"staggered {sum(stagger)} plants {stagger}")
```
We ran it under Python 3 on 22 October 2026. math.floor counts whole spaces; the plus 1 counts the first plant at the start of a row.

**What it prints** (text)
```text
Bed 240 x 120 cm: square 32 plants [8, 8, 8, 8], staggered 30 plants [8, 7, 8, 7]
Bed 225 x 160 cm: square 35 plants [7, 7, 7, 7, 7], staggered 42 plants [7, 7, 7, 7, 7, 7]
```

1. Predict first. Most students guess that staggered rows always win, because the rows are closer together.
2. Read the first result. In a 240 by 120 cm bed, square rows hold 32 plants and staggered rows only 30. The usable width, 90 cm, fits four rows either way, and the shifted rows lose one plant each.
3. Read the second result. In a 225 by 160 cm bed, staggered rows hold 42 against 35. The usable width, 130 cm, now fits a sixth staggered row but only five square rows.
4. Explain why. Staggering helps only when the spare width is enough for an extra row. The student writes this as a sentence, then tests it on a bed of their own measurements.
5. Check by hand. Draw one bed to scale on squared paper and count. The drawing and the program must agree, or one of them is wrong.

### The math inside it

Measurement and margins, division with remainders, an equilateral-triangle height with a square root, and comparing two methods with evidence. That spans junior high geometry and early high school.

Students who are not ready for square roots can run the program and focus on the comparison; older students derive the 26 cm figure themselves.

### For the portfolio

The prediction, the scale drawing, the program, its output and a short conclusion, each dated. One project, a week 3 sample and a week 4 check in a single folder.

This is the lesson family behind the Alberta page: compare two methods with evidence before choosing one, and date what you find.

## PATs and Waterloo contests for home-educated students

Alberta's provincial tests and the University of Waterloo's contests both involve a school. The regulation and CEMC's own standards say how.

**Provincial assessments and CEMC contests for home-educated students (official wording read 22 October 2026; the last column is ours)**

| What | What the official source says | Our suggestion |
|---|---|---|
| Provincial Achievement Tests | Alberta: PATs "are administered annually to all Alberta students in grades 6 and 9." | Ask your school authority how this applies to your child's program. |
| Supervised programs and PATs | AR 89/2019 s.4(2)(a): the associate board or school "must ensure that a student is given the opportunity to write" the "applicable provincial assessments", at the time the Minister designates and under its supervision. | Ask the associate board early in the year when and where its students write. |
| CEMC contests | CEMC: "Contests may not be taken remotely or without supervision." and "Participants may only write the contest at the school which ordered the contest." | A home-educated student needs a school that orders the contest and agrees to host them. An associate school is a natural first place to ask, though it is not obliged to. |
| When to ask | CEMC says creating a new school account can take weeks to review. | Ask by October for winter contests and by January for spring ones. |

Our teachers prepare students for the contests, for example the Gauss Contest in Grades 7 and 8 and the Pascal, Cayley and Fermat contests in Grades 9 to 11, but cannot enter anyone or supervise a sitting. Our [CEMC contests page](/cemc-waterloo-contests-preparation) covers each contest, and the [Pascal](/pascal-contest-preparation) guide shows what Grade 9 students meet.

## Class times on Alberta's single clock

Alberta no longer changes its clocks, which makes class times simple to keep for a whole year.

Alberta's Official Time Act defines official time as "the time 6 hours behind Coordinated Universal Time", and Saskatchewan shares that offset. Ontario still changes, so the gap between Calgary and Toronto moves: a 10:00 a.m. class in Calgary is 12:00 noon in Toronto in winter 2026 to 2027 and 1:00 p.m. in Toronto after Ontario moves its clocks forward on 14 March 2027. That is our arithmetic, shown so families with relatives in the east are not caught out. For a home-educated student the clock barely matters, because classes can sit on a weekday morning as easily as a weekend.

## Homeschool coding and math in Alberta: common questions

### What are the two kinds of home education in Alberta?

Alberta's page lists a program supervised by a school authority, which it labels funded, and a program not supervised by a school authority, which it labels notification only and non-funded. Read the page and talk to a school authority before choosing.

### Do I have to follow the Alberta curriculum when homeschooling?

No. The Home Education Regulation says a home education program may follow the Alberta Programs of Study. Programs that do not follow them work towards the learning outcomes in the regulation's Schedule instead.

### What records does Alberta ask home-educating parents to keep?

Section 4 of the regulation says parents must evaluate progress at regular intervals and record the methods and dates, and must keep dated samples of student work and a general record of the student's activities.

### Can a home-educated student earn Alberta high school credits through your classes?

No. We do not grant provincial high school credits. Section 6 of the regulation says credits come on the recommendation of a school principal, for a program that follows the Alberta Programs of Study.

### Are your classes covered by home education funding?

We do not advise on funding. Alberta's page and its Standards for Home Education Reimbursement set the rules, and your associate board or school decides. Please check those sources directly.

### Do home-educated students write PATs?

Alberta says PATs are administered annually to all Alberta students in Grades 6 and 9, and the regulation says an associate board or school must give students in supervised programs the opportunity to write applicable provincial assessments. Ask your school authority.

### Can a home-educated Alberta student write Waterloo contests?

Yes, at a school that orders the contest. CEMC says contests may not be taken remotely or without supervision, and participants may only write at the school which ordered the contest. Ask a nearby school early.

### How does coding fit Alberta home education?

If you use the regulation's Schedule, coding supports outcomes such as demonstrating competence in using information technologies and using mathematics to solve problems. Our classes pair each math idea with a short program.

### When are classes held for Alberta families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Alberta stays on UTC-6 all year, so the time never shifts.

### Is this page legal or funding advice?

No. It quotes alberta.ca and the Home Education Regulation so you can find the source quickly. Check the official text and your school authority before you decide anything.
