Cayley Contest preparation for Grade 10

The Cayley Contest is the Grade 10 paper among the Waterloo math contests, written each February in schools across Canada and abroad. It sits between Pascal in Grade 9 and Fermat in Grade 11 and looks exactly like them: an hour, twenty-five questions, multiple choice until the last five. What changes is the math, which now reaches quadratics, coordinate geometry and similar triangles, and the final five questions, where there are no options to fall back on. This guide is drawn from the University of Waterloo CEMC Cayley pages for 2027, the cover of the 2025 Cayley booklet and the 2026 results booklet, and it adds eight problems of our own with every step of the solution shown.

One of three, once a year

A student may write only one of Pascal, Cayley and Fermat in a school year. So a Grade 10 student cannot sit Cayley for the real result and Pascal for practice in the same February.

A Grade 9 student who is far ahead may choose Cayley instead of Pascal; CEMC says "Exceptional participants may elect to write a contest beyond their actual grade level." The reverse is not allowed: a Grade 10 student cannot drop down to Pascal.

Not the UK Cayley

The name is shared with a Cayley paper set by the UK Mathematics Trust, which is a different paper from a different organizer. Everything on this page is the Waterloo contest.

Families who do mean the UK paper will find it on our Cayley, Hamilton and Maclaurin page.

The last five questions

Part C on Cayley: an answer from 0 to 99, and no options to lean on

In Parts A and B a student can test the five options, rule some out, or estimate their way to a letter. Part C takes that away. The answer is a whole number, and the 2025 booklet explains the convention used when a natural answer would be bigger than 99.

One line in that booklet deserves a second look. On question 22 of the 2026 Cayley, the single most popular answer was a wrong one: 15.51% of contestants gave it, slightly more than the 14.72% who gave the correct answer. A wrong number written confidently in Part C earns nothing, and in 2026 more students did that on question 22 than got it right. That is what Part C asks of a student: to check an answer when no list of options can confirm it.

  1. Put it back in. If the question gives a condition, test your number against it. Roots go back into the equation; a count goes back into the rule.
  2. Try a smaller case. Shrink the question (20 terms to 4, 30 to 9) and solve the small version by listing. If your method gives the wrong small answer, it is wrong for the big one too.
  3. Check size and parity. Should the answer be even? Divisible by 3? Smaller than some obvious bound? A count of pairs that comes out odd when the pairs are symmetric is a warning sign.
  4. Read the last line again. Many Part C answers are "the rightmost two digits of" something, a sum, or a count of objects. Make sure the number you code answers that, and not a step on the way.
  5. Code it correctly. A one-digit answer needs a leading zero, so 7 is coded 07, and an answer of 0 is coded 00.

Those five checks are ours, not CEMC's. We teach them from the first week and use them on every Part C question in class, including the last three problems in the practice set below.

Eight practice problems

Eight Cayley-style problems we wrote, with every step shown

These are our practice problems, not CEMC problems. Our teachers wrote all eight for Grade 10 students, and nothing in them is taken or reworked from an old Cayley paper. Two are at Part A level, three at Part B level and three are Part C questions with an answer from 0 to 99. Every answer was confirmed by a brute-force check in Python before this page went up.

Cayley practice 1 (our practice problem, not a CEMC problem; Part A standard). The line y = 3x - 6 meets the x-axis at P and the y-axis at Q. If O is the origin, what is the area of triangle OPQ? (A) 3  (B) 6  (C) 9  (D) 12  (E) 18

Solution. At P, y = 0, so 3x = 6 and P is (2, 0). At Q, x = 0, so Q is (0, -6). The triangle has a right angle at O, with legs of length 2 and 6, so its area is (2 x 6) / 2 = 6, answer (B). A length is never negative: the leg along the y-axis is 6, not -6.

Cayley practice 2 (our practice problem, not a CEMC problem; Part A standard). If x + y = 11 and x - y = 3, what is the value of x2 - y2? (A) 24  (B) 30  (C) 33  (D) 36  (E) 49

Solution. There is no need to find x and y. Factor: x2 - y2 = (x + y)(x - y) = 11 x 3 = 33, answer (C). Check by solving: adding the equations gives 2x = 14, so x = 7 and y = 4, and 49 - 16 = 33. The factoring takes ten seconds, the solving a minute, and on a 60-minute paper that difference adds up.

Cayley practice 3 (our practice problem, not a CEMC problem; Part B standard). The product of two consecutive positive odd integers is 323. What is their sum? (A) 32  (B) 34  (C) 36  (D) 38  (E) 40

Solution. Call the even number between them n, so the odd integers are n - 1 and n + 1. Their product is n2 - 1 = 323, so n2 = 324 and n = 18. The integers are 17 and 19, and their sum is 2n = 36, answer (C). Check: 17 x 19 = 323. Choosing the middle number as the unknown turns a messy quadratic into a perfect square.

Cayley practice 4 (our practice problem, not a CEMC problem; Part B standard). A student 1.8 m tall stands 6 m from the foot of a lamp post. The student's shadow, cast by the lamp, is 3 m long on level ground. How tall, in metres, is the lamp post? (A) 3.6  (B) 4.8  (C) 5.4  (D) 6.0  (E) 7.2

Solution. The tip of the shadow is 6 + 3 = 9 m from the post. The light ray from the top of the post to the tip of the shadow passes over the student's head, so the small triangle (student and shadow) is similar to the large one (post and full distance). Heights compare as bases: h / 9 = 1.8 / 3, so h = 9 x 0.6 = 5.4, answer (C). The usual slip is using 6 m as the base of the large triangle, which gives 3.6, option (A).

Cayley practice 5 (our practice problem, not a CEMC problem; Part B standard). How many three-digit positive integers have three odd digits that strictly increase from left to right, such as 139? (A) 6  (B) 10  (C) 20  (D) 60  (E) 125

Solution. The odd digits are 1, 3, 5, 7 and 9. Any choice of three different odd digits can be arranged in increasing order in exactly one way, so the count equals the number of ways to choose 3 digits from 5, which is (5 x 4 x 3) / (3 x 2 x 1) = 10, answer (B). Option (D), 60, counts every order instead of only the increasing one, and (E), 125, allows repeats.

Cayley practice 6 (our practice problem, not a CEMC problem; Part C standard). The equation x2 - 24x + k = 0 has two different positive integer roots. How many different values can k take? Give a whole-number answer between 0 and 99.

Solution. If the roots are r and s, then r + s = 24 and rs = k. With r smaller than s and both positive, r can be 1, 2, 3 and so on up to 11 (r = 12 would make the roots equal). Each r gives a different product r(24 - r): 23, 44, 63, 80, 95, 108, 119, 128, 135, 140 and 143. That is 11 values, so the answer is 11. Check with the first value: x2 - 24x + 23 = (x - 1)(x - 23), whose roots, 1 and 23, are different positive integers.

Cayley practice 7 (our practice problem, not a CEMC problem; Part C standard). Let S = 3 + 32 + 33 + ... + 320. What is the integer formed by the rightmost two digits of S?

Cayley practice 8 (our practice problem, not a CEMC problem; Part C standard). In how many ways can 30 be written as a + b + c, where a, b and c are positive integers with a ≤ b ≤ c, and a, b and c can be the side lengths of a triangle?

Timing the set

Sixteen minutes for all eight matches the pace of a real Cayley. Part A problems should take a minute or two each, which leaves more time for the Part C ones.

Mark yourself with the booklet's scale (5, 6, 8, and 2 for a blank) before checking the solutions, so the score means what a real Cayley score means.

The ideas to carry forward

Choosing a good unknown (problem 3), factoring instead of solving (problem 2), and fixing the largest piece first (problem 8) all appear again and again in Fermat and Euclid.

Problem 7 is the one to redo in a month. Pairing terms that sit a fixed distance apart is an idea students forget, and it unlocks whole families of last-two-digit questions.

Ontario and British Columbia

In Ontario, the Grade 10 academic math course is MPM2D, Principles of Mathematics, and it is the prerequisite for Grade 11 Functions (MCR3U). Its quadratics, analytic geometry and trigonometry of right triangles line up well with the Cayley paper.

In British Columbia the Grade 10 course is Foundations of Mathematics and Pre-calculus 10. Students elsewhere in Canada meet similar content in their own Grade 10 course, which is why CEMC builds the paper on what the provinces share.

Where school stops and the contest starts

Very little on a Cayley paper is outside the school course. What is outside is the way it is used: a school question tells you to factor, a contest question expects you to notice that factoring helps.

That is why our classes spend less time on new content than families expect, and more on recognizing which tool a question needs.

Getting a seat

How a Grade 10 student gets into the Cayley Contest

Only a school can order the paper. CEMC's own figure for how far this reaches: "Each year, educators order CEMC contests for over 300 000 students worldwide". A family's job is to make sure their child is one of them.

If the school already orders

Ask the math teacher in November 2026 whether Cayley will be ordered, on which of the two February days it will run, and whether the school has chosen paper or the online platform.

The student then writes at that school on that day. CEMC's standards say "Participants may only write the contest at the school which ordered the contest."

If it does not

A school without a CEMC account needs one, and CEMC reviews new applications by hand, which can take three weeks. That is a conversation for the head of math before the winter break.

Where a school declines, CEMC points students to other local schools that might take them in for the sitting. A homeschooling family would take the same route; we found no separate home-education process on the CEMC pages we read.

On the day, the rules about aids come from the paper itself: CEMC says "Only permitted aids indicated on the Contest Question Booklet or Supervisor Instructions Document may be used while writing the contest." From CEMC's How contests work page, as it read on 22 October 2026.

Cayley Contest: questions and answers

What is the Cayley Contest?

It is the University of Waterloo CEMC math contest for Grade 10 students. The paper has twenty-five questions, takes an hour and is marked out of 150, and it is written at school in February.

When is the Cayley Contest in 2027?

In North and South America CEMC lists Tuesday, February 23, 2027 and Wednesday, February 24, 2027, and the school picks one of those days. Schools in Canada must have their order in by Tuesday, February 9, 2027.

Who can write the Cayley Contest?

CEMC says students in Grade 10 are eligible, and that it may be written officially by any participant in Grade 10 or below. Pascal, Cayley and Fermat count as a set, so a student writes just one of them each school year.

What is Part C on the Cayley Contest?

It is the last five questions. Instead of choosing a letter, the student gives a whole number from 0 to 99. When a natural answer is larger, the question usually asks for the integer formed by its rightmost two digits, and a one-digit answer is coded with a leading zero, such as 07.

Is there a penalty for wrong answers on Cayley?

No. Under the 2025 booklet rules a wrong answer simply scores zero. Correct answers earn 5, 6 or 8 marks by part, and each unanswered question earns 2, up to ten unanswered questions.

What was the Cayley average in 2026?

CEMC lists a 2026 Cayley average of 90.0 out of 150, a Certificate of Distinction cutoff of 106 and a School Medal Champion cutoff of 90, with 19 953 contestants.

Is the Waterloo Cayley Contest the same as the UK Cayley?

No. The Waterloo Cayley is CEMC's Grade 10 paper. The UK Cayley belongs to the UK Mathematics Trust and is a different paper, with its own route to entry and its own date.

How does my child enter the Cayley Contest?

Through their school, which orders the contest from CEMC. Students cannot enter on their own. If the school does not take part, CEMC suggests looking for another school nearby that will let the student write there.

Should a Grade 10 student also write Galois?

We think so, if they enjoy Cayley. Galois is CEMC's Grade 10 full-solution contest, on Wednesday, April 7, 2027 in the Americas, with four questions in 75 minutes, and it rewards clear written reasoning.

When are your Cayley classes held?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Times are set in Eastern time and shown to each family in its own time zone.
