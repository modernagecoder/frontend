## Is digital literacy compulsory in Dutch schools?

The starting position is worth stating plainly, because most marketing in this sector implies the opposite. **The Netherlands has had no compulsory education in digital literacy or informatics in primary or lower secondary schooling.** Not a thin version of it. None required.

- **2022**: the Ministry of Education, Culture and Science tasks **SLO**, the Netherlands Institute for Curriculum Development, with developing core objectives for digital literacy across primary and lower secondary education
- **March 2024**: that curriculum is finalised: **nine core objectives grouped into three domains**

| Domain | What it covers | Where a coding class sits |
|---|---|---|
| A. Practical knowledge and skills | Using digital technology capably | Partly. A useful side effect rather than the target |
| B. Designing and making | Building things with digital technology | Squarely. This is the whole of what we do |
| C. Interaction between digital technology, media, people and society | Judgement, consequence, media and information literacy | Indirectly, through the data project below |

Digital literacy in the Dutch definition is **ICT skills, media literacy, information literacy and computational thinking** taken together, with computational thinking integrated through all of them rather than parked in one corner.

Sources: [OECD Review of Digital Education Policy in the Netherlands](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-review-of-digital-education-policy-in-the-netherlands_91118813-en/full-report/the-curriculum-framework-assessments-and-school-evaluations_bde116eb.html) and [K-8 Digital Literacy Curriculum in the Netherlands](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-73474-8_3). ## So how much coding does my child actually get?

Nobody can tell you nationally, and any provider who quotes you a Dutch hours figure has invented it.

Core objectives being finalised in March 2024 is a starting gun, not a description of classrooms. Whether a given school has staff who can teach domain B, timetable space to do it in, and equipment to do it with, is a separate question with a separate answer per school.

**Ask your child's school directly.** It is a fair question and they will have an answer. In Hong Kong you can look the number up; here you have to ask.

## Why will "designing and making" be the hard domain?

Not because Dutch schools are weak. Because of what that domain costs to deliver.

Two of the three domains can largely be taught inside existing lessons by existing teachers. Using digital technology capably fits into almost any subject. Thinking about media, information and consequence fits naturally into language and citizenship work, and Dutch schools were doing versions of it long before it was a core objective.

**Designing and making does not fit anywhere.** It needs sustained time on the same task across weeks, a teacher who can read a learner's broken code and say something useful about it, and enough repetition that a child stops being afraid of an error message. Those are the three things a school timetable is worst at giving a single subject, and no amount of goodwill in a curriculum document changes that.

| Stage | What we would do |
|---|---|
| Groep 3 to 5 | Before most schools reach any of this. Ordering, counting, predicting what a thing will do before running it |
| Groep 6 to 8 | Where domain B work becomes possible. Making something real and explaining why it works |
| First years of VO | The best window in the Dutch system. Blocks into Python, no examination pressure yet |
| Examination years | Reduce or wait. Nothing here is on a Dutch certificate |

## The Netherlands student data project

Every market page here works through one analytical trap. The Dutch one is a mistake governments and newspapers make constantly: **a policy date is not an outcome date.**

The learner is given two dates and a question. The core objectives were finalised in March 2024. Suppose a national measurement of pupil skill is taken in 2026 and the result is better than the last one. Did the new curriculum work?

Every learner says yes, or probably. The dates are in the right order, the direction is right, and the story writes itself.

Now consider who the 2026 pupils are. **A learner measured in the last year of primary school in 2026 spent most of their schooling before March 2024.** Whatever they can do, the great majority of it was taught by teachers working without the core objectives, because those objectives did not exist yet. The policy cannot have caused most of what is being measured. It had not happened for most of the time in question.

The error is not that the conclusion is definitely wrong. It might be partly right at the margins. The error is that the reasoning does not support it, and nothing in the numbers separates "the curriculum worked" from "these children were already improving" or "the test changed" or "the schools that implemented first were the strongest schools anyway".

So the learner writes a program that takes a policy date and a measurement date and reports how much of each cohort's schooling actually fell inside the policy. For a 2026 primary leaver the answer is a small fraction. Then they write the sentence they would be willing to defend, which turns out to be much duller and much more useful than the one they started with.

This is deliberately not a correlation-and-causation exercise in the usual sense. Both things are real and the order is correct. The problem is **exposure**: the cause was present for only part of the period it is being credited with.

## What should a child learn now that AI writes the code?

The Dutch framework answers this without setting out to. There is no domain called programming. There is a domain called **designing and making**, and computational thinking runs through all three.

Give an AI the two dates above and ask whether the curriculum worked, and it will produce a balanced, readable, professional paragraph. It may even mention that correlation is not causation, because that phrase is everywhere in its training. What it will not reliably do is notice the specific thing that matters here: that a 2026 primary leaver was barely exposed to a 2024 policy, so the question is close to unanswerable with the data given. That takes holding the cohort in your head, and nobody prompted for it.

## What about the time difference?

This is the market where we are least willing to publish a fixed weekly slot, and it would be easy to pretend otherwise.

The Netherlands moves between CET and CEST. Indian Standard Time never shifts. So **India is four and a half hours ahead in Dutch winter and three and a half in summer**, and a recurring booking that works in November is an hour out in April.

- **Weekend slots and late-afternoon Dutch times** are the easiest to arrange
- **Weekday evenings** are agreed case by case
- Every time is confirmed on the free call before any payment
- If we cannot offer one that suits your week, we will say so rather than book you into something that fails in a month

Otherwise: groups of **five to eight** or **one to one**, **twice a week** for normally eight lessons a month, classes in **English**, and a laptop or desktop with stable internet, working audio and a current browser.
