## Nine core objectives, three domains, finalised in 2024

A short timeline, because the dates are the part that matters.

The starting position is worth stating plainly, because most marketing in this sector implies the opposite. **The Netherlands has had no compulsory education in digital literacy or informatics in primary or lower secondary schooling.** Not a thin version of it. None required.

In **2022** the Dutch Ministry of Education, Culture and Science tasked **SLO**, the Netherlands Institute for Curriculum Development, with developing core objectives for digital literacy across primary and lower secondary education. In **March 2024** that curriculum was finalised. It sets out **nine core objectives grouped into three domains**.

| Domain | What it covers | Where a coding class sits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Practical knowledge and skills | Using digital technology capably | Partly. A useful side effect rather than the target |
| B. Designing and making | Building things with digital technology | Squarely. This is the whole of what we do |
| C. Interaction between digital technology, media, people and society | Judgement, consequence, media and information literacy | Indirectly, through the data project below |

Digital literacy in the Dutch definition is **ICT skills, media literacy, information literacy and computational thinking** taken together, with computational thinking integrated through all of them rather than parked in one corner. That is a better definition than most countries manage, and it happens to describe the order we teach in: the reasoning is the subject and the code is how you practise it.

Why a finalised curriculum does not tell you what your child is doing

Core objectives being finalised in March 2024 is a starting gun, not a description of classrooms. Whether a given school has staff who can teach domain B, timetable space to do it in, and equipment to do it with, is a separate question with a separate answer per school. So there is no national hours figure to quote you, and any provider implying there is has invented it. Ask your child's school directly. It is a fair question and they will have an answer.

The hard domain

## Designing and making is the one schools will struggle with

Not because Dutch schools are weak, but because of what that domain costs to deliver.

Of the three domains, two can largely be taught inside existing lessons by existing teachers. Using digital technology capably fits into almost any subject. Thinking about media, information and consequence fits naturally into language and citizenship work, and Dutch schools were doing versions of it long before it was a core objective.

Designing and making does not fit anywhere. It needs sustained time on the same task across weeks, a teacher who can read a learner's broken code and say something useful about it, and enough repetition that a child stops being afraid of an error message. Those are the three things a school timetable is worst at providing to a single subject, and no amount of goodwill in a curriculum document changes that.

The student data project

## The policy that gets credit for things it could not have caused

Every market page here works through one analytical trap. The Dutch one is a mistake governments and newspapers make constantly.

The learner is given two dates and a question. The digital literacy core objectives were finalised in March 2024. Suppose a national measurement of pupil skill is taken in 2026 and the result is better than the last one. Did the new curriculum work?

Every learner says yes, or at least probably. The dates are in the right order, the direction is right, and the story writes itself.

The trap

A policy date is not an outcome date

Consider who the 2026 pupils are. A learner measured in the last year of primary school in 2026 spent most of their schooling before March 2024. Whatever they can do, the great majority of it was taught by teachers working without the core objectives, because those objectives did not exist yet. The policy cannot have caused most of what is being measured. It had not happened for most of the time in question.

The error is not that the conclusion is definitely wrong. It might be partly right at the margins. The error is that the reasoning does not support it, and nothing in the numbers distinguishes "the curriculum worked" from "these children were already improving" or "the test changed" or "the schools that implemented first were the strongest schools anyway".

So the learner writes a program that takes a policy date and a measurement date and reports how much of each cohort's schooling actually fell inside the policy. For a 2026 primary leaver the answer is a small fraction. Then they write the sentence they would be willing to defend, which turns out to be much duller and much more useful than the one they started with.

This is deliberately not a correlation-and-causation exercise in the usual sense. Both things are real and the order is correct. The problem is exposure: the cause was present for only part of the period it is being credited with, and once a learner has seen that once they start asking about it everywhere, including in claims made by companies like this one.

The question parents ask first

## What should a child learn now that AI writes the code?

The Dutch framework answers this without setting out to. There is no domain called programming. There is a domain called **designing and making**, and computational thinking runs through all three.

Give an AI the two dates from the project above and ask whether the curriculum worked, and it will produce a balanced, readable, professional paragraph. Ask it directly and it may even mention that correlation is not causation, because that phrase is everywhere in its training. What it will not reliably do is notice the specific thing that matters here: that a 2026 primary leaver was barely exposed to a 2024 policy, so the question is close to unanswerable with the data given. That takes holding the cohort in your head, and nobody prompted for it.

Deciding what to build, working out which comparison is even valid, and refusing to publish a conclusion the numbers do not support. That is the work. It is learnable, it is what the project trains, and it becomes more valuable as generated prose gets more fluent.

## Questions Dutch families ask before booking

Including two answers that are against our own interest: wait a year, and no we have no office here.

### Is digital literacy compulsory in Dutch schools yet?

The core objectives were finalised in March 2024, after the Ministry of Education, Culture and Science asked SLO to develop them in 2022. Before that there was no compulsory digital literacy or informatics in primary or lower secondary education at all. What that means in practice is that provision depends on your school and on how far it has got with implementation, so the honest answer to what your child is already covering is that you would have to ask them.

### What are the three domains, and which one does a coding class touch?

Nine core objectives grouped into three domains: practical knowledge and skills, designing and making, and the interaction between digital technology, media, people and society. A coding class sits squarely in the second one. Designing and making is what a learner does here every week, and it is the domain that needs the most contact time and the most equipment, which is exactly why schools find it the hardest to deliver.

### Where does computational thinking fit in?

Dutch digital literacy is defined as ICT skills, media literacy, information literacy and computational thinking together, with computational thinking running through all of them rather than sitting in a box of its own. That is a good definition and it matches how we teach: the reasoning is the subject and the code is how you practise it.

### What time are classes, and does the time difference change?

It does change, and this is the market where we are least willing to promise a fixed slot. The Netherlands moves between CET and CEST, while Indian Standard Time never shifts, so India is four and a half hours ahead in winter and three and a half in summer. Weekend slots and late-afternoon Dutch slots are the easiest to arrange, weekday evenings are agreed case by case, and every time is confirmed on the free call before any payment.

### How much do coding classes in the Netherlands cost?

The first class is free. Group tuition is USD 100 per month for two live lessons each week, normally eight lessons monthly, in a group of five to eight compatible learners. Private tuition is USD 150 per month for one learner with one teacher on the same usual frequency. There is no enrolment fee, nothing to buy and no minimum term.

### Do you have a location in the Netherlands?

No. No Dutch office, branch or classroom is claimed. Every lesson is live online from India. A learner needs a laptop or desktop, stable internet, working audio and a current browser. The phone number on this page is Indian, and email or WhatsApp are the practical ways to reach us.

### Is coding still worth learning now that AI writes it?

The Dutch curriculum answers this without meaning to. It does not have a domain called programming. It has a domain called designing and making, and it threads computational thinking through everything else. Deciding what to build and judging whether the result is right is the part that survives, because a generated answer arrives with the same confidence whether it is correct or not.

Probably not, and we would rather say so. The year a child is being placed into VMBO, HAVO or VWO is a poor year to add a new commitment, and nothing we teach affects that decision. ### Do you teach in English, and is that a problem here?

Classes are in English. In the Netherlands that is rarely the obstacle it might be elsewhere, and for learners at bilingual or international schools it is an advantage. Programming keywords are English regardless of the language of instruction. The teacher slows down on terminology rather than assuming it.
