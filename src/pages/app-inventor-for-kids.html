MIT App Inventor for kids

App Inventor projects tend to start with a specific idea: a quiz for a younger brother, a soccer score keeper, a button that makes the family dog bark. This guide covers what parents ask before starting: how a child under 13 signs in, which devices can test an app, why the phone sometimes refuses to connect, and what a first proper app looks like, with a dice game written in App Inventor blocks that you can build yourself.

In short

MIT App Inventor is a free, web-based tool from MIT for building phone apps with blocks. Kids design screens in the Designer, add behavior in the Blocks Editor, and test live on an iPhone, Android phone or Chromebook through the free Companion app. It works with a Google account, or without one through code.appinventor.mit.edu and a revisit code. Modern Age Coders teaches this live online for US students, in a batch or one to one, and the first class is free.

What it is

What MIT App Inventor is, and who uses it

App Inventor comes from a small team at the MIT Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory (CSAIL). Its own FAQ describes it as a free, web-based platform for creating, testing, and sharing mobile apps.

MIT App Inventor in numbers, from its FAQ and 2025 User Survey (read 17 October 2026)
PointWhat App Inventor says
People who code apps with it"Over 24 million people code their own apps with App Inventor"

US schools in that month"over 900 US schools in 50 states"

Two windows, one app

Every App Inventor project has two halves. The Designer is where a child drags components onto a phone-shaped screen: buttons, labels, images, and invisible ones such as a sound player or a motion sensor. The Blocks Editor is where they decide what happens when someone taps, shakes or types.

MIT’s own first tutorial calls components the ingredients in a recipe. We find that comparison works well with eight-year-olds: the Designer is the shopping list, the blocks are the method.

Is it real coding?

The FAQ asks this question itself and answers it with who uses the tool: engineers and research scientists who normally work in Java, Python and Swift use App Inventor to build working apps quickly.

Our view, from teaching it: the blocks hide spelling and punctuation, not thinking. Events, variables, conditions, loops and lists are all there, and a child who builds three App Inventor apps arrives at typed code already knowing what an event handler is for.

Sources: the MIT App Inventor FAQ and the HelloPurr tutorial, read 17 October 2026. The FAQ’s figures are App Inventor’s own; we have not added them up or checked them. We are not connected with MIT.

Accounts

How a child under 13 signs in to App Inventor

This is where many American families get stuck, because the usual sign-in is a Google account and children have their own rules on Google. There are three routes.

Three ways in (from the App Inventor FAQ and terms, and Google’s age page, read 17 October 2026)
RouteWho it suitsWhat to know
A personal Google accountTeens of 13 and overGoogle says 13 is the minimum age to manage your own account in every country not on its exception list; the United States is not on that list (our reading)
A Google account managed with Family LinkChildren under 13Google says parents can help create and manage a younger child’s account with Family Link
A school Google accountStudents whose district uses GoogleGoogle notes its consumer age rules may not apply to Workspace for Education accounts; the school sets the rules
No account, at code.appinventor.mit.eduAnyone, including classesChoose "Continue Without An Account" and keep the random revisit code; without it the project cannot be found again

The revisit code, explained

The no-account route gives each user a randomly generated code. App Inventor says that if the user wants to return to the project later, they must copy that code and keep it somewhere safe, because otherwise projects cannot be retrieved after logging out.

Our tip for families: write the code on paper and put it in a phone note as well. Without that code, the project cannot be found again.

What App Inventor keeps

The FAQ says App Inventor does not keep personally identifiable information and keeps Google account details only so that project files can be saved. Its terms add that projects stay private unless you share them, and that young people especially should be careful about putting personal information on the site.

For schools, the terms describe the code.appinventor.mit.edu version as one that does not collect or keep personally identifiable information, which is why many US teachers use it.

Signed in and the projects have vanished? The FAQ says this is almost always because you are logged in to a different Google account from the one that created them. Check the address shown at the top right before anything else.

Testing on a device

Four ways to test an app, and the one MIT recommends

Building happens in the browser on a Mac, Windows computer or Chromebook. Testing happens on a device, live, while the child is still changing blocks. MIT’s setup page lists four options.

Test options, from the App Inventor setup pages (read 17 October 2026)
OptionWhat you needMIT’s note
1. Phone or tablet over Wi-FiAn iPhone, Android phone, iPad or Android tablet, plus the free Companion appRecommended; the easiest way
2. ChromebookA Chromebook that can run Android appsBuild, test and run on the same device
3. Emulator on the computerEmulator software installed on a Mac or PCUnder Revision: Not Recommended
4. USB cableAn Android device and a cableAndroid only; for networks that block the Wi-Fi link
  1. Install the Companion once. Get the MIT App Inventor Companion from the App Store or Google Play. It stays on the phone for every future project.
  2. Put both devices on the same Wi-Fi. App Inventor shows the app on the phone only when the computer and the phone are on the SAME network, in MIT’s capitals.
  3. Connect from the project. In the browser, choose Connect, then AI Companion. A QR code and a code of six characters appear.
  4. Scan or type. In the Companion, scan the QR code, or type the six characters and press the orange Connect with code button, without pressing Enter.
  5. Watch it update. Within a few seconds the app appears on the phone and changes as blocks change. MIT calls this live testing.
  6. If nothing appears, update the Companion first, since an outdated version is the first problem MIT lists, then check that both devices really are on the same network.

iPhone and iPad

App Inventor has worked with iPhones and iPads since March 2021, and MIT says almost all apps in its tutorial list work in the iOS Companion. A few features are still missing on iOS, among them Bluetooth, the Charts and Spreadsheets components and Android extensions.

Turning an app into a permanent iPhone app file is harder, because Apple requires a paid developer account and signing; MIT says that packaging process is currently in a closed beta.

Keeping an app on the phone

The simplest way to keep a finished app works on both iPhone and Android: while it runs in the Companion, tap the book icon at the top right. It is then saved in the Companion’s library and opens offline, with nothing to install.

On Android you can also build an installable .apk file that runs on its own. Building still needs the internet, because App Inventor itself runs in the browser.

A first real app

Build a shake-to-roll dice app in App Inventor blocks

HelloPurr, MIT’s classic first app, plays a meow when you tap a kitten. Our next step for students is a dice that rolls when the phone is shaken, speaks the result and remembers how many rolls there have been. The blocks are ours, written with components from the App Inventor reference; build them and shake your own phone.

The idea underneath: events change state

Nothing in App 1 runs until the phone is shaken. Shaking is the event; the blocks inside it are the handler; the global roll is the state that the handler changes. That pattern runs nearly every app on a phone.

App 2 adds the harder idea: state that outlives the app. Children who understand why the count resets in App 1 and survives in App 2 have understood what a database is for.

Two things that surprise students

One hard shake can count as several. The AccelerometerSensor has a MinimumInterval property, in milliseconds, that ignores further Shaking events until the time has passed; raising it is the fix.

While testing, every app run in the same Companion shares one TinyDB, so a count from yesterday’s project can appear in today’s. The reference says this sharing disappears once apps are packaged, and suggests clearing the Companion’s data when starting a new app.

A quick check with arithmetic, ours: a fair dice shows a 6 one time in six on average, so in 60 shakes you would expect about 60 / 6 = 10 sixes. Ask your child to shake sixty times, count, and explain why the answer is rarely exactly 10. That is probability, taught by their own app.

At school

App Inventor in US classrooms, including phone-free schools

With over 900 US schools in all 50 states using it in a single month, according to App Inventor’s 2025 survey, many children meet it in class first. Two school questions come up most.

When phones are banned

The App Inventor FAQ answers the phone-ban case directly. Students can test on a Chromebook in a second window, on an iPad, on an Android tablet shared by a team of two to four, on the free emulator (which cannot reach some sensors and Bluetooth), or on a Mac with Apple Silicon that runs the Companion on the desktop.

Coding on a touchscreen alone is possible on an iPad or Android tablet, but MIT warns it can be tricky and suggests trying it before a class depends on it.

App Inventor and AP CSP

For high schools, the FAQ points teachers who want App Inventor as an official course to the "AP board certified Mobile CSP", an AP Computer Science Principles course built on it.

If your teen is heading toward AP CSP, our page on AP Computer Science Principles coaching covers the exam itself, and App Inventor apps make good practice for the course’s programming work.

For parents

The Wi-Fi connection step is where younger children need an adult. After that, testing is something a child can learn to do alone.

Ask to use the app. A child who watches someone else get confused by their screen learns more about design than any lesson.

For kids

Make the app small and finished before you make it big. A dice that works beats a game that almost works.

Name your components. LabelRoll is easier to find in the blocks than Label7.

Questions parents ask

MIT App Inventor for kids: questions

What is MIT App Inventor?

A free, web-based tool from MIT for building phone apps with blocks. You design the screen in the Designer, add behavior in the Blocks Editor, and test on a real phone through the Companion app.

Is MIT App Inventor free?

Yes. App Inventor and its Companion app are free. MIT says the site is supported through grants, collaborations with educational organizations and donations.

What age is App Inventor for?

MIT says people from ages 4 to 84 use it every month. We teach it from about 8, when children can read the blocks comfortably; in our experience it suits ages 9 to 14 especially well.

Does my child need a Google account?

No. App Inventor can be used without one at code.appinventor.mit.edu by choosing Continue Without An Account. Keep the revisit code it gives you, because without it the project cannot be found again after logging out.

Can a child under 13 use a Google account for App Inventor?

Google says 13 is the minimum age to manage your own Google Account in countries not on its list of exceptions, and the US is not on that list. A parent can create and manage a younger child's account with Family Link, or the child can use a school account or the no-account route.

Does App Inventor work on iPhone?

Yes. MIT says App Inventor has worked with iPhones and iPads since March 2021. Kids test live with the iOS Companion app. Packaging a permanent iPhone app is harder because Apple requires a paid developer account.

Can you use App Inventor on a Chromebook?

Yes. App Inventor runs in the browser on a Chromebook, and many Chromebooks can also run the Android Companion, so a child can build and test on the same machine.

Why won't my phone connect to App Inventor?

Check that the computer and phone are on the same Wi-Fi network and that the Companion app is up to date. Some school networks block the connection; on Android a USB cable is the fallback.

Where did my App Inventor projects go?

Usually you are signed in with a different Google account from the one that made them, since projects are attached to that account's email address. With the no-account route, you need the revisit code.

What times are classes for US families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible. Choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

How much are app-making classes?

The first lesson is free. After that, families pay for teaching one month at a time, and the fees section shows the monthly figure in US dollars.
