Eureka Math homework help for parents

A worksheet asks your second grader to "solve using a number bond" and you are not sure what one is. Or your fourth grader has been told not to "just" use the method you learned. Eureka Math homework confuses many families, not because it is especially hard but because it is written in a visual language parents were never taught. The good news is that the language is small and consistent: Great Minds, the publisher, says students use the same models from grade to grade. Learn those models and most homework nights get calmer. This guide explains them, with worked examples of our own.

What it is

What is Eureka Math, and how is it related to EngageNY?

Eureka Math is published by Great Minds. Many families first met its lessons as the free EngageNY modules from New York State. There is now also a newer edition, Eureka Math squared (written Eureka Math²).

Eureka Math, Eureka Math squared and EngageNY (greatminds.org and the NYSED EngageNY page, checked 31 October 2026)

Why the homework looks the way it does

Great Minds says Eureka Math has a "focus on fewer topics centered on the major work of the grade band", and that it aims for an understanding of the why behind the numbers, not only the how.

For Eureka Math squared it adds: "This spiraling curriculum helps students build, revisit, and retain knowledge over time." That is why a model from last year turns up again in this week’s homework.

Which version is my child using?

Look at the cover of the student book or the name on the school’s parent letter. Eureka Math squared is printed with a small raised 2. If the worksheet footer mentions EngageNY or a New York module, it comes from the original modules.

The models are close enough that everything on this page applies to all three.

The models

What is a number bond, and what other models will I see?

A number bond shows a whole split into parts. Great Minds describes it as a model "used throughout grades K-5". The table below is our plain-English glossary of models families commonly meet on elementary homework; your child’s tip sheet names the exact ones each topic uses.

A parent’s glossary of math models (our descriptions)
ModelWhat it looks likeWhat it is for
Number bondA circle for the whole joined to circles for its partsSeeing that 13 is 10 and 3, or 8 is 5 and 3
Ten-frameA two-by-five grid of dotsPicturing numbers to 10 and making ten
Number lineA marked line, sometimes with jumps drawn as arcsCounting on, comparing, placing fractions
Place value chartColumns for hundreds, tens and ones (later tenths)Regrouping, decimals, why digits move
Bar or tape drawingRectangles split into equal unitsWord problems: parts, wholes and comparisons
Area modelA rectangle split by place valueMulti-digit multiplication and division

Why so many models?

Great Minds’ family page asks the question directly: "Why do children need to learn multiple strategies for solving problems?" Its answer is that a model like the number bond is "another tool for understanding mathematical concepts and problem-solving."

In our experience the models matter most for word problems. A child who can draw a bar diagram can usually solve a problem they could not solve by guessing which operation to use.

Is the standard method banned?

No. The grade 4 Module 1 title itself includes "Algorithms for Addition and Subtraction", and the standard methods appear in later modules too. The models come first so the method makes sense when it arrives.

If your child’s teacher asks for a model, use the model on that assignment. On your own practice at home, it is fine to show the method you know as a way of checking.

Worked examples

How do you solve Eureka-style problems with the models?

Five worked examples written by us, on topics from the modules above. None are copied from Eureka Math lessons or homework; they show the kind of thinking the homework asks for.

Worked examples by grade (ours)
GradeProblemModelWorked reasoning
18 + 5Number bond, make tenSplit 5 into 2 and 3. 8 + 2 = 10, then 10 + 3 = 13.
246 + 38Tens and ones40 + 30 = 70 and 6 + 8 = 14, so 70 + 14 = 84.
3A class collects 48 cans. Group A collects three times as many as Group B. How many does each group collect?Bar drawingB is one unit, A is three units: four equal units make 48, so one unit is 12. Group B 12, Group A 36.
3Where does 3/4 go between 0 and 1?Number lineSplit the space from 0 to 1 into 4 equal parts. Count three parts from 0.
51/2 + 1/3Area or bar modelCut both into sixths: 1/2 = 3/6 and 1/3 = 2/6, so the sum is 5/6.

Make ten, step by step

The number bond for 8 + 5 works because 8 is two short of 10. The child asks "what does 8 need to make 10?", takes that 2 from the 5 and has 3 left over. Ten and three is 13.

The same move later solves 38 + 5 (make 40, then 43) and 298 + 7 (make 300, then 305). That is the payoff of using one model for years.

Checking the bar drawing

For the cans problem, a parent can check two ways: 36 + 12 = 48, and 36 is three times 12. If either check fails, the units were drawn unequal.

Children often draw Group A’s bar the same length as Group B’s. Ask "how many of B’s bars fit inside A’s?" before they start.

Homework night

How can parents help with Eureka Math homework?

Start with the free resources Great Minds publishes for families. Then use a short routine so homework does not become an argument about methods.

Free family resources from Great Minds (family engagement page, checked 31 October 2026)
ResourceWhat Great Minds says it isGrades
Parent Tip SheetsTopic overviews with strategies, models, key vocabulary and home tips, "arranged in the same sequence as the student homework"K to 8, English and Spanish
Homework Helpers"step-by-step explanations of how (and why!) to work problems similar to those found in your child’s Eureka Math homework assignments"K to 12 in English; Spanish digital K to 8
Grade RoadmapsThey "bridge the gap between the way math was once taught and the techniques used in Eureka Math"K to 7, English and Spanish
Card gamesRules for fluency games with a Eureka deck or a standard deck of playing cardsElementary

Great Minds also says: "There is a Homework Helper to go with every homework assignment in the curriculum." If your school has not mentioned them, ask the teacher which version and module your child is on, then find the matching Helper.

  1. Find the topic. The worksheet footer usually names the module and lesson. Read that topic’s tip sheet first.
  2. Ask your child to explain the model. "Show me how the number bond works" is a better start than "show me your answer".
  3. Do one problem together, one alone. If the second goes well, step back.
  4. Check with your own method, quietly. If both agree, the model is probably right.
  5. Stop at 20 to 30 minutes for younger children. Write a note to the teacher about where it broke down; that is useful information for them.

Source: Great Minds, Eureka Math family engagement, checked 31 October 2026. The routine is our advice.

Questions from parents

Eureka Math homework help: common questions

Can you help my child with Eureka Math homework online?

Yes. A live teacher follows the module your child is on and teach the same models the homework uses. Classes run as a batch of 15 to 20 or one to one.

What is a number bond in Eureka Math?

A number bond shows a whole number split into parts, such as 13 as 10 and 3. Great Minds says it is used throughout grades K to 5, often to make ten when adding.

Is EngageNY the same as Eureka Math?

The EngageNY math modules were the free New York State version of the same lessons. The New York State Education Department stopped supporting the EngageNY website on July 7, 2022, and keeps the files in an archive.

What is the difference between Eureka Math and Eureka Math squared?

Eureka Math squared is the newer Great Minds edition, described as a PreK to Algebra I curriculum. Its models stay consistent across grades, so the help on this page applies to both.

Where can I find Eureka Math homework help for free?

Great Minds publishes free Parent Tip Sheets, Grade Roadmaps and Homework Helpers on its family engagement page. It says there is a Homework Helper for every homework assignment in the curriculum.

Why can't my child just use the standard method?

Eureka Math teaches models before the standard method so the method makes sense later. The standard algorithms do appear, for example in the grade 4 Module 1 title. On an assignment that asks for a model, use the model.

How do I find which module my child is on?

The worksheet footer usually names the module and lesson. If not, ask the teacher; the Parent Tip Sheets follow the same order as the homework.

Will you do the homework for my child?

No. We teach the model and the idea, then set similar problems of our own. The homework remains your child's work.

When are classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

Are you part of Great Minds or EngageNY?

No. We are an independent online school. Eureka Math is a trademark of Great Minds, and we quote only its public pages.
