Euclid Contest preparation, 2027

The Euclid Contest is the senior paper of the University of Waterloo's Centre for Education in Mathematics and Computing, written on one spring morning by final-year secondary students and CÉGEP students, and by many younger ones who want a look at it early. It is the CEMC paper that most clearly rewards writing: ten questions over two and a half hours, where the later answers are only worth their marks if the reasoning is on the page. This guide is built from CEMC's Euclid page for 2027, the cover of the 2025 Euclid booklet, CEMC's 2026 results booklet and its preparation advice. It shows how the 100 marks are earned, how far into the paper students got in 2026, a complete written solution of ours taken apart line by line, five two-part practice problems, and what the University of Waterloo says about the Euclid in its own words.

The short version

The Euclid Contest is CEMC's senior math contest for students in their final year of secondary school or at CÉGEP: ten questions, two and a half hours, 100 marks, mixing short answers with full written solutions, written at school on Tuesday, April 6, 2027 in the Americas. Entry is through a school. Modern Age Coders prepares students for it live online, in a batch or one to one, in its Waterloo Math Contests course, Gauss to Euclid.

A change for 2027 schools should know about

CEMC has marked a new shipping rule on its Euclid page: the question booklets "will NOT be shipped to the schools". Instead, teachers download and print them from CEMC's Contest Material Repository "roughly one day before contest day in their timezone".

Solution booklets and Student Information Forms still arrive by post, and the finished papers go back to CEMC in one package for marking. Nothing in this changes what a student does on the day.

Who counts as an official participant

For a student writing at a school in Canada, CEMC counts an official participant as one who completes the Student Information Form, "were born on or after January 1, 2008, and" "live in Canada."

Official status decides inclusion on honour rolls and in a school's team score. CEMC says unofficial participants are still marked and still eligible for certificates and medals.

The 100 marks

How a Euclid paper is marked: boxes, then arguments

The front cover of the 2025 Euclid booklet sets out the marking in a few lines, and those lines explain how the paper should be written. The quotations below are from that cover; the 2027 booklet may word them differently.

Euclid marking, quoted from the cover of CEMC's 2025 Euclid Contest booklet
Part typeWhat the cover says
Every question"Number of questions: 10" and "Each question is worth 10 marks"
Short answer parts"worth 3 marks each"; "full marks given for a correct answer which is placed in the box"; "part marks awarded only if relevant work is shown in the space provided"
Full solution parts"worth the remainder of the 10 marks for the question"; "marks awarded for completeness, clarity, and style of presentation"; "a correct solution poorly presented will not earn full marks"
Form of answers"Express answers as simplified exact numbers except where otherwise indicated."
How to write"Use mathematical statements and words to explain all of the steps of your solution." "Work out some details in rough on a separate piece of paper before writing your finished solution."

What that means in practice

A short-answer part is a box: the right answer in the box earns all 3 marks, whatever the working looks like. A wrong answer earns part marks only if relevant work is shown, so a line or two of working is insurance, not decoration.

A full-solution part is an essay in miniature. A student who writes only the final number to a full-solution part has done the least valuable part of the job.

Calculators do not write solutions

CEMC's calculator page is direct: on contests with full-solution questions, "other mathematical steps must be shown and justified in your written solutions and specific marks may be allocated for these steps."

So a root found on a calculator is still a root that has to be found on paper. "Simplified exact numbers" also rules out a decimal from the screen where a surd or a fraction is expected.

CEMC's own Euclid page says the same thing in one sentence: each submitted solution "is assessed for completeness, clarity, and presentation style; a correct solution, poorly presented, will not earn a full score for that problem." The model solution further down this page is our attempt to show what that sentence looks like on paper.

A complete written solution

What a full-marks solution looks like: one of ours, taken apart

Below is a Euclid-style full-solution question we wrote and the complete solution our teachers would hope to see. It is our problem and our solution, not CEMC's, and the notes after it explain what each part of the write-up is doing for the marker.

Why each part of the model solution is there (our notes, written the way we mark in class)
Part of the write-upWhat it does for the marker
The identity n2 + 20 = (n - 2)(n + 2) + 24States the key idea at once, in a line anyone can check by expanding
"Because n - 2 is an integer..."Gives the reason the first term is divisible, instead of leaving the marker to supply it
Both directions, "if" and "conversely"Shows the condition is exactly right: every n found works, and no n is missed. This is the step most first drafts leave out
"n + 2 is at least 3"Explains why the divisors 1 and 2 are thrown away, so the list is complete for a reason, not by luck
The check with n = 22Optional, but a quick sign that the answer was tested
The final sentenceAnswers the question as asked, in words, so the marker never has to hunt for the result

The draft that loses marks

A typical first attempt tries n = 1, 2, 3 and so on up to 10, finds that 1, 2, 4, 6 and 10 work, and writes "the answer is 1, 2, 4, 6, 10". It misses 22, and even with 22 added it gives no reason why there are no more.

On a short-answer box that might earn part marks. On a full-solution part it shows the student found a pattern, not that the pattern is complete, and that is the difference CEMC's marking is designed to see.

CEMC's own advice on writing

CEMC's General Preparation Advice for the CSMC and Euclid gives short rules we use in class: "Use words! You do not need to use full sentences, but words can make things clearer." and "If you introduce variables, be clear about what those variables represent."

It also suggests separating rough work from the solution itself. The model above is what is left after the rough work: every line is there because the argument needs it.

The lesson behind this page is that a solution is written for a stranger. The marker has never met the student and cannot ask what they meant. Our students hand in one written solution a week from the start of the season, and get it back marked against the three words CEMC uses: completeness, clarity, presentation.

Five two-part problems

Five Euclid-style practice problems, with solutions

Each of these is our practice problem, not a CEMC problem. We wrote them for students preparing for the Euclid; none is taken from or modelled on a past paper. Like the contest, most come in parts, with part (a) a short answer and part (b) either a harder short answer or a full solution. They climb roughly as questions 1 to 7 of a Euclid paper do. All answers were checked in Python.

Problem 1. Our practice problem, not a CEMC problem. (a) What is the value of log2 48 - log2 3? (b) Determine all real numbers x for which log3(x + 6) + log3 x = 3.

Solution. (a) log2 48 - log2 3 = log2(48 / 3) = log2 16 = 4. (b) Both logarithms must be defined, so x > 0. Combining, log3(x(x + 6)) = 3, so x(x + 6) = 27, giving x2 + 6x - 27 = 0 and (x + 9)(x - 3) = 0. The root x = -9 is rejected because log3 x is not defined there, so x = 3. Check: log3 9 + log3 3 = 2 + 1 = 3. The rejected root is where marks are won or lost: writing "x = 3 or x = -9" is an incomplete answer.

Problem 2. Our practice problem, not a CEMC problem. (a) In triangle ABC, the angle at C is 90 degrees, sin A = 5/13 and AB = 26. What is the length of BC? (b) Determine all angles t with 0 ≤ t ≤ 360 degrees for which 2 sin2 t = 3 cos t. Full solution.

Solution to (a). BC is opposite angle A and AB is the hypotenuse, so BC / AB = sin A = 5/13, giving BC = 26 x 5/13 = 10.

Problem 3. Our practice problem, not a CEMC problem. (a) The first three terms of a geometric sequence are 6, x and 24, and x > 0. What is x? (b) An arithmetic sequence has first term a and common difference d. The sum of its first 10 terms is 155 and the sum of its first 20 terms is 610. Determine a and d.

Solution. (a) In a geometric sequence the ratio x / 6 equals 24 / x, so x2 = 144, and since x > 0, x = 12. (b) The sum of the first n terms is (n/2)(2a + (n - 1)d). For n = 10: 5(2a + 9d) = 155, so 2a + 9d = 31. For n = 20: 10(2a + 19d) = 610, so 2a + 19d = 61. Subtracting, 10d = 30, so d = 3, and then 2a = 31 - 27 = 4, so a = 2. Check: the first 10 terms run from 2 to 29, and 10 x (2 + 29) / 2 = 155.

Problem 4. Our practice problem, not a CEMC problem. (a) When p(x) = x3 + kx - 6 is divided by x - 2, the remainder is 8. What is k? (b) The roots of x2 - 7x + c = 0 are r and s, and r2 + s2 = 29. What is c?

Solution. (a) By the remainder theorem the remainder is p(2) = 8 + 2k - 6 = 2 + 2k. Setting 2 + 2k = 8 gives k = 3. (b) The roots satisfy r + s = 7 and rs = c. Then r2 + s2 = (r + s)2 - 2rs = 49 - 2c, and 49 - 2c = 29 gives c = 10. Check: x2 - 7x + 10 = (x - 2)(x - 5), and 4 + 25 = 29. Neither part needs the roots themselves, which is the point of both.

Problem 5. Our practice problem, not a CEMC problem. (a) What is the distance from the origin to the line 3x + 4y = 20? (b) The points A(1, 3) and B(7, -5) are given. Determine all points P on the x-axis for which angle APB is 90 degrees. Give exact coordinates.

Solution. (a) The distance from (0, 0) to 3x + 4y - 20 = 0 is |0 + 0 - 20| / √(32 + 42) = 20 / 5 = 4. (b) Angle APB is 90 degrees exactly when P lies on the circle with diameter AB (with P different from A and B). The centre is the midpoint of AB, (4, -1), and the radius is half of AB: AB = √(62 + 82) = 10, so the radius is 5. The circle is (x - 4)2 + (y + 1)2 = 25. On the x-axis y = 0, so (x - 4)2 = 24 and x = 4 ± 2√6. The points are (4 + 2√6, 0) and (4 - 2√6, 0). The exact form matters: "Express answers as simplified exact numbers", so 8.90 and -0.90 would not do.

Using the set

Problems 1 to 3 are the pace of Euclid questions 1 to 4: aim for about ten minutes each, written up properly. Problems 4 and 5 sit nearer questions 5 to 7.

Write problem 2(b) and problem 5(b) as full solutions, then compare them with ours line by line. Every line of ours that you skipped is a line to ask about.

What links the five

Each has a moment where the obvious answer is incomplete: a root to reject, an impossible cosine, a positive ratio, an identity that avoids solving, an exact value instead of a decimal.

The Euclid rewards students who notice those moments and say so in writing. That habit, more than any formula, is what our classes build.

Calculus is not on the list

CEMC's General Preparation Advice says the CSMC and Euclid "use ideas from all areas of high school mathematics except for calculus and vectors." CEMC also notes that the topics "are not posted in any particular order" and that contest problems often mix them.

For an Ontario student, that means MHF4U, Advanced Functions, matters far more to the Euclid than MCV4U, Calculus and Vectors, although many Euclid writers take both.

Beyond the school course

CEMC says some problems "might require students to use knowledge beyond the curriculum in their school." In our experience that usually means a counting or number theory idea, or a classical geometry result, rather than new topics.

CEMC itself points students who need a review to its free courseware, naming the Grades 9/10/11 and Advanced Functions and Pre-Calculus material as the most helpful.

The last ten minutes are the ones students most often give away. Read in a hurry, a full solution usually looks finished. Read slowly, it often turns out to skip the "conversely" step, a rejected root or the closing sentence, and each of those can be put right in under a minute.

What the day means for a student

The paper starts in the morning, at school, so the student needs the usual school-day routine, not a late night of revision. The question booklet is printed at the school, so there is nothing to collect beforehand.

The paper is written on paper only, individually. Bring the permitted calculator, pencils and an eraser, and nothing that stores notes.

Our classes in April 2027

Eastern clocks move forward on 14 March 2027, three weeks before the contest. We confirm class times with every family after that change, because Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Alberta and British Columbia do not move.

The final class before 6 April is a short review on a weekday evening, never a long session the night before.

Entry and integrity

Entering the Euclid, and the rule every writer should know

A student writes the Euclid through a school, at that school, under supervision. The school's order is due by Thursday, March 11, 2027.

  1. Ask early in the final year. Find out from the head of math whether the school orders the Euclid, and tell them your child intends to write.
  2. If the school has no CEMC account, its head of math or principal applies for one, and CEMC reviews applications by hand over as long as three weeks.
  3. If the school will not order, CEMC suggests looking for another school in the area that will let the student sit the paper with its students.
  4. Write on the official date. CEMC says a contest can never be written before its official date, and the Euclid is always written in person.

CEMC's integrity rule carries particular weight for a final-year student. Participants who break the contest rules, CEMC says, "will be disqualified from the contest, will not be considered for CEMC invitational workshops, and any current or future offers of admission to the University of Waterloo may be revoked." We teach students to write every line themselves, in class and on the day, and we mean it: no shortcut is worth that risk. Source: CEMC, How contests work.

The plan

Five blocks from November 2026 to 6 April 2027

Our plan for a final-year student who starts in early November and writes on Tuesday, April 6, 2027. It runs on two live classes a week and one written solution handed in every week from the first block.

  1. Block 1, 2 November to 6 December 2026: logarithms, exponents and sequences. Questions 1 to 4 from two past Euclid papers, untimed. First written solutions marked for completeness, clarity and presentation.
  2. Block 2, 7 December 2026 to 10 January 2027: trigonometry and polynomials. Identities, exact values, the remainder theorem and sums and products of roots. The holiday weeks carry one full-solution question a day, nothing more.
  3. Block 3, 11 January to 14 February 2027: analytic and Euclidean geometry. Circles, distances, similar triangles and circle theorems. One full past paper, timed at 150 minutes, at the end of January. Waterloo lists February 15, 2027 as its form deadline for most programs, at the end of this block, so we keep the load lighter for applicants.
  4. Block 4, 15 February to 21 March 2027: counting, probability and properties of numbers. The later questions: part (a) of questions 8 to 10 from past papers, and model-solution practice like our problem 6. Confirm the school's order before Thursday, March 11, 2027.
  5. Block 5, 22 March to 6 April 2027: rehearsal. Two more full papers under contest conditions, each reviewed line by line, then a last week of rewriting old solutions more clearly rather than learning anything new.

For parents

Most strong students start at stage 1, because school math rarely asks for a full written argument. The move to stage 3 takes months, which is why we start written solutions in the first week.

The Euclid is one morning. What lasts is the ability to explain a piece of mathematics in writing, which first-year university courses ask for at once.

For students

Write the final sentence of every full solution: "Therefore..." It costs five seconds and saves the marker a search.

When a root, an angle or a case is thrown away, say why in the same line.

Final-year families ask

Euclid Contest: questions answered

What is the Euclid Contest?

It is CEMC's senior math contest, from the University of Waterloo, for students in their final year of secondary school or at CÉGEP. It has ten questions, takes two and a half hours, is marked out of 100, and mixes short answers with full written solutions.

When is the Euclid Contest in 2027?

CEMC lists Tuesday, April 6, 2027 for North and South America and Wednesday, April 7, 2027 elsewhere. Schools must order it by Thursday, March 11, 2027.

How is the Euclid marked?

Each question is worth 10 marks. On the 2025 paper, short-answer parts were worth 3 marks each, with full marks for a correct answer in the box, and full-solution parts were worth the rest of the 10, marked for completeness, clarity and style of presentation.

Are calculators allowed on the Euclid?

CEMC says some calculators are permitted, but on full-solution questions the other mathematical steps must still be shown and justified, and specific marks may be given for them. Answers are expected as simplified exact numbers unless a question says otherwise.

Is the Euclid required for University of Waterloo admission?

No. CEMC says participation in the Euclid is not required for admission to Waterloo's Faculty of Mathematics. Waterloo says writing the Euclid or the CSMC is required for some Faculty of Mathematics scholarships. We make no promise about admission or scholarships.

Can a Grade 11 student write the Euclid?

Yes, if the school orders it for them. Younger students who are keen are welcome, says CEMC, and its Euclid page says many students aiming for a strong final-year score write it more than once before then.

What was a good Euclid score in 2026?

CEMC lists a 2026 average of 52.2 out of 100, a Certificate of Distinction cutoff of 66 and a School Medal Champion cutoff of 52, with 23 985 contestants. The question averages fell from 9.5 out of 10 on question 1 to 0.73 on question 10.

Does the Euclid include calculus?

CEMC's preparation advice says the CSMC and Euclid use ideas from all areas of high school math except calculus and vectors. Its eight preparation topics run from exponents and logarithms to properties of numbers.

How do we register for the Euclid?

Through the school. Students cannot register themselves; the school orders the contest from CEMC and the student writes there in person. If the school will not order, CEMC suggests finding another local school that will let the student join.

What times are your Euclid classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. We plan in Eastern time and confirm every class in the family's own time zone, including after the clocks change on 14 March 2027.
