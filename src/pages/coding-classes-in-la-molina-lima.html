Speaking English

Why does a live coding class help with spoken English?

Because Peruvians, on average, understand written English much better than they speak it, and a live class makes you speak. EF's English Proficiency Index 2025 scores Peru 540 in reading and 480 in speaking, a gap of 60 points by our subtraction.

What the table says

Two patterns stand out. Reading is the strongest skill and speaking the weakest, which is what you would expect from English learned mostly in class from books. And the youngest adults EF tested, aged 18 to 20, score lowest of all age groups, 469, while people in their late twenties score highest, 559.

One reading of the second pattern is that many Peruvians get most of their English after school, at university or work. EF does not say why; we only note that the years around leaving school are exactly when a teenager starts to need English for study.

What a live class adds

School English in Peru is a few periods a week: under the CNEB, regular secundaria gives Inglés 3 of its 35 weekly periods, 135 minutes by our calculation at 45 minutes a period. Bilingual schools give much more, but even there speaking time per pupil is limited.

In our classes, the learner explains a plan, reads a line of code aloud, says why it failed, and asks for help, all in English and all for a real reason. Over months, that adds up to a lot of speaking about precise ideas, which is exactly the kind university and work demand.

La Molina in figures

How many children and teenagers live in La Molina?

INEI's 2025 census gives La Molina a total population of 151,360. Among the 143,899 people it counted in person, 8,435 were aged 6 to 11 and 10,681 were aged 12 to 17, so the district has more teenagers than primary-age children.

The numbers

By our own division, teenagers of 12 to 17 make up about 7.4% of the people INEI counted in La Molina and children of 6 to 11 about 5.9%. The largest group is people of 60 and over, 35,219, followed by those of 45 to 59, 30,484.

INEI estimates that a further 7,461 residents were missed by the count, and reports 79,783 women and 71,577 men in the total. We quote those totals as published and never build new ones by adding INEI's groups.

Where the district sits

La Molina lies at the eastern edge of the city, in a valley bordered by Ate, Cieneguilla, Pachacámac, Villa María del Triunfo and Santiago de Surco. For many families, any activity outside the district starts with a drive down one of a few main roads.

Inside a lesson

What does a learner actually say in a live coding class?

More than most parents expect. Here is the shape of a typical hour for a teenager, with the English each part asks for. The structure is ours, and mentors adapt it to the learner.

  1. Recap, five minutes. The learner explains last week's program in two or three sentences: what it does and one thing that went wrong. The mentor listens, then asks one question.
  2. The new idea, ten minutes. The mentor introduces a concept on the shared screen, slowly, and asks the learner to predict what a short piece of code will print before running it. Predicting aloud is the key habit.
  3. Building, thirty minutes. The learner writes code while thinking aloud. When stuck, they describe the problem in English rather than just pointing; half the time, describing it is enough to solve it.
  4. Testing, ten minutes. The learner chooses a case that might break the program, says why, and runs it. This is where the reasoning gets sharp.
  5. Summary, five minutes. One sentence on what was learned, one on what is still unclear. The mentor writes a short note for parents.

For a child of seven the hour is shorter and simpler: "first", "then", "repeat", "if". For an adult, the conversation is closer to a code review at work. At every age, the learner is the one doing most of the talking about their own program, which is rare in a school English lesson.

We teach in English only. A learner who reaches for a Spanish word is understood and offered the English one; nobody is corrected harshly. Our page on learning coding and English together in Peru goes further into how the two skills support each other.

School and IB

Do La Molina pupils get computing or IB computer science at school?

Not from the national curriculum. The CNEB has no computing area in primaria or secundaria. Schools that teach the IB Diploma or Cambridge courses may offer computer science as an examined subject, in English.

National curriculum

The CNEB treats digital skills as a cross-curricular competency that all areas share, so a pupil may use technology in many subjects but is not taught to program in any of them.

Our classes add programming alongside school. They do not replace a subject, they do not award grades, and the school is always the one that decides what a pupil has achieved there.

IB and Cambridge

According to a MINEDU release of 17 April 2026, Peru produced 2,502 IB Diploma holders in 2025, and 515 pupils in the final year at its COAR schools gained Cambridge English certificates the same year. English and international programmes are a real route for Peruvian students.

For Diploma or IGCSE pupils, our courses follow the syllabus in English and leave assessment to the school, the IB or Cambridge. We are not connected with any of them, or with MINEDU or COAR.

A real lesson

What does a lesson look like? A grid that destroys itself

We give this lesson to teenagers of about 13 and up. It uses the Game of Life, a famous set of rules invented by the mathematician John Conway, and it teaches a bug that appears in games, simulations and spreadsheets alike: changing data while you are still reading it.

The rules

A grid of cells, each alive (#) or dead (.). In each step, a live cell with two or three live neighbours survives, and a dead cell with exactly three live neighbours comes to life. Everything else dies or stays dead. All cells change at the same moment.

The learner starts with a "blinker": three live cells in a vertical line. Before writing any code, they say what should happen. The answer: it should turn into a horizontal line, then back again, for ever.

What goes wrong

Version 1 walks over the grid and changes each cell as soon as it has decided. The top cell of the blinker has only one neighbour, so it dies. Then the middle cell counts its neighbours, and finds only one, because its upper neighbour has already been switched off.

One by one, the cells read a grid that is half old and half new. The blinker vanishes completely, and no new cells are born. The rule "all cells change at the same moment" was broken without anyone noticing.

Version 2 never touches the old grid while it is deciding. It counts neighbours on the old grid and writes results into a fresh one, so every cell sees the same "before". The vertical line becomes horizontal, and after a second step it is back where it started, which the program checks for us. Programmers call this keeping two buffers; the habit is read from the old, write to the new.

The same bug turns up far from games: a spreadsheet formula that refers to a cell it has just changed, a program that edits a list while looping over it, a database update that reads its own half-finished work. Once a learner has watched a blinker die for this reason, they recognise the pattern everywhere.

  1. Ages 8 to 10, on squared paper. Draw the blinker and apply the rules by hand, writing the next grid on a new sheet. Then try doing it on the same sheet with an eraser. What goes wrong?
  2. Ages 11 to 13. Run Version 1 and explain, in English, which cell died first and why the next one followed.
  3. Ages 13 to 15. Change Version 2 to run ten steps and print each one. Try a 2 by 2 block of live cells. Why does it never change?
  4. Ages 15 and over. Make the grid wrap around at the edges, so that cells on the right touch cells on the left. Write a test that shows the blinker still returns after two steps.

Coding classes in La Molina: questions and answers

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. All classes are live, online and taught only in English. Mentors talk plainly, tune pace and pronunciation to the person in front of them, and give time to reply, so a learner who reads English well but speaks it hesitantly can take part fully. That speaking practice is one of the benefits.

My teenager reads English well but is shy about speaking. Will this help?

Usually, yes. In a coding class the learner speaks about something concrete, their own program, in short sentences, with a patient mentor. That is easier than open conversation, and it builds confidence quickly. One-to-one classes suit the shyest learners at first.

What age can my child start?

From 6, with Scratch blocks that need little reading or typing. Around 9, typing Python becomes realistic. Older learners go straight to Python or Java.

When are classes in La Molina time?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India is ten and a half hours ahead of Peru on every date, so 16:00 in La Molina is 02:30 the next day in India.

Is there help for IB or IGCSE computer science?

There is. Diploma students at either level and Cambridge 0478 candidates follow their syllabus with us in English. Grades, coursework marks and exam results stay with the school, the IB or Cambridge; our job is understanding.

Do you have premises in La Molina?

No. We have no centre anywhere in Lima. Every class is live over video with a mentor based in India, so nobody has to drive out of the district.

Does my child get to use AI in lessons?

Later, and only what their age allows. A child first works the problem through without help. Claude's terms require users of 18 or more, and ChatGPT expects parental consent under 18, so we keep children away from adult chatbot accounts.

Are you linked to any school, university or the municipality?

No link of any kind: not with the Municipalidad de La Molina, MINEDU, COAR, the IB, Cambridge, a university or a school. Finishing a course earns our own completion certificate, which is not a Peruvian qualification.

How are fees paid?

Monthly, by card, in US dollars. With a card in soles, your bank converts each charge, so the amount on the statement can vary a little. The first class is free and needs no card.
