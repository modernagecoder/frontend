The short version

Modern Age Coders gives live online coding classes in English to children, teenagers and adults in Cusco, Peru. A six year old starts by snapping Scratch blocks into games, children of about nine move to typed Python, secundaria students learn Python, Java, AI and problem solving, and adults take Python, data analysis or AI tools. A real mentor leads every lesson on camera, for a class of fifteen to twenty or a single learner, on the days and at the hours the family picks.

Most of Cusco lives outside the centre

For a child in San Jerónimo, an after-school class in the centre can mean a long trip each way. A live online class starts the moment the computer is on.

Beyond the city

Primaria

What does a Cusco child of 6 to 11 learn at school each week, and where does coding fit?

Primaria lasts six years under the Ley General de Educación, and the national plan of study gives each grade 30 teaching hours a week. Maths and Spanish take the largest share; there is no computing area.

English hours that depend on a teacher

The table promises two or three hours of English a week. The same curriculum adds a condition: English needs a specialist teacher, so "la implementación de esta área será progresiva dependiendo de la contratación de estos docentes". In other words, a school without that teacher may not teach the hours.

This is why many Cusco parents ask whether a child can follow a coding class in English. Our answer is in the next section, and it is usually yes, with patience.

Digital skills, but no coding

Primaria has no computing area. The curriculum lists a digital competency, number 28, but it is transversal: teachers of every area are expected to develop it, and nothing in it asks a child to write a program.

A weekly coding class gives a primaria child what the timetable cannot: an hour of building something that only works if every step is in the right order. It is also, quietly, extra maths: counting, patterns, coordinates and comparing numbers.

  1. Age 6 or 7. Scratch, with blocks and pictures. The child makes a character move, talk and repeat. Reading is helpful but not required.
  2. Age 8. Games with a score, a timer and simple rules. Children start to plan a game before they build it.
  3. Age 9 or 10. The move to typed Python for most children: short programs, drawings with code, and the first error messages read together with the mentor.
  4. Age 11. Small projects with real numbers, like the Cusco puzzle on this page, and a first look at how an AI tool learns from examples.

Taught in English

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. We teach only in English, always live and online, with mentors in India who are used to young learners for whom English is a second language.

How a seven year old copes

Young children follow more than adults expect. In Scratch the mentor points, demonstrates and names: "Drag the blue block. Now press the green flag." After a few weeks the words become familiar because the child uses them every class.

Mentors speak slowly, use the same phrases each week, write key words on screen and wait for an answer. If a child replies in Spanish, the mentor understands the idea and gives back the English words for it.

What the English figures say about Cusco

EF's 2025 English Proficiency Index scores Cusco city 520, in its moderate band. Of the eight Peruvian cities in that index, only Lima scores higher, at 555. The Cusco region as a whole scores 504.

Those figures describe adults who took EF's test, not children. Still, they suggest many Cusco families already use English, and a weekly class adds steady listening and speaking practice. Our page on learning coding and English together explains how.

Secundaria and the AI olympiad

Secundaria in Peru lasts five years, and the regular plan of study gives 35 teaching hours a week with no computing area. Teenagers who want to program learn it outside school.

UTEC runs national olympiads that are free for secundaria students, including the OPI for programming and the OPEIA for artificial intelligence, which was held on 19 March 2026; its 2025 team represented Peru at the international AI olympiad, the IOAI. We are not connected with UTEC and teach with our own problems.

Students and adults

University students in Cusco use our Java, Python and data-structures classes alongside their degree. We teach, explain and give practice; we never write assessed work.

Adults, from young professionals to retirees, usually start with Python and then choose data analysis or AI agents. Some of our learners began in their sixties. Our page on courses for university students in Peru covers the student side.

A real Cusco puzzle

Can the last digit of a number tell you if someone counted it?

Yes, often. The Gobierno Regional de Cusco publishes a register of drinking-water coverage, row by row, for communities across the department. Our learners look at the last digit of each population figure and find out which numbers were counted and which were estimated.

The data

The dataset is "Indicadores de Cobertura en el Servicio de Agua Potable en el Departamento de Cusco 2016 - 2019", published by the Gobierno Regional de Cusco on Peru's open-data portal, datosabiertos.gob.pe, under the Open Data Commons Attribution licence (ODC-By), and last changed on 23 August 2023. Our copy, saved on 9 November 2026, has 4,250 rows from all 13 provinces, most of them from 2018 and 2019.

Each row describes a local water service: the district, the year, the number of people, the number of homes, how many homes are lived in, and how many people have drinking water. We use only the people and homes columns, as a numbers puzzle.

The idea

If people really count every person in a village, the result is as likely to end in 3 or 7 as in 0. Each last digit should turn up about one time in ten.

If instead someone writes "about 250" or "around 1,000", round numbers pile up. So Version 1 takes each population of 10 or more and counts its last digit with people % 10, the remainder after dividing by ten.

Water data: Gobierno Regional de Cusco, drinking-water coverage indicators 2016 to 2019, datosabiertos.gob.pe, ODC-By. Census comparison: the 116 district totals for the department of Cusco in INEI's 2025 census table 1, where 15 end in 0. All percentages are ours.

More than a third of the register's populations end in 0, and another sixth end in 5. The census district totals, which INEI built by counting, look nothing like that: their zeros are close to one in ten. So many of the register's figures were rounded or estimated before they were typed in. That is not a scandal; field staff often estimate. But a careful analyst writes it down before quoting any total from the file.

There is a second clue. Odd digits are rare in the register, and even ones common. One explanation a student can test is that people were worked out from homes, by multiplying. Version 2 checks every row with lived-in homes: is the population an exact multiple of the number of homes?

In 1,739 of 4,177 rows, the population is exactly 3, 4 or 5 times (or another whole number of times) the homes. Some of that happens by chance, but 755 rows at exactly four people per home is far too many to be luck. The file does not say how its figures were made, so we do not claim to know; we can only say that the numbers carry the fingerprints of estimation. The register covers local water services, not whole districts, so none of its rows should be compared with INEI's census figures for Cusco.

  1. Ages 7 to 9, unplugged. Ask ten classmates how many steps there are from the door to their desk. How many answers end in 0? Then count the steps for real.
  2. Ages 9 to 11, in Scratch or Python. Make a list of twenty numbers you invent quickly, and twenty from a real source such as page numbers. Count the last digits of each list.
  3. Ages 12 to 14. Run Version 1 on the homes column, VIVIENDAS_HABITADAS, instead of people. Is it heaped too?
  4. Ages 15 and over. Change Version 2 to print the share of rows at exactly 4 per home in each province. Which province's rows look most estimated?
  5. Adults. Draft the one-paragraph note you would attach to any report that uses this file.

Younger Cusco children often do best early, before school or on a Saturday morning, when they are fresh. Older students tend to choose late afternoon or evening. Coding classes in Cusco: questions and answers

Are the classes in Spanish?

No, never. We teach only in English, live and online. Mentors use short, clear sentences, show as well as tell, and adjust to each child's accent and speed. A young learner who answers in Spanish is understood and hears the English words back.

Can a six year old in Cusco really learn to code?

Yes. At six, children use Scratch blocks that need no typing, and classes are short and practical. They learn order, repetition and simple rules by making a character move, which is coding in its first form.

Will my child follow a class in English if their school English is weak?

Usually yes. Scratch is visual, mentors repeat the same phrases every week and wait for answers, and the free first class shows how comfortable your child is before you decide anything.

Do schools in Cusco teach coding?

Not as a subject. Primaria has 30 hours a week and secundaria 35 under the regular plan, and neither includes a computing area; digital skills are spread across all areas. Coding is learned outside school.

Is there a centre in Cusco I can visit?

No. All our classes are live online, so a child in San Sebastián, Santiago or Wanchaq joins exactly as a child in the historic centre does.

What time are classes in Cusco?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. Cusco keeps Peru time all year; 08:30 in Cusco is 19:00 for a mentor in India.

Do you teach teenagers and adults too?

Yes. Teenagers learn Python, Java, AI and problem solving; university students add data structures; adults take Python, data analysis or AI tools. Every age group has its own classes.

How is the fee charged?

Monthly, in US dollars, the currency this page shows, charged to your card; your bank converts it into soles. Visitors in India see rupees. The trial class is free and no card is needed for it.

Is there a certificate?

Where a course provides one, it is our own Modern Age Coders certificate of completion. It is not from MINEDU, a school or a university, and it is not a Peruvian qualification.
