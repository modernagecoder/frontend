## A year of Ottawa River temperatures, modelled with a cosine curve

The City of Ottawa publishes the daily temperature of raw river water at the Britannia water purification plant. We built a sinusoid from the 2025 column and compared it with every day of that year. The model and its errors are our calculations.

The City&rsquo;s description is unusually exact. Each daily value is the average of two readings, recorded 12 hours apart, around 06:00 and 18:00, using a certified probe accurate to plus or minus half a degree. The intake sits approximately 427 m out from the plant, where 12 reinforced fibreglass bells on the last 29 m of pipe rest at an approximate depth of 10 m, with an intake capacity of 364 ML/day. The workbook we downloaded has one row per calendar day and one column for each year from 2005 to 2025; it has no row for 29 February, so leap days are absent.

**Our figures from the City of Ottawa workbook, 2025 column, downloaded 22 October 2026 (degrees Celsius)**

| Measure | 2025 |
|---|---|
| Warmest day | 26.75 on 13 August (day 225) |
| Coldest day | 0.27 on 17 January (day 17) |
| Mean and median of the 365 days | 11.17 and 9.5 |
| Monthly means, January to March | 0.89, 1.02, 1.15 |
| Monthly means, July and August | 24.87 and 24.76 |
| Days at or above 20 degrees | 102 |
| Days at or below 1 degree | 47 |

From those numbers a Grade 11 student can write a model in four moves. The amplitude is half the range, (26.75 &minus; 0.27) &divide; 2 = 13.24. The midline is the average of the extremes, 13.51. The period is one year, 365 days. And the curve peaks on day 225, so a cosine shifted right by 225 fits: T(d) = 13.24 cos(2&pi;(d &minus; 225) &divide; 365) + 13.51. The program below builds exactly that model from the workbook and measures how far it sits from the real temperatures:

```
import datetime, math
import openpyxl  # pip install openpyxl

sheet = openpyxl.load_workbook("2025_Ottawa_River_water_temperature.xlsx", read_only=True).worksheets[0]
rows = list(sheet.iter_rows(max_row=400, values_only=True))
header = next(i for i, row in enumerate(rows) if row[0] == "Date")
col = rows[header].index(2025)
temps = [row[col] for row in rows[header + 1:] if isinstance(row[0], datetime.datetime)]

amp = (max(temps) - min(temps)) / 2
mid = (max(temps) + min(temps)) / 2
peak_day = temps.index(max(temps)) + 1

def model(day):
    return amp * math.cos(2 * math.pi * (day - peak_day) / 365) + mid

errors = [abs(temps[d - 1] - model(d)) for d in range(1, 366)]
print("amplitude", amp, "midline", mid, "peak on day", peak_day)
print("mean error, degrees:", round(sum(errors) / 365, 2))
```

### How good is it?

Across all 365 days the model is off by 2.49 degrees on average. For a curve built from only two numbers and a date, that is respectable, and it captures the shape of the year.

### Where it fails

In spring the model runs warm: on 1 June it predicts 17.60 degrees against 13.45 measured. The coldest day is 208 days before the warmest, not the 182.5 a symmetric sine wave assumes, so the river warms more slowly than it cools.

### The winter floor

From January to March the monthly means stay between 0.89 and 1.15. River water at the intake cannot cool far below freezing, so the bottom of the curve is flat. A sinusoid has no flat parts, which is a limitation worth naming in any report.

## How fast was the river warming on 1 June 2025?

A calculus question with a twist, for an MHF4U or MCV4U student. The temperatures are the City of Ottawa&rsquo;s 2025 values; the question and solution are ours.

**Selected 2025 daily temperatures from the City of Ottawa workbook (degrees Celsius)**

| Date | 1 May | 17 May | 27 May | 31 May | 1 June | 2 June | 6 June | 16 June |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature | 7.25 | 13.0 | 12.6 | 14.2 | 13.45 | 13.6 | 16.65 | 19.6 |

**Part (a), an average rate.** From 1 May to 1 June the temperature rose from 7.25 to 13.45 degrees over 31 days, an average rate of change of (13.45 &minus; 7.25) &divide; 31 = 0.20 degrees per day.

**Part (b), narrowing in.** MCV4U estimates an instantaneous rate with ever smaller intervals centred on the moment. Using 17 May and 16 June (15 days either side): (19.6 &minus; 13.0) &divide; 30 = 0.22. Using 27 May and 6 June (5 days either side): (16.65 &minus; 12.6) &divide; 10 &asymp; 0.405.

**Part (c), the twist.** Using 31 May and 2 June (1 day either side): (13.6 &minus; 14.2) &divide; 2 = &minus;0.30. The river was not cooling in early June; day-to-day wobble in the measurements, each only accurate to half a degree, swamps the signal when the interval is tiny. With real data, shrinking the interval does not converge the way it does for a formula.

**Part (d), use the model instead.** Differentiate T(d) = 13.24 cos(2&pi;(d &minus; 225) &divide; 365) + 13.51 to get T&prime;(d) = &minus;13.24 &times; (2&pi; &divide; 365) sin(2&pi;(d &minus; 225) &divide; 365). At d = 152, the angle is 2&pi;(&minus;73) &divide; 365 &asymp; &minus;1.257 radians and its sine is about &minus;0.951, so T&prime;(152) &asymp; 13.24 &times; 0.01721 &times; 0.951 &asymp; 0.217 degrees per day: close to the 15-day estimate, and far steadier than the 1-day one.

**Try it yourself.** On which day of the year does the model say the river warms fastest, and what is that rate? Compare it with the real temperatures a week either side of that day.

## Can an Ottawa student hand the river model to AI?

It will fit a curve. It will not tell you the curve is wrong in winter unless you already know to ask.

Give an AI tool the 2025 column and it can return a fitted sinusoid in seconds. It is much less likely to notice the flat winter floor, the lopsided spring, or that a one-day derivative from noisy data is meaningless. Seeing those things is mathematical judgement, and it belongs to the student.

Our lessons keep that judgement where it belongs: sketch the year first, estimate amplitude and period by eye, build the model by hand, and only then let software check. Work handed in for a school mark is the student&rsquo;s own.

Ontario math asks students to model, reason and communicate. Software can draw the curve; deciding whether to trust it is still a person&rsquo;s job. The longer argument is in [why careful thinking still matters when software does the sums](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
