Online coding and AI classes in the Huancavelica region

Huancavelica sits high in the central Andes, with seven provinces and a capital at about 3,700 metres. Its schools are receiving tablets by the thousand, yet most homes still have no computer. Our classes are live, online and taught in English. This page explains where a tablet is enough and where it is not, who in the region is ready for English-taught classes today, and how a university's own data can teach a teenager to report an honest range instead of a tidy but wrong number.

Devices in the region

Can a child in Huancavelica learn to code on a school tablet?

For first steps, yes. For real programming, a keyboard and a bigger screen make the difference. The regional government is handing out tablets in large numbers, and it helps to know what they are good for.

What the region has delivered

On 26 August 2026 the Gobierno Regional de Huancavelica reported that, through the regional education directorate and with PRONATEL, it "entregó 11 mil 907 tabletas electrónicas" to the heads of the region's eight UGELs, for pupils of 443 schools.

The same note splits them into 5,577 tablets for primaria and 6,330 for secundaria, and says the regional broadband project, now in its seventh year of tablet deliveries, has reached a running total of 71,442 devices.

What a tablet can and cannot do for coding

A tablet is well suited to block coding: a child drags pieces, taps run, and watches a character move. It is also fine for joining a video class and following the mentor's screen.

Typed programming is different. A learner writing Python spends most of an hour typing, reading error messages and switching between windows. On a touch screen that becomes slow and tiring, so from about ten we ask for a laptop or desktop, even a shared or second-hand one.

Read across the rows and the pattern is clear. In 2025 roughly a sixth of Huancavelica households owned a computer and fewer than a quarter paid for internet at home, while 69.1 per cent of residents from age six upward went online somewhere, most of them on a phone. Internet use has almost quadrupled since 2015. Home computers have not kept pace. That gap, not interest or ability, is the first thing a family here should think about before signing up for any online programming course, ours included.

Honest fit

Who in Huancavelica is ready for classes taught in English?

We teach only in English, live and online; there are no Spanish or Quechua groups. For some learners in the region that works well straight away, for others it will work better in a year or two.

What we know about English here

EF's English Proficiency Index 2025 gives Peru a score of 519, in its moderate band. EF also scores sixteen Peruvian regions, and Huancavelica is not among them, so there is no regional English figure to quote.

Schools do teach English: the regular secundaria week includes three hours of Inglés. Three hours a week is a foundation rather than fluency, and we plan the first months of classes with that in mind.

Who usually does well

Teenagers in 3° to 5° de secundaria who like maths and are willing to meet English in small steps. University students, especially in engineering, systems and mathematics, where much reading is in English already. Adults who use English words at work and want programming or data skills.

Younger children can learn with us too, mostly one to one, with blocks, short repeated phrases and a parent within reach for the first few weeks.

Huancavelica's pupils are not weak at maths. In MINEDU's 2025 census evaluation, 35.5 per cent of the region's fourth-grade pupils reached the satisfactory level in maths, above the national 30.0 per cent. In second year of secundaria the picture is harder: 11.2 per cent in maths against 12.7 nationally, and 10.8 per cent in reading against 18.5. A learner who reads English slowly can still reason well, and programming rewards reasoning. Spanish-speaking parents who would like to read first can open our guide to clases de programación en inglés en Perú.

Towns, with their own years

INEI's 2017 census table of department capitals gives the city of Huancavelica 49,570 people. In INEI's 2017 listing of centros poblados, the town of Huancavelica stands at 3,746 metres, Lircay had 9,724 residents and Pampas 7,839.

For 2025 INEI reports districts rather than towns: Huancavelica district 40,240, Lircay 29,006, Yauli 22,244, Acoria 18,774, Ascensión 14,436 and Pampas 9,895. A district and a town are different areas, so we print the figures side by side and never subtract one from the other.

School-age learners

Among the people the 2025 census actually counted in the department (362,744), 38,323 were aged 6 to 11 and 40,087 were aged 12 to 17, the two age bands that match primaria and secundaria.

Public schools have fewer pupils than a decade ago. INEI's compendium shows public primaria enrolment in the department falling from 51.6 thousand in 2015 to 38.5 thousand in 2025, and public secundaria from 45.7 thousand to 34.4 thousand. The region had 11,704 teachers in 2025.

Most of the region's people live far from any classroom that teaches programming. For a teenager in Churcampa or Castrovirreyna with a connection and a laptop at home, a live online class is one of very few ways to study coding with a person, rather than alone with videos.

Connection

Is the internet in Huancavelica good enough for a live class?

In the towns, usually. In smaller places it depends on the locality, and the regional government says so itself.

What is being built

A regional government note from the first national technical forum of regional governments on telecommunications, held in Huancavelica city, describes a broadband extension "en beneficio de 412 localidades de las siete provincias", with connections of up to 200 Mbps over fibre to the home or institution, replacing older microwave links.

The same note says that the regional broadband projects were designed when demand was much lower, and that virtual education, telemedicine and cloud services have since outgrown the original capacity.

What a class actually needs

A live coding class sends the mentor's voice and screen to the learner and the learner's voice back. A steady connection matters more than a fast one, and the shared screen uses far less data than a film.

The free class tests this. If the picture keeps freezing, the mentor can switch off video, keep audio and a shared screen, and see whether the lesson still works.

  1. Find the device. A laptop or desktop for typed code; a tablet only for block coding with a younger child.
  2. Check the room. A quiet corner, a headset if possible, and the same place every week.
  3. Test the line. Join the free class from that room at the hour you plan to use.
  4. Decide together. The mentor tells you plainly whether the connection and the English level are ready, and suggests a group or one-to-one start.

A Huancavelica data project

How many new university students come from Huancavelica itself?

The Universidad Nacional de Huancavelica publishes its 2025 entrants as open data. The question looks simple. The answer teaches the most useful habit in data work: when part of a column is unknown, say how wide your uncertainty is.

The file

The university publishes "Lista de Ingresantes del año 2025 I y II a la UNH" on datosabiertos.gob.pe under the Open Data Commons Attribution License (ODC-By), released on 23 December 2025. It covers undergraduate entrants, postgraduate entrants and second-speciality entrants for both 2025 admission rounds.

Our copy, ingresantes-unh-2025.csv, has 1,417 rows and no names. Each row carries a TOTAL_INGRESANTES count, so a row can stand for several people. Every row says its cut-off date is 30 June 2025, although 228 rows record an entry date of 6 September 2025, so we describe the file as published rather than guess which date is right.

The column that matters

DEPA_PROCEDENCIA records the department each entrant comes from. Most rows name one: HUANCAVELICA, JUNIN, AYACUCHO and so on. But 815 of the 1,417 rows say "NO INDICA", not stated.

A first attempt adds up the people, counts those from Huancavelica, and divides. It runs without error and prints a confident percentage, which is exactly what makes it dangerous.

Twenty-seven per cent suggests that most new students came from outside the region. That is not what the file shows. Version 1 treats every "NO INDICA" entrant as someone from elsewhere, when they could just as well be from Huancavelica. The only honest statement is a range: the lowest possible share assumes no unknown entrant is local, the highest assumes all of them are. Version 2 works that range out separately for each level of study, because the unknowns are not spread evenly.

Now the answer has two very different parts. For undergraduates the range is narrow, about 90 to 94 per cent, because only 17 of 412 did not state an origin: the university's first-degree students are overwhelmingly from the region. For postgraduate and second-speciality entrants the range runs from almost nothing to nearly nine in ten, which means the file simply cannot answer the question for them. Saying "we cannot tell" is a result, and a more useful one than 26.8 per cent.

  1. Around 8, with counters. Ten counters in a bag: four red, two blue, four hidden under a cloth. How many red could there be? Write the smallest and the largest.
  2. Blocks, around 9 to 11. Make a list of ten answers with three marked "?". Count the yes answers twice, once treating "?" as no and once as yes.
  3. First Python, around 11 to 13. Edit Version 1 so that it counts JUNIN. Is the new share a lower bound, an upper bound or neither?
  4. Ages 13 and over. Add a third line to Version 2 that prints the share among entrants who did state an origin. Explain why it sits between the two bounds.
  5. Going further. Group the undergraduate rows by ESPECIALIDAD and find the programmes whose range is widest. Which ones should the university ask about first?

School and beyond

Do schools in Huancavelica teach programming?

Not as a subject. The region follows Peru's national curriculum, which treats digital skills as something every area should develop rather than as a course of their own.

The national rules

Under the General Education Law, primaria lasts six years and secundaria five. MINEDU sets each grade by the child's age on 31 March, so a six year old enters 1° de primaria and 5° de secundaria is the year of sixteen year olds.

The curriculum's technology competency, number 28, "Se desenvuelve en entornos virtuales generados por las TIC", is transversal. In a regular 35-hour secundaria week, Matemática has four hours, Inglés three and Educación para el Trabajo two; programming has none.

Where programming appears

MINEDU's Fábrica de Programadores, launched in July 2025, trains 1,000 public-school pupils of 4° and 5° de secundaria in Lima Norte in Python with an international certificate. None of the sources we read mention it in Huancavelica.

Beyond school, the region signed an exchange agreement in 2026 with the Lycée Le Chesnoy Amilly in France for students of its technological institutes; six students and two teachers travelled to France that September for training in farming and food production.

Contests are open here as everywhere in Peru. For ONEM, the national maths olympiad, a pupil sits the first round at their own school; that round took place on 8 July 2026, and the 2026 edition called on more than 1.7 million secundaria pupils. UTEC runs the OPI computing olympiads and charges secundaria entrants nothing. We have no connection with MINEDU, UTEC, the regional government or either contest, and we teach with problems we write ourselves. Our page on maths olympiad coaching in Peru explains how we prepare pupils with original practice.

Peru: UTC minus 5 hours all year, with no daylight saving (tz database, America/Lima). India: UTC plus 5 hours 30 minutes. India is 10 hours 30 minutes ahead of Huancavelica every day of the year.

Online coding and AI classes in Huancavelica: questions families ask

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. We teach in English only, in live online lessons. Our mentors keep their English slow and plain, follow the learner's speed and accent, and when a learner replies in Spanish they offer the English phrase to repeat; the lesson itself stays in English.

Can my child use the tablet from school?

For block coding with a younger child, often yes, if the school allows it at home. For typed programming from about age ten, a laptop or desktop works far better, because the learner types for most of the lesson.

We have no computer at home. Should we wait?

Tell us in the free class. If there is truly no computer available, even a shared one, we will say honestly that it is better to wait than to pay for lessons that cannot work.

Do you teach learners in Lircay, Pampas or Acobamba?

Yes. The classes are online, so every province is the same to us, provided the connection is steady at the hour you choose.

What time are the classes?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. India runs 10 hours 30 minutes ahead of Huancavelica in every season; a lesson at 08:30 in Huancavelica starts at 19:00 in India.

Is English a problem for a beginner?

It can be at first. The free class includes a short task in simple English so the mentor can judge. Teenagers and adults usually manage; young children often do better one to one.

Do Huancavelica schools teach coding?

Not as a subject of its own. The national curriculum wants digital skills woven through all areas, and no hour of the standard secundaria week is labelled programming.

Is this useful for university students at the UNH?

Often. Python, data analysis and databases support degrees in engineering, economics and education, and much technical reading is in English already. We teach alongside your degree; the university decides your grades.

Do you teach AI?

Yes. Older learners build and test small models and learn why they fail. Children explore the ideas in games and sorting tasks; nobody under 13 is asked to open a grown-up chatbot account.

How do we pay?

A card payment once a month, priced in US dollars, which your bank changes into soles. If you browse from India the page shows rupees. Nothing is charged for the trial.

Is there a certificate?

Where a course has one, it is a completion certificate issued by us. Schools, universities and Peruvian authorities do not issue or recognise it as a qualification; it simply shows the work done.
