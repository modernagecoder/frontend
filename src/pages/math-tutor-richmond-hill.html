If one idea carries a Richmond Hill student from Grade 7 to Grade 12, it is slope. It begins as “goes up by 3 each time” in a pattern, becomes rate of change in MTH1W, turns into the slope of a line of best fit in data management, and ends as the slope of a tangent in calculus. Students who understand it as a rate, so many units of this per unit of that, carry it through every course; students who memorise “rise over run” as a rule tend to lose it at the first unfamiliar graph. This page traces slope through the Ontario courses, links each stage to the page that covers it, and then measures it on real ground: York Region’s survey benchmarks, the marked points whose heights above sea level are known, inside Richmond Hill.

How slope grows through Ontario math, from Grade 7 patterns to calculus

Richmond Hill public schools teach the Ontario curriculum. Here is one idea followed through the grades, with our summary of each stage and the page where we teach it in full.

Slope and rate of change through Ontario math: our summary; course titles and order from the Ontario curriculum service, EQAO dates for 2026 to 2027 from EQAO
WhereSlope looks likeGo deeper

The Grade 9 assessment is the first place many Richmond Hill families see slope tested formally. EQAO describes it as an online assessment that includes two sessions, Session A and Session B, totalling 54 questions, and lists the 2027 window as 12 January to 5 February. Linear relations run through much of MTH1W, and the ministry’s coding expectation asks students to use coding to demonstrate an understanding of variables, parameters, equations and inequalities. A parameter is exactly what slope is in y = mx + b.

In Grade 12 the idea splits. MDM4U uses slope to describe a trend in messy data; MCV4U uses it to define the derivative. The ministry asks for MHF4U before or alongside MCV4U, so a student planning calculus should be secure on rates of change by the end of Grade 11.

Richmond Hill data lesson

How steep is Richmond Hill? Slope from 31 survey benchmarks

York Region publishes the survey benchmarks it maintains, each with an elevation above sea level. We kept the benchmarks that fall inside York Region’s own Richmond Hill boundary and fitted a line. The results are our calculations.

In York Region’s words, a benchmark is a relatively permanent material object, natural or artificial, bearing a marked point whose elevation above or below an adopted datum is known, and the datums are based on mean sea level. The Region adds a warning that belongs in any student report: most were established approximately 20 years ago, and while most are still considered accurate, it is suggested that they should be checked against other benchmarks to make sure.

To fit the line we measured each benchmark’s position as kilometres north of the grid line at northing 4,850,000 m, so the numbers stay small. The program below keeps only benchmarks inside Richmond Hill, using the odd-even crossing test on York Region’s boundary, and asks Python’s statistics module for the least-squares line and the correlation. It needs Python 3.10 or newer:import json, statistics def crosses(x, y, ring): count = 0 for (x1, y1), (x2, y2) in zip(ring, ring[1:] + ring[:1]): if (y1 > y) != (y2 > y) and x < x1 + (y - y1) * (x2 - x1) / (y2 - y1): count += 1 return count towns = json.load(open("muni-boundary.json"))["features"] rings = [ring for t in towns if t["attributes"]["NAME"] == "Richmond Hill" for ring in t["geometry"]["rings"]] km_north, height = [], [] for mark in json.load(open("benchmarks.json"))["features"]: x, y = mark["geometry"]["x"], mark["geometry"]["y"] if sum(crosses(x, y, ring) for ring in rings) % 2 == 1: km_north.append((y - 4_850_000) / 1000) height.append(mark["attributes"]["ELEVATION"]) fit = statistics.linear_regression(km_north, height) print("benchmarks:", len(height)) print("slope, metres per km:", round(fit.slope, 2)) print("intercept, metres:", round(fit.intercept, 1)) print("r:", round(statistics.correlation(km_north, height), 3))

Reading the slope

A slope of 8.16 metres per kilometre means that, on this trend, each kilometre north is about eight metres higher. It is a rate with units, which is exactly how MTH1W wants a rate of change described.

Reading r

A correlation of 0.895 is a strong positive linear relationship, and r squared of about 0.80 says the line accounts for about four fifths of the variation in elevation. The rest is hills, valleys and where each benchmark happens to sit.

Two location fields

Twenty-one benchmarks show 0 for latitude and longitude, which would put them in the Atlantic off Africa. Their map geometry is fine. We used the geometry and reported the empty fields rather than silently trusting either.

A worked problem, ours

Grade, residual and the danger of extrapolating

A four-part problem we give Richmond Hill students after the benchmark lesson. The numbers come from our analysis above; the questions and answers are ours.

Part (a), percent grade. The lowest benchmark, 249-66, is at 186.83 m, and the highest, 848145, is at 314.578 m. They are 10,582.8 m apart horizontally. The rise is 314.578 − 186.83 = 127.748 m, so the slope is 127.748 ÷ 10,582.8 ≈ 0.0121. As a road sign would write it, that is a grade of about 1.21 percent: gentle, even though the height difference sounds large.

Part (b), using the line. Our line is E = 8.16k + 151.0, where E is elevation in metres and k is kilometres north of northing 4,850,000 m. Predict the elevation at k = 12. E = 8.16 × 12 + 151.0 ≈ 248.9 m (the unrounded slope, 8.164, gives 249.0 m). Notice how rounding the slope moves the answer by a tenth of a metre.

Part (c), a residual. Benchmark 848145 lies 14.88 km north. The line predicts about 8.164 × 14.88 + 151.0 ≈ 272.5 m, but the benchmark is at 314.6 m. The residual, actual minus predicted, is about +42.1 m: the highest point is well above the trend, so it is not simply the most northerly point. A residual that size tells a student to look at a map, not to doubt the arithmetic.

Part (d), the trap. Use the line to predict the elevation 30 km north. The formula happily gives about 396 m. But our 31 benchmarks only span 4.3 km to 18.4 km north, and the line knows nothing beyond that. Extrapolating a fitted line far outside its data is the most common way a correct calculation produces a wrong conclusion.

Try it yourself. Benchmark 78-125 sits 9.89 km north at 202.91 m. Find its predicted elevation from the line and its residual, then explain in one sentence what a negative residual means on the ground.

The Canadian Team Mathematics Contest: math as a team sport

Most Waterloo contests are written alone. The CTMC, run by CEMC at the University of Waterloo, puts six students on one team, and for students who learn by explaining to each other it is often the most enjoyable contest of the year.

Twenty-five problems in forty-five minutes cannot be solved by one person, so the team event rewards something school rarely tests: dividing work, checking each other quickly and explaining a solution in a sentence. The residual question above is a good team exercise for exactly that reason; one student computes, another checks the units, a third asks whether the answer makes sense. Math and AI

Will AI make slope pointless for a Richmond Hill student?

Software fits a line instantly. Knowing when the line is lying still takes a person.

Any spreadsheet or AI tool will fit our 31 benchmarks and report 8.16 metres per kilometre. None of them will volunteer that the line should not be used 30 km north, that 21 benchmarks have empty coordinate fields, or that the highest point sits far above the trend. Those observations are the mathematics, and the student has to make them.

Our teachers keep that work with the student: sketch the scatter plot first, estimate the slope by eye, then compute and compare. Anything submitted for a school mark is the student’s own work.

Ontario math asks students to reason about models, not only to produce them; a tool helps with the producing. The longer argument is in why reasoning, not button-pressing, is the skill that lasts.

How does an online class compare with a home tutor?

A home tutor comes to you and usually charges by the hour, travel included. Our online class has one teacher and 15 to 20 students at one level for a monthly fee; our one-to-one option is a teacher for one student, also monthly, with no travel.

Is my child too young to start?

My teen gets the right answers but loses marks for explanations. Why?
