## From a school formula to a penny's worth of physics

Learners code the pendulum formula, compare it with the real clock, and then work out what one penny actually does.

### 1. The formula

A simple pendulum's full swing takes 2 pi times the square root of its length divided by g, the pull of gravity, about 9.81 metres per second squared.

### 2. Real against simple

For a four-second swing the formula gives a length of 3.98 metres. The real pendulum is 4.4 metres, because its weight is spread along its length rather than all at the tip.

### 3. The penny

Gaining 0.4 seconds a day is 4.6 parts in a million. Because the period depends on the square root of length, that needs the effective length to shrink by twice as much: about 37 micrometres.

**What the numbers imply, computed 25 September 2026**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Length of a perfect simple pendulum with a 4-second swing | 3.98 m |
| Swing of a perfect simple pendulum 4.4 m long | 4.21 s |
| Change in rate from one penny | 4.6 parts per million faster |
| Shortening of effective length that one penny represents | about 37 micrometres, 0.037 mm |
| Distance a 4.2 m minute-hand tip travels in a year | about 231 km |

The last row is a small puzzle. Parliament gives 190 kilometres a year for the minute hands, while the very tip of a 4.2 metre hand travels about 231. A point 3.45 metres from the centre would travel 190. Learners discuss which part of the hand the published figure might describe, rather than assuming either number is wrong: a good habit whenever a calculation and a published figure disagree.

No summary can do that for them.

## Why square roots make pendulums forgiving

The period depends on the square root of the length, which halves the effect of any change. That is exactly why fine adjustment is possible.

### Halving effect

Change the length by 1% and the period changes by only about 0.5%. Clockmakers use this to make tiny, controllable corrections.

### Tiny numbers

Parts per million are hard to picture. Learners convert them into seconds a day, then into micrometres of length, until the size makes sense.

### Models have limits

The simple formula assumes all the mass is at one point. The gap between 3.98 m and 4.4 m shows exactly where that assumption fails.

GCSE physics students meet pendulums and can follow every step; A-level students can go further with the physics of real, compound pendulums and derive the square-root sensitivity. ## AI can quote the pendulum formula. Why should a Westminster teenager use it on a real clock?

Because applying a formula to reality is where understanding is tested.

Ask an AI for the period of a pendulum and it will give you the textbook formula. Ask it why the Great Clock's pendulum is 4.4 metres rather than 3.98, or what a penny does in micrometres, and a careful answer needs exactly the reasoning this project practises. Learners who have done it know that every model has assumptions, that tiny rates hide measurable physical changes, and that published figures sometimes need a second look.

Those habits matter far beyond clocks, in any job where a calculation meets the real world. AI will keep supplying formulas; people who can apply and question them will keep being needed.

Westminster keeps the country's most famous clock running with a pile of old pennies. A learner here who can explain why that works has turned a curiosity into real physics. The longer argument is in [why teenagers should still learn to code in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).

### Why is the real pendulum longer than the formula predicts?

The simple formula assumes all the mass is at one point on a weightless rod. A real pendulum spreads its mass along its length, so its effective length is shorter than its overall length.
