Three things the table shows

First, the language is rarely fixed by the province. Ontario's ICS3U describes skills, not a language, so one school teaches it in Python and another in Java. Nova Scotia's virtual Programming 12 is an exception that names Java outright.

Second, the courses come late. In the documents above, a dedicated programming course first appears in Grade 10 or 11, so a curious Grade 8 or 9 student has no programming course of their own to take yet. Third, the Ontario chain is strict: a student who skips ICS3U cannot take ICS4U, the university preparation course.

What a credit is, and what we are not

In Ontario, the 2008 computer studies document says "Each of the courses listed above is worth one credit", and computer studies sits in the group of subjects a student can use for one of the compulsory diploma credits. Credits come only from a school.

We teach alongside those courses. A teen in our classes gets the programming practice, explanation and project feedback the school timetable rarely has room for, and earns the credit at school as usual. We do not grant Ontario credits, and no province's credit comes from us.

The student who is ahead of school

Some Grade 9 students have already taught themselves loops from videos, and find the first school course slow. The risk for them is not boredom but gaps: they can make things work without being able to say why. We test that early with a short program they have to explain line by line.

If the explanation holds, they move quickly to object-oriented code, data structures and contests. If it does not, a few weeks of honest fundamentals save months later.

The student who is behind

Others reach the middle of ICS3U and realise the first unit never landed. The fix is rarely a new topic; it is going back to variables, conditions and loops with harder problems, until reading a program is as natural as reading a paragraph.

We ask such students to bring their actual school assignment, read it together, and plan the solution in plain words before any code. We never write the assignment for them; the school's marks have to reflect the student's own work.

A real data project

Searching Calgary's public art for the word "light"

The City of Calgary publishes descriptions of its public artworks under a licence that allows commercial reuse. A teen asked to find the artworks that use light will usually write one line, check the count, and move on. The count is wrong. This project, ours from start to finish, is about why.

The task: list the Calgary artworks whose descriptions mention light, lights, lighting, lit or illuminated. The file has 140 rows with columns including ART_ID, TAB_NAME (the city quadrant or Downtown), TITLE, SHORT_DESC and five long description columns, DESC1 to DESC5.

Why the four counts differ (our analysis of the same file)
MethodCountWhat it gets wrong or right
"light" in the text30 rowsMisses one row where the only match is a capital "Light"
"light" in text.lower()31 rowsCatches capitals, but 18 of these rows match only through other words: highlight 7 times, lighthearted 4, highlighting 3, and flight, sunlight, enlighten, highlighted and slight once each
Whole words with \b, five word forms16 rowsDrops the false matches and adds 3 rows that say only lit or illuminated
The same, keyed by ART_ID13 artworksLuminous Crossings alone is 4 rows sharing one ART_ID, P2013.002.001, because it is listed at more than one LRT station

The three ideas a teen should leave with

Case. Text typed by people mixes capitals; compare lower-cased text, or tell the search to ignore case. Words, not letters. A substring search finds the letters l-i-g-h-t inside highlight and flight. A word boundary, written \b in a regular expression, stops that.

Rows are not things. A spreadsheet row is a record, and one artwork can have several records. Counting distinct keys, here ART_ID, is the difference between 16 and 13. The file has 140 rows but 134 distinct ART_ID values.

The judgement call left open

Is sunlight about light? Our list of five word forms leaves it out, so one artwork described with sunlight is not counted. A different reader could include it. We ask students to write their rule down in a sentence before they search, and to report the count with the rule beside it.

That habit, stating the rule before the result, is what makes a number trustworthy, in a school assignment, a contest or a job.

Practice set

Five search traps to try before your next assignment

Our own short exercises, in the same family as the Calgary project. Write each one in Python, then check it against the answer. None needs a file; make your own small lists.

  1. Count the cats. Given the sentence "The cat sat by the category of catalogue cats.", count how many times the word cat or cats appears as a whole word. (Answer: 2.)
  2. Case trap. Given the list ["Ottawa", "ottawa", "OTTAWA", "Ottawa River"], count the entries equal to the city name Ottawa, ignoring case. (Answer: 3; "Ottawa River" is not equal to it.)
  3. Duplicate records. Given rows [("A1", "Bridge"), ("A2", "Tower"), ("A1", "Bridge"), ("A3", "Bridge")], count distinct IDs and distinct titles. (Answer: 3 IDs, 2 titles; two different IDs share a title.)
  4. Prefix or word. Find every word in "Snowshoe snow snowy snowflake Snow" that is exactly snow, ignoring case. (Answer: 2.)
  5. Write the rule first. Choose a word with several forms, such as run, runs, running, ran. Write your rule in one sentence, then a regular expression that matches only those forms, and test it on a sentence that also contains "brunch" and "rerun".

Exercise 5 has no single right answer, and that is deliberate. In class, two students' rules are compared on the same sentence, and each has to defend what theirs includes and leaves out.

Entering through a school

The CCC and Skills Ontario both run through schools: CEMC says students must take part through their school, and Skills Ontario says a student cannot register directly. A teen who wants either should ask a computer science or technology teacher in September or October, not in February.

FIRST teams are usually based at a school or a community group, so the practical question is whether one exists nearby.

Choosing one

A teen who enjoys working alone against a clock suits the CCC. One who likes building with a team suits FIRST. Skills Ontario rewards a strong, reliable performance on the day. None of them needs to be chosen this year, and none is required for university.

Waterloo, for example, names Grade 11 U Introduction to Computer Science as a recommended course for its computer science programme; it does not require a contest for admission.

AI tools and teenagers

What the AI companies allow at 13 to 17, and how we use it

Teenagers will meet AI coding assistants whether or not a class mentions them. The age rules are set by each company, and they differ. We teach teens to use the tools their age allows to explain and check code, never to hand in code they cannot explain.

Age rules from each company's own terms (read 22 October 2026)

Our rule in class

A teen may ask an allowed assistant to explain an error message or suggest a test case. They may not paste in code they cannot explain. Every program a student submits to us, they walk through aloud, and a line they cannot explain is rewritten by them.

New Brunswick's Computer Science 110 already mentions "AI-assisted debugging tools", so this is not a fringe question. The skill being tested in school, in contests and in interviews is still the student's own reasoning.

Why this matters more for teens than adults

Grade 11 and 12 are when a student builds the habits they will take to university. A student who learns to reach for an assistant before thinking ends up unable to start a problem alone, which is exactly what a contest, an exam or a technical interview asks.

Our promise is the same in every country we teach: students learn how to think through a problem and solve it, not how to copy an answer or an AI's output.

Four levels

How far along is your teen?

Our levels for placing a teenager honestly in the first class.

Four levels for teenagers (ours)
LevelWhat the teen can do
1. Reads codeFollows a short program and predicts what it prints
2. Writes codeBuilds programs with loops, lists and functions from a blank file
3. Designs codeSplits a larger problem into functions or classes and tests each part
4. Judges codeCompares two working solutions and explains which is better and why

For parents

Most teens who are new to coding reach level 2 within a few months. Level 3 is roughly where ICS4U sits, and level 4 is where contest and university work begins.

A teen who can produce working code but not explain it is at level 1, whatever the program does.

For teens

Write your plan in comments before the code. If you cannot write the plan, you are not ready to write the program.

When a test fails, change one thing at a time. Two changes at once teach you nothing.

Teens and parents ask

Coding for teens in Canada: common questions

What coding courses do Canadian high schools offer?

It depends on the province. Ontario lists ICD2O in Grade 10 and ICS3U, ICS3C, ICS4U and ICS4C in Grades 11 and 12. British Columbia has Computer Programming 11 and 12 and Computer Science 11 and 12. Saskatchewan has Computer Science 20 and 30, and Manitoba 20S to 40S.

Do your classes give my teen an Ontario credit?

No. We teach alongside school courses such as ICS3U and ICS4U, and we do not grant Ontario credits or credits from any other province. Credits come only from the student's school.

Which language should a teenager learn first?

We start most teens in Python, because the code reads clearly and the same skills carry into Java or C++ later. A teen whose school course uses Java can learn Java with us from the start.

My teen is in ICS3U and struggling. Can you help?

Yes. Our Ontario Computer Science Companion is paced to the student's own ICS3U or ICS4U course, in Python or Java. We explain and practise; we never write the school assignment for the student.

When is the Canadian Computing Competition in 2027?

CEMC lists the 2027 CCC for Wednesday, February 17, 2027 in North and South America. Students choose Junior or Senior regardless of grade and enter through their school.

Can teenagers use ChatGPT or Claude for coding?

OpenAI's terms allow users from 13, with a parent's or guardian's permission under 18. Anthropic's terms require Claude users to be at least 18, so we never recommend Claude to teens. GitHub Copilot is free for verified students aged 13 or over.

Is coding useful for university admission in Canada?

Waterloo names Grade 11 U Introduction to Computer Science as a recommended course for its computer science programme. We make no admission promises; what coding gives a teen is the problem solving behind any strong application.

What class times suit Canadian teenagers?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Every class is booked in your own province's time.

How big is a teen batch?

Fifteen to twenty teenagers with one regular teacher. One-to-one classes suit a teen who wants a pace of their own or help with a specific school course.

What will my teen build?

Programs that answer real questions, such as our Calgary public art search, which shows why four ways of counting the same thing give 30, 31, 16 and 13. Later, web apps, games or AI projects, depending on the course.
