The Canadian Math Olympiad path, from the COMC to the IMO team

The Canadian Mathematical Olympiad is Canada's national olympiad for advanced high school students, and nobody enters it by signing up. Every writer is invited by the Canadian Mathematical Society, almost always on the strength of the Canadian Open Mathematics Challenge the October before. From the CMO, and from a few other contests, the CMS chooses Math Team Canada for the International Mathematical Olympiad each July. This guide follows that road as the CMS itself describes it, page by page, with nothing added and no promises: who is invited and how, what the 2026 olympiads looked like in numbers, what changes from 2027, what an olympiad problem asks for, and six problems of ours with complete proofs.

Three numbers, three pages

The CMS gives the size of the invited group in slightly different ways on different pages. The CMO page says "Typically 75-100 students are invited each year to write the CMO and a further 20 students are invited to write the CJMO."

On the COMC home page the figure is roughly 100 to 120 invitations, covering the CMO and the CJMO together, and the COMC Prizes page says "the top 100 (all figures are approximate)". We print each as its page states it and do not combine them.

Only one division counts

From 2026 the COMC has a Competitive Division and a Global Division. According to the CMS Changes page, a CMO invitation is possible only for writers in the Competitive Division, who are also the only ones eligible for prize money.

To be Competitive, a writer must also start the COMC inside a fixed afternoon window set in Eastern time. Our olympiad classes page covers how contests like this compare internationally.

One more change sits behind all of this. The CMS has discontinued the CMO Qualifying Repêchage, a take-home round that used to give students just below the COMC cut a second route in. Its Repêchage page says: "Starting in 2027, we will instead be increasing the number of people invited directly from the COMC to the Canadian Mathematical Olympiads (CMO/CJMO)." For a student aiming at the CMO, the October COMC is now the whole qualifying round.

The CMS Prizes page describes the CMO as "a conventionally-supervised three-hour competition with five questions", and the scores are out of 35, so each question is worth 7 points on our arithmetic. A median of 11 out of 35 means that half of the students invited, all of them among the strongest COMC writers in the country, scored 11 or less. Put another way, a typical invited student solved about one and a half problems completely in three hours. That is not a sign the students were weak; it is what olympiad problems are like, and it is why the CMS warns that "success will require mathematics at a higher level than is taught in most schools".

What the IMO paper is

The CMS describes the IMO exam as "six questions, split over two days (a total of nine hours of testing)". It calls the IMO "the world championship mathematics competition for high-school students."

The 2027 IMO is listed for July 16 to 26, 2027, in Budapest, Hungary.

What a solution looks like

An olympiad answer is a proof: one of ours, in full

Olympiad problems do not ask for a number. They ask the student to show that something is always true, or to find every answer and prove there are no others. Here is a standard exercise we use in the first month of olympiad training, with the proof we expect. The problem is a well-known one; the proof as written, and the notes on it, are ours.

What each step of the proof is for (our teaching notes)
StepWhy it is there
Reduce 30 to 2, 3 and 5Turns one hard claim into three small ones, and says why that is enough
The factorizationMakes the structure visible: three consecutive integers and one extra factor
Consecutive integers for 2 and 3A known fact, stated with its reason in one line
Five cases for 5Every remainder is covered; none is skipped because it "obviously" works
The closing line about common factorsWithout it, divisibility by 2, 3 and 5 separately would not prove divisibility by 30

The tempting shortcut

A student who checks n = 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 and finds 0, 30, 240, 1020 and 3120, all multiples of 30, has evidence. They do not have a proof, because there are infinitely many integers.

On an olympiad paper, evidence earns little or nothing. The CMS publishes past solutions precisely so students can see what a complete argument looks like.

How we mark it in class

We look for the claim, every case, and a reason for each step, in that order. A proof missing one of the five remainder cases is marked as incomplete, even if the missing case is easy.

Students rewrite the proof until a classmate who has never seen it can follow it without asking a question.

Five more problems

Five olympiad-style problems of ours, with complete proofs

These are standard olympiad exercises of the kind every training program uses, written up by us; none is taken from a past CMO, CJMO, COMC or IMO paper, and the proofs as written are ours. They cover the four areas olympiads draw on: number theory (above), combinatorics, algebra and geometry. Try each for twenty minutes before reading the proof.

Problem 2. A standard olympiad exercise, not from any CMS paper; the write-up is ours. Combinatorics: an invariant. The numbers 1, 2, 3, ..., 10 are written on a board. A move erases any two numbers a and b and writes the single number |a - b| in their place. After nine moves one number is left. Can that number be 0?

Proof that it cannot. Look at the sum of all the numbers on the board. A move replaces a + b by |a - b|. The difference between these, (a + b) - |a - b|, is twice the smaller of a and b, which is even. So every move changes the sum by an even amount, and the parity of the sum never changes. At the start the sum is 1 + 2 + ... + 10 = 55, which is odd. So the last number is odd, and in particular it is not 0. The idea, finding a quantity that never changes, is called an invariant, and it settles questions that look as though they need a search through every sequence of moves.

Problem 3. A standard olympiad exercise, not from any CMS paper; the write-up is ours. Combinatorics: pigeonhole. Five points are placed inside or on the boundary of a square of side 1. Prove that some two of them are at distance at most √2 / 2 from each other.

Proof. Divide the square into four smaller squares of side 1/2 by joining the midpoints of opposite sides. Every point of the large square lies in at least one small square (a point on a dividing line can be assigned to either). Five points and four small squares means some small square contains at least two of the points, by the pigeonhole principle. Two points in a square of side 1/2 are at most the length of its diagonal apart, which is √((1/2)2 + (1/2)2) = √2 / 2. So those two points are at distance at most √2 / 2.

Problem 4. A standard olympiad exercise, not from any CMS paper; the write-up is ours. Algebra: an inequality. Prove that for all positive real numbers a, b and c, (a + b)(b + c)(c + a) ≥ 8abc, and find when equality holds.

Proof. For positive x and y, (√x - √y)2 ≥ 0 expands to x + y ≥ 2√(xy). Apply this three times: a + b ≥ 2√(ab), b + c ≥ 2√(bc) and c + a ≥ 2√(ca). All six quantities are positive, so the inequalities may be multiplied: (a + b)(b + c)(c + a) ≥ 8√(a2b2c2) = 8abc. Equality in each step needs a = b, b = c and c = a, so equality holds exactly when a = b = c. Note the line about positivity: multiplying inequalities is only safe because every side is positive, and saying so is part of the proof.

Problem 5. A standard olympiad exercise, not from any CMS paper; the write-up is ours. Geometry: the angle bisector. In triangle ABC, the bisector of angle A meets BC at D. Prove that BD / DC = AB / AC.

Proof, by areas. Triangles ABD and ADC share the height from A to the line BC, so the ratio of their areas is BD / DC. Now compute the same areas a second way, using two sides and the included angle. Let the angle at A be 2t, so the bisector makes angle t with each of AB and AC. Then area(ABD) = (1/2)(AB)(AD)sin t and area(ADC) = (1/2)(AC)(AD)sin t, so their ratio is AB / AC. Both expressions are the ratio of the same two areas, so BD / DC = AB / AC. Computing one quantity in two ways is among the most useful ideas in olympiad geometry.

Problem 6. A standard olympiad exercise, not from any CMS paper; the write-up is ours. Algebra: a functional equation. Find all functions f from the real numbers to the real numbers such that f(x + y) = f(x) + 2y for all real x and y.

Solution. Put x = 0: f(y) = f(0) + 2y for every real y. Writing c = f(0), any solution must be f(x) = 2x + c. Conversely, if f(x) = 2x + c for a constant c, then f(x + y) = 2x + 2y + c and f(x) + 2y = 2x + c + 2y, which are equal. So the solutions are exactly the functions f(x) = 2x + c, for any real constant c. The second half, checking that the candidates really work, is the step students most often leave out, and on an olympiad it is part of the answer.

Using the set

Spend twenty minutes on each problem before reading the proof, then write your own proof from memory the next day. The second write-up is where the learning happens.

Problems 2 and 3 suit a strong Grade 8 or 9 student. Problems 4 to 6 suit students already writing the COMC.

Six ideas worth naming

Reduce to primes. Find an invariant. Use pigeonhole. Combine simple inequalities. Compute one thing two ways. Substitute, then check.

Olympiad training is largely the collection of ideas like these, practised until a student reaches for the right one without being told.

Why proofs every week

The 2026 statistics show that even invited students solved few problems completely. The gap is rarely knowledge; it is the ability to turn an idea into a complete argument under time.

That skill only improves through writing proofs and having them marked, week after week, for months.

What we do not do

We do not hand out answer keys or tricks to memorize. We teach students how to think through a problem they have never seen, which is the only thing an olympiad tests.

We do not claim any influence over invitations, and we never promise a CMO place, a medal or a team spot.

For parents

Almost every strong student starts at stage 1, because school rewards correct answers rather than complete arguments. The move to stage 3 usually takes a year of weekly proof writing.

Olympiad training is worth doing for its own sake. The habit of proving things carefully pays off in every university mathematics or computer science course.

For students

When you think you have a proof, ask what would happen in the case you did not check.

After finding all the answers, prove there are no others.

The Canadian Math Olympiad: questions answered

What is the Canadian Mathematical Olympiad?

The CMO is the Canadian Mathematical Society national olympiad for advanced high school students. It is invitation-only, has five questions in three hours, and is the most important competition for students hoping to be selected for Math Team Canada at the IMO.

How does a student get invited to the CMO?

Mainly through the COMC. The CMS CMO page says the top 75 to 100 qualifying students from the COMC Competitive Division are invited, with a few invitations through the Alberta High School Mathematics Competition, the AMQ secondary concours and the APMO team.

What is the CJMO?

The Canadian Junior Mathematical Olympiad, held at the same time as the CMO. The CMS invites up to 20 top COMC scorers in Grade 10 or below who are not invited to the CMO. It is less challenging, though some problems may appear on both papers.

When is the 2027 CMO?

The CMS had not published a 2027 date on 22 October 2026. The 2026 CMO and CJMO were held on Thursday, March 12, 2026, and the CMS says it plans to hold the olympiads earlier from 2027, so check the CMS CMO page.

How is the Math Team Canada for the IMO chosen?

The CMS says team members are chosen based on their results in previous competitions and their eligibility to represent Canada at the IMO, and names the COMC and the CMO as two important Canadian competitions on that road. The APMO results are another measure the CMS uses.

Is the Repechage still a route to the CMO?

No. The 2026 Repechage was the last one. The CMS says it will make up for it from 2027 by sending more olympiad invitations straight from COMC results.

How hard is the CMO?

In 2026 the CMS reported a CMO mean of 11.1 and a median of 11 out of 35 across 95 competitors, and a CJMO median of 18 out of 35 across 18 competitors. The CMS warns that success needs mathematics beyond what most schools teach.

Who is eligible for the CMO?

Official participants must meet the IMO rules for representing Canada, which the CMS sets out as citizenship or Canadian schooling, must not yet be at university or have finished secondary school, must attend school full-time and must be under 20 as of June 30 of the competition year.

Can you guarantee a CMO invitation or a place on the team?

No. Invitations and team selection are decided by the CMS alone. We teach olympiad mathematics and proof writing, and we make no promise of any invitation, score or medal.

When are your olympiad classes?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. We plan in Eastern time and confirm each class in your own province's time.
