The short answer

"Should my child learn AI" is three questions. Should they learn to judge AI output, literacy: yes, essentially every child, starting as young as 6 with concepts and no accounts, because they already live among these systems. Should they learn to build with AI, vibe coding and app building: if they enjoy making things, from 6 with age-appropriate tools, and it should follow interest rather than fear. Should they learn to build AI itself, machine learning: only the genuinely curious, typically from the teen years. The falling-behind panic is a sales device; a calm start beats a scared one in every outcome we can measure. Modern Age Coders teaches all three paths live, worldwide, from USD 100 a month, first class free with parents welcome.

The separation

## Three meanings of "learn AI", finally kept apart

Conflating these is how families buy the wrong course, and how the wrong course convinces a child that "AI is not for me". Kept apart, each question answers itself.

|   | Judging it | Building with it | Building it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Knowing what AI is, why it errs, when to check | Making apps, games and tools by directing AI | Training and understanding models themselves |
| Who needs it | Essentially every child | Children who love making things | The persistently curious minority |
| Sensible start | 6 to 8, concepts only, no accounts | 6 in blocks; 13 for real tools | Usually 13 plus, maths tolerance helps |
| Driven by | Necessity, like road safety | Interest, and only interest | Fascination, self-selected |
| Wrong reason to start | There is none; it is baseline | A parent's fear of falling behind | Prestige or someone else's child |

The table's most useful row is the last one. Literacy tolerates any motivation because it is short, universal and protective, the swimming-lesson of the digital decade. The building tracks do not: they run on wanting to make things, and a child enrolled on fear produces the saddest object in education, a finished course and a closed door. Our own [literacy page](/ai-literacy-for-students) covers the first column at depth, and [what is vibe coding](/what-is-vibe-coding) opens the second.

One more inoculation, since you will meet it everywhere: the falling-behind claim. In six years of teaching we have watched eleven year old starters draw level with eight year old starters within months, every time, because the real prerequisites are reading, logic and curiosity, which ordinary childhood builds. The gap that does not close easily is a different one: between children taught to check machine output and children taught to accept it. That gap is worth your attention. The stopwatch is not.

Reading your child

## Ready signals, and honest reasons to wait

### Green lights, per track

For literacy, the only signal needed is age six and the ability to sit with a group for an hour, because the track is universal by design. For building with AI, watch for the child who modifies rather than consumes: house rules renegotiated, game settings tweaked, a "what if it could" spoken aloud at the screen. For building AI itself, the tell is the question behind the question: not "how do I make it stop losing" but "how did it decide that", asked more than once, unprompted.

The strongest green light of all is boringly simple: the child asks to continue after the free class. We weight that one answer above everything a parent tells us, and we built the free class partly so families could collect it cheaply.

### Honest reasons to wait

A school term already at capacity: a child with no slack learns nothing from one more scheduled hour, whatever the subject. A sibling's enthusiasm doing the choosing: the younger child deserves their own door, found at their own speed. Or a recent string of educational purchases that did not stick: pause, let appetite return, and note that the door has no closing time, since our oldest beginners are in their sixties.

What should never drive the timing is the feed: a viral clip of somebody's nine year old shipping an app is content, not evidence, and the family it should influence least is yours. When in doubt, take the free class, ask the child, and believe their answer.

## Answered the way we would want them answered

### Is my child falling behind if they have not started AI yet?

No, and we say this as people who sell the classes. The field's foundations, curiosity, logic, reading, precise language, are exactly what childhood already builds, and a child who starts AI literacy at 11 catches a panicked 8 year old starter within months. The falling-behind framing sells courses and ruins the fun; the honest framing is that this is a good time to start calmly, not a last chance to start desperately.

### Which of the three meanings should we actually pick?

Literacy for every child, because judging machine output is becoming as basic as reading. The other two follow interest, not strategy: building with AI suits makers who light up at shipping things, and building AI itself suits the smaller group who keep asking why the machine answered that way. Start with literacy, watch which question your child asks next, and let that question choose the second course.

### Can it wait a year without cost?

Literacy is the one we would not delay, for the same reason you would not delay teaching a child to cross roads they already walk beside: your child is likely already inside AI-shaped feeds and tools, and the checking habit protects them now. The building tracks wait beautifully. A year later, the tools will be better, the child will be more capable, and nothing real will have been lost.

### My child shows no interest in AI or coding. Should I push?

Push literacy gently, the way you would push swimming: a safety skill does not require enthusiasm, only completion. Do not push the building tracks, because building runs on wanting to build, and a conscripted maker learns resentment faster than syntax. Often the interest arrives sideways anyway, through games, music, sport statistics or a sibling's project, and the door stays open at every age from 6 to 67.

### Is 6 too young to start anything AI-related?

Six is right for the beginnings of literacy: what is a computer guess, can the machine be wrong, how did it learn. Taught through games and stories, no accounts, no screens beyond what the teacher shows. It is too young for the mainstream tools, whose own terms say 13, and any programme that puts a 6 year old on a chat account has failed your first vetting question. Concepts young, accounts later, in that order.

### Will AI not replace the very careers this prepares for?

Some tasks will keep automating, and nobody honest will promise your child a specific job in 2040. What we can say with confidence is directional: the person who can judge, direct and check machine output will be employable across far more futures than the person who can only consume it. You are not buying a career guarantee; you are buying range, and range is the rational purchase under uncertainty.

### Is this equally for daughters?

Emphatically, and early matters here: research and our own classrooms agree that girls opt out of technical subjects through accumulated small signals, not ability. Our batches are mixed, our teachers are deliberate about airtime, and the project-first format helps because a girl who has shipped something real has evidence no stereotype survives. If your daughter is curious, that curiosity is the green light.

### School already covers some AI. Is a class redundant?

Check what the school actually delivers, because coverage varies from excellent to a poster in the corridor. Where school does the concepts well, our tracks complement with the part schools rarely have room for: sustained project work with a live teacher and small batch. Where school coverage is thin, the literacy course fills the base. The free class doubles as a placement conversation and we will tell you plainly if school has it covered.
