## Can a Peruvian child follow a coding class in English?

Most children of 9 or more who have had English at school can, with a patient mentor. Younger children follow through the screen as much as the words. What helps is knowing which English a child really meets in the first weeks.

### The words on the screen

Python 3.14 reserves 35 keywords, and every one of them is an English word or a shortening of one: if, else, while, for, return, import, True, False and the rest. Add a few everyday functions such as print, input and range, and that is most of the vocabulary of a first term.

Error messages are English too. A child who misspells print sees NameError: name 'pirnt' is not defined. Did you mean: 'print'?. Reading that sentence calmly is a skill we practise from the first class.

### The English in the room

EF's English Proficiency Index 2025 places Peru 52nd of 123 countries with a score of 519, and Lima at 555, in EF's "high" band. Speaking is the lowest of Peru's four skill scores at 480, against 540 for reading. EF tests adults who choose to take its test, so treat this as a signal about the country, not about any one child.

Our reading of that pattern: Peruvian learners often read English better than they speak it. A live class, where a child has to say a short sentence aloud every few minutes, works on exactly the weaker side.

## What does an English-taught coding lesson look like? Spelling mistakes, measured

This lesson is for children of about 11 and up who have written a little Python. It starts from something every beginner does, misspelling an English keyword, and turns it into an algorithm: how many single-letter changes turn one word into another?

### The idea, in plain words

Take the typo pirnt. To turn it into print you could change the i to r and the r to i: two changes. That count of insertions, deletions and substitutions is called the edit distance, or Levenshtein distance.

The program below works it out with a table, one row at a time. For each typed word it then finds the nearest real word from Python's own keyword list plus four everyday functions, and prints the match and its distance.

### Three things the output teaches

**A swap costs two.** pirnt, whlie, esle and improt each have two letters in the wrong order, and each is 2 away, because swapping is two substitutions in this method.

**Capitals count.** Print is 1 away from print. Python treats them as different names, and so does the distance.

**Nearest is not the same as meant.** A child who typed fro almost certainly meant for. But fro is 1 away from from and 2 from for, so the program suggests the wrong word. The computer measures; the child decides.

**Nearest keyword by edit distance** (Python)
```python
import keyword

def distance(a, b):
    prev = list(range(len(b) + 1))
    for i, ca in enumerate(a, 1):
        row = [i]
        for j, cb in enumerate(b, 1):
            cost = 0 if ca == cb else 1
            row.append(min(prev[j] + 1, row[j - 1] + 1, prev[j - 1] + cost))
        prev = row
    return prev[-1]

words = keyword.kwlist + ['print', 'input', 'range', 'len']
typed = ['pirnt', 'retrun', 'whlie', 'esle', 'improt', 'Print', 'fro']

for t in typed:
    closest = min(words, key=lambda w: (distance(t, w), w))
    print(t, '->', closest, distance(t, closest))
```

**What it prints** (Output)
```output
pirnt -> print 2
retrun -> return 2
whlie -> while 2
esle -> else 2
improt -> import 2
Print -> print 1
fro -> from 1
```
Exact output from our run with Python 3.14. The word list is Python's keyword list plus print, input, range and len; ties are broken alphabetically by the second item in the key.

Python itself uses a similar idea. In Python 3.14, which we ran, a misspelt name comes with a suggestion of a nearby one, which is why the misspelt pirnt earlier came with Did you mean: 'print'? The lesson shows children that the suggestion is arithmetic, not magic, and that it can be wrong, as fro proves.

1. **Predict first.** Before running anything, say aloud in English how far retrun is from return, and why.
2. **Add a word.** Put for in the typed list and check its distance to itself.
3. **Fix the trap.** Change the program so it prints every word within distance 2, not only the nearest, and see for appear for fro.
4. **Explain it.** Tell the class, in two English sentences, why a swap of two letters costs 2 here.

## How do you choose a coding school for a child in Peru?

Use the same short test for every school, in Spanish or English, online or in person. It applies to us exactly as it applies to anyone else.

1. **Ask for a trial class with your child present.** Watch how much of the hour your child spends building, not watching.
2. **Listen for the teacher's questions.** A good teacher asks the child to predict what the code will do before running it.
3. **Check the language plainly.** Ask which language every lesson is taught in, and whether it changes when a child struggles.
4. **Ask what happens after a mistake.** The answer should be "we find it together", never "the teacher fixes it".
5. **See real work.** Ask for a project a child of the same age built, and ask the child to explain one part of it.
6. **Confirm the practical side.** Group size, who sets the hour, how missed classes are handled, and how you can stop.
7. **Check the equipment.** Typed coding needs a laptop or desktop; INEI found that 36.4% of Peruvian households had at least one computer in the second quarter of 2026.
8. **Decide on evidence.** After a month, ask your child to show you something they made and explain it without help.

### Signs a school suits your child

Your child talks about what they built, not only what they watched. They can explain a bug they found. They want to keep going after the class ends.

For an English-taught class, add one more sign: after a few weeks your child uses words like loop, condition or variable naturally, in either language.

### Signs to slow down

Most of the hour is copying from a teacher's screen. Every project looks identical. Your child cannot say what any line does.

In an English-taught class, if your child is still lost after three or four sessions, ask for a slower group, try one-to-one, or choose a Spanish-language class for now. There is no prize for struggling.

Online access matters too. INEI reports that 92.5% of Peruvians aged 12 to 18 used the internet in the second quarter of 2026, but only 68.2% of children aged 6 to 11 did. For younger children, a parent nearby for the first sessions makes live online classes far smoother.
