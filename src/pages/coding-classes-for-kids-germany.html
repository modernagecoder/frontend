Coding classes for kids in Germany

Two ten year olds in the same grade can have very different first years of computing in Germany. In Nordrhein-Westfalen every fifth grader now has Informatik on the timetable; in Hessen no school type has to teach it at all. This page sets out, Land by Land, when school computing begins, what a child of 6 to 12 can learn before that, what our lessons in English are like, and a first data project that sorts the sixteen Länder three different ways.

School computing by Land

At what age do children in Germany start computing at school?

It depends on the Land, and the gap is wide. The Gesellschaft für Informatik tracks every Land in its Informatik-Monitor; the table below reads its 2026/27 pages for the first grade in which computing is compulsory.

By the Monitor's count, six Länder still have no Informatik that every pupil must take; Hessen, Brandenburg and Bremen are among them. "First compulsory grade" is our reading of each Land page; where it depends on the school type, both are shown.

Grade 5 is about age 10

Children in Germany start school in the year they turn six, with each Land setting a cut-off date that the KMK says must fall between 30 June and 30 September. So grade 5, the earliest start in the table, usually means a child of 10 or 11. In Berlin and Brandenburg, where Grundschule runs to grade 6, the first compulsory computing comes later still.

For a child of 6 to 9, then, there is no Land in Germany where the timetable guarantees a computing lesson.

Primary school is next

The KMK published a key-points paper in 2024, "zur Verankerung von Medienbildung und informatischer Bildung in der Grundschule": on anchoring media education and informatics education in primary school. That is a direction of travel, not a lesson your child has this year.

Until it arrives, a curious seven year old in Germany learns to code at home, in a club, or in a class like ours.

This matters less as a race and more as a head start. A child who has spent a year or two putting steps in order, repeating them and fixing mistakes arrives at grade 5 or grade 7 with the habit that school Informatik is trying to build. Our Informatik Companion course then follows the school subject itself, in English, from grade 5.

Taught in English

Can a child in Germany learn to code in English?

Yes, and most do it more easily than their parents expect. Every class is live and in English only; the mentor fits the English to the child.

Live, never recorded

Your child joins a real lesson with a real mentor, on video, and builds while the mentor watches and helps. Nothing is a pre-recorded course the child must push through alone.

English that fits the child

Mentors slow down for a six year old, use short words, show before they tell, and repeat in a new way when a child looks lost. They also get used to each child's accent, and a child who answers half in German is understood and given the English word.

The code does not change

Scratch blocks look the same in every classroom, and Python's words, such as print, if and for, are English already. A child in Germany who learns them in English is learning them in their real form.

The mentors work from India and every lesson is in English; there is no German-language option, and we say so before you book. For families at a bilingual or international school this is simply the classroom language. For a child at a German Grundschule it is a second gain on top of the coding: an hour of English spoken for a purpose, about something the child chose to build.

A first data project

Which Land is the biggest? It depends what you sort by

Ask a class of ten year olds in Germany which Land is the biggest and you will hear Bayern and Nordrhein-Westfalen. Both are right, and that is the lesson: a computer sorts by exactly the thing you tell it to.

The data

The Statistisches Bundesamt, Destatis, publishes a small workbook listing every Land with its area in square kilometres and its population on 31 December 2024, counted on the basis of the 2022 census. Its licence line allows reproduction with the source named.

We copy the sixteen rows into a file called laender.csv with three columns: land, km2 and people. Sixteen rows is small enough to check by hand, which is the point.

The question

Which Land is the biggest, and which the smallest? The program answers twice, once by area and once by people, using the same list and one small function for each way of measuring.

Then it invents a third measure the children have not met: people per square kilometre. That one gives a winner nobody guesses.

Bayern covers about 70,542 km2 and Nordrhein-Westfalen about 34,113, yet Nordrhein-Westfalen has more people: 18,034,454 against 13,248,928. So "biggest" was never one question. It was two, and the program needed to be told which. Bremen is smallest both ways, the one easy answer.

The last step is the one we care about most. The workbook has its own column of people per square kilometre, so the children compare. All sixteen of our rounded answers match the Destatis column, 16 of 16. A program that agrees with a trusted published number has earned some trust; one that disagrees has found either a mistake in the code or a question worth asking.

  1. Ages 6 to 7, no screen. Cut out sixteen cards, one per Land, with a dot sticker for every million people. Line them up by dots. Now line them up by how big the shape is on a map. Did the order change?
  2. Ages 8 to 9, Scratch. Put the populations in a list and write a script that walks through it, keeping the largest so far. Then change one block so it keeps the smallest.
  3. Ages 10 to 12, in Python. Type and run the first version, then try the second on your own before you read ours, and check every answer against the Destatis column.
  4. Going further. Berlin, Hamburg and Bremen take the three most crowded places. All three are city-states. Find out what a city-state is, and why that puts them at the top.

Age by age

What should a child in Germany learn first, at 6, 8 and 10?

The right first step depends far more on reading and patience than on the grade. These are the starting points we use, and they move with the child.

At 6 and 7

Short lessons of stories and moving characters, made from coloured Scratch blocks with almost nothing to type. The goal is to put steps in order, notice when something goes wrong, and try again without upset.

At 8 and 9

Real games in Scratch: a score, a timer, a level that gets harder. Children meet variables and conditions without the names first, and finish projects they want to show their family.

At 10 to 12

Typed Python for children who read well: drawings with turtle, small games, and data like the Länder file. This is the age at which most Länder start school Informatik, so the overlap helps.

We do not teach children to copy answers or to paste code from an AI tool. A child who can explain why a loop runs four times, or find the one block that makes a sprite vanish, has learned something that lasts. That is the measure we report to parents, and it is the same in every country we teach in.

  2. Get a placement. You hear which course and which age group fit, or whether one to one suits better.
  3. Two classes a week. The same mentor each time, building on the last lesson, with short notes for you on what was made.
  4. Move up when ready. A child moves from Scratch to Python when they are ready, not on a fixed date.

Class times

When are classes in German time?

After school and at weekends. Germany is three and a half or four and a half hours behind India, so a German afternoon is an Indian evening, which suits the mentors well.

Three example slots, with the clock on the mentor's desk
Your clock in GermanyMentor in India, winterMentor in India, after 28 March 2027
15:4520:1519:15
Saturday 11:0015:3014:30
Sunday 10:0014:3013:30

The Einheitenzeitgesetz fixes German legal time: MEZ, one hour on top of UTC, and MESZ in summer, two hours on top. German clocks went back on 25 October 2026 and go forward again on 28 March 2027, while Indian clocks never change (UTC+5:30). That leaves a gap of four and a half hours this winter and three and a half from late March. The slots are examples only; no batch is tied to them.

The booking page works in your own time zone and confirms the class in German time. Autumn, Christmas and Easter holidays differ from Land to Land, and classes carry on through them unless you ask to pause.

Parents in Germany ask

Coding classes for kids in Germany: common questions

Are the classes in German?

No. We teach in English, and only in English, live, with mentors who work from India. They keep sentences short, show on screen before they explain, and get used to how each child talks, so a child with school English can follow from the first lesson.

At what age can a child in Germany start?

From 6. Six and seven year olds begin with Scratch, where blocks snap together and little reading is needed. From about 9, children who read comfortably can move to typed Python.

Does school in Germany teach coding?

Later, and it depends on the Land. The Informatik-Monitor 2026/27 shows compulsory computing from grade 5 in Länder such as Nordrhein-Westfalen, Baden-Württemberg, Mecklenburg-Vorpommern and Thüringen, from grade 7 in several others, and not for every pupil in six Länder, including Hessen and Brandenburg.

Is there any computing in Grundschule?

Not as a compulsory subject in any Land at present. The KMK published a key-points paper in 2024 on anchoring informatics education in primary school, but for a child of 6 to 9 in Germany, coding today happens outside the timetable.

When do classes take place in German time?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. A class at 15:45 in Germany is 20:15 for the mentor in India until 28 March 2027.

Does it matter which Land we live in?

Not for our classes, which are online and the same everywhere. It matters for school, because the year Informatik starts differs by Land, so we place older children with that in mind.

How many children are in a group?

A group class has fifteen to twenty children close in age and one mentor. Families who want private lessons choose one to one instead: two classes a week, just your child and the mentor.

Why is the fee in euro but the payment in dollars?

We show the monthly fee in euro, worked out from the European Central Bank reference rate, so you can compare it easily. The card is charged in US dollars, and your own bank turns that into euro on the day, which is why a statement can be a few cents out. Trying the first class costs nothing.

Will my child just watch videos?

No. Classes are live, and your child does the building while the mentor guides. Children are asked to explain what their code does, not to copy it, and never to paste answers from an AI tool.

What does my child need at home?

A laptop or desktop computer with a keyboard, a stable internet connection, and headphones with a microphone if the room is noisy. Scratch and Python both run free in a browser or on the computer.
