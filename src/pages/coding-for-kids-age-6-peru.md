# Coding for kids age 6 in Peru

Six is the age when most children in Peru walk into 1° de primaria, still learning to read and very ready to play. A coding class at this age looks less like typing and more like a game with rules: drag a block, watch a cat move, try again. ## Can a six year old really learn to code?

Yes, as long as "coding" means giving clear instructions in order and watching what happens, not typing lines of text. That is the real skill under every program, and six year olds are surprisingly good at it.

### What a six year old can do

Put three or four steps in the right order so a character walks to a door, opens it and says hello. Notice when the order is wrong, because the character walks into the wall instead. Use a "repeat" block once they see the same steps coming round again.

React to a click or a key: when the green flag is pressed, the cat starts; when the space bar is pressed, it jumps. Simple cause and effect is the heart of coding at six, and the child sees it the moment they press the key.

### What can wait until later

Typing words of code. Most children are ready for that between nine and ten, once reading and spelling feel easy. Before then, blocks do the same job with no spelling to get wrong.

Long projects, variables that keep a score, and anything that needs a child to hold more than three or four ideas in their head at once. We bring those in around seven or eight, when the child asks for them by trying to build a game that needs them.

A good sign that coding suits your six year old: they like to arrange things. Toy cars in a line, stickers in a row, a story told in the right order. Coding gives that instinct a screen and a purpose. Children who do not show it yet often grow into it after a few weeks of play; it is a habit, not a talent.

## What does first grade in Peru give a six year old in computing?

A great deal of reading, writing, numbers and English, and no separate computing lesson. Coding, if it happens at school at this age, depends on the school.

### Where a six year old sits

Under Ley 28044, the education law of 2003, primaria "dura seis años"; the Reglamento that puts the law into practice opens primaria to children "a partir de los 6 años de edad". Before that, the law describes inicial as serving children of 3 to 5 in a school setting.

MINEDU places each child by their age on 31 March of the school year, which it calls the edad normativa. So a child who is six by that date belongs in 1° de primaria, while one who turns six in April waits a year and is then among the oldest in the room. For that reason our groups follow a child's age and stage, never the grade printed on a school report.

### What the national plan sets for 1°

In MINEDU's national curriculum, the CNEB, the weekly plan for 1° de primaria gives Matemática 5 teaching periods, Comunicación 5 and Inglés 2. A teaching period, the hora pedagógica, lasts 45 minutes, so a first grader in a school that follows the plan gets about 90 minutes of English a week.

Digital skills are not an area of their own. The CNEB lists "Se desenvuelve en entornos virtuales generados por las TIC" among the competencies shared across all areas, the competencias transversales. How much screen work a first grader actually does is therefore up to each school.

We say this plainly so nobody is misled: Modern Age Coders is not linked to MINEDU, to any UGEL or to any school in Peru. We award no Peruvian grades. A child who completes one of our courses receives a certificate of completion issued by us, and nothing more official than that.

### Reading the calendar as a parent

The management weeks are the gaps. In 2026 there was a week in late May, two weeks from late July into early August, and a week in mid-October. For a six year old, starting coding in one of those weeks means the first two or three classes happen without homework competing for energy.

The longest gap of all is the summer, from January until the school year opens in March. In 2026 the first management block began on 2 March, so January and February sat outside the school calendar entirely.

## What does a parent do during an online class for a six year old?

In the first few weeks, quite a lot; after that, very little. A six year old needs help with the set-up and a calm adult within earshot, not a second teacher.

1. **Choose the machine.** A laptop or desktop with a mouse works far better than anything else. Small hands find a trackpad hard, and a phone is too small for blocks and a mentor's face at the same time.
2. **Fit a headset.** Children's headsets with a built-in microphone cut out the noise of a busy home and help the child hear English clearly.
3. **Sit nearby for the first classes.** Help with logging in and with the mouse, then step back. When the mentor asks your child a question, let your child answer, even slowly, even in a mix of words.
4. **Stay within earshot later.** After three or four weeks most six year olds manage alone, as long as an adult can be called if the internet drops.
5. **Ask for a show-and-tell.** After each class, ask your child to show you one thing they made and to tell you what it does. That one minute fixes the lesson in their memory.

### What Peruvian homes have

INEI's household survey for the second quarter of 2026 found that 68.2% of children aged 6 to 11 in Peru used the internet, the lowest share of any age group except people over 60. In Lima Metropolitana, 84.7% of households had an internet service.

In that same quarter only 36.4% of homes across the country owned so much as one computer. If the family laptop is shared, choose a class hour when nobody else needs it; with timing that is entirely flexible, that is easy to arrange.

### Screen time, kept sensible

A class for a six year old is a short, active session: the child is building and talking, not watching. We keep the screen part tight and fill the rest with things to do with the hands, such as the toy lesson below.

If your child is tired, it is fine to stop early and pick up next time. Pushing a six year old through a bad day teaches them that coding is a chore, which is the one lesson we never want them to learn.

## What does a first sorting lesson look like? Lining up toys by size

Sorting is one of the oldest problems in computing, and a six year old can solve it with five toys on the floor. The lesson is ours; we run it in the first month with children of six and seven.

### The game, with toys

Your child brings five toys to the camera: say a lion, a duck, a bear, a frog and a cat, all different sizes. The mentor asks for them in a line, smallest first. Most six year olds just shuffle them about until it looks right, which works but cannot be explained.

So the mentor gives one rule to follow, and only one. **Find the smallest toy. Put it at the front. Now do the same with the toys that are left.** The child says the rule out loud in English, then plays it, round by round.

### What the child notices

In some rounds nothing moves, because the smallest toy left is already in the right place. Children find this funny and slightly suspicious: did we do the round at all? Yes, we looked at every toy, and looking counts.

By the end the child has done something real programmers do: followed the same short rule again and again until the job is finished. That is a loop, and this way of sorting has a name, selection sort.

Then the mentor shows the same game on screen, written in Python. A six year old does not type this. We show it so that the child can watch the computer play their game, and so that you, the parent, can see the rule your child has just used turned into code. The numbers are the toys' sizes in centimetres, which we made up.

**The toy line-up, as the mentor runs it in Python** (Python)
```python
toys = [("lion", 9), ("duck", 4), ("bear", 12), ("frog", 2), ("cat", 7)]

comparisons = 0
for start in range(len(toys)):
    smallest = start
    for i in range(start + 1, len(toys)):
        comparisons = comparisons + 1
        if toys[i][1] < toys[smallest][1]:
            smallest = i
    toys[start], toys[smallest] = toys[smallest], toys[start]
    print("Round", start + 1, ":", [name for name, size in toys])

print("Comparisons:", comparisons)
```

**What it prints** (Output)
```output
Round 1 : ['frog', 'duck', 'bear', 'lion', 'cat']
Round 2 : ['frog', 'duck', 'bear', 'lion', 'cat']
Round 3 : ['frog', 'duck', 'cat', 'lion', 'bear']
Round 4 : ['frog', 'duck', 'cat', 'lion', 'bear']
Round 5 : ['frog', 'duck', 'cat', 'lion', 'bear']
Comparisons: 10
```
Exact output when we ran it with Python 3 (python3 -I). Rounds 2, 4 and 5 change nothing, just as on the floor, and the computer compared two toys 10 times in all.

Ten comparisons for five toys comes from 4 + 3 + 2 + 1: in the first round you check four toys against the smallest so far, then three, then two, then one. Older children in our classes later work out that ten toys take 45 comparisons, and start to wonder whether there is a quicker way. There is, and that question leads to a lot of good computing.

1. **Our practice, on the floor.** Line up five spoons by length using the rule. Say "smallest to the front" each round.
2. **Our practice, a twist.** Now line them up biggest first. What one word in the rule has to change?
3. **Our practice, counting.** With three toys, how many times do you compare two of them? Try it, then try four toys.
4. **Our practice, in Scratch (with the mentor).** Drag three sprites into a row and make each one say its size when clicked.

## Which English words will a six year old hear in a coding class?

Short, concrete ones, said slowly and shown on screen at the same moment. By the end of the first month most six year olds understand all of these and use several.

**The words our mentors use most with six year olds (our classroom list)**

| Word | What it means in class | What the child does |
|---|---|---|
| Click | Press the mouse button once | Clicks the green flag to start |
| Drag | Hold the button down and move | Pulls a block into place |
| Move, turn | The two things a sprite does most | Snaps a "move" or "turn" block on |
| Repeat | Do it again, a set number of times | Wraps steps in a repeat block |
| First, next, last | The order of the steps | Says the steps aloud in order |
| Bigger, smaller | Comparing sizes | Sorts toys and sprites |
| Try again | A mistake is fine, run it once more | Runs the program after a change |
| Show me | Point to it on your screen | Moves the mouse to the block |

Classes for six year olds are in English only, but the mentor never rushes and never makes a child feel slow. If a child answers in Spanish, the mentor nods, says the English word back, and moves on. Understanding comes long before speaking, and that is perfectly normal at six.

## Should a six year old use AI tools?

Not chatbots. The companies that make them do not allow children this young, and a six year old has better things to do than ask a machine for answers.

### What the companies say

OpenAI's terms for ChatGPT say "You must be at least 13 years old". Google says that to use Gemini with a personal or school account "You must be 13 (or the applicable age in your country) or over". Neither tool is meant for a six year old, and we never put one in front of a child this age.

Our full list of tools and the ages their makers allow is in our [AI tools age guide](/ai-tools-age-guide), which quotes each company's own terms.

### What AI looks like at six

At six, AI is a game of sorting and guessing done with the mentor: which of these pictures shows a cat, and how did you know? Later, around eight, children try Teachable Machine, a Google tool that trains in the browser without signing in, and see a computer learn from examples they show it.

The habit we build now is the one that matters later: think first, test second, and do not believe something just because a machine says it.

## How does a six year old grow as a coder over a year?

In small, visible steps. These four stages are ours; they tell the mentor what to work on next and tell you what to look for at home.

**Our four stages for the first year, for children of six**

| Stage | Usually by | On screen | Out loud |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Order | Month 1 to 2 | Puts three or four blocks in order so a sprite reaches a goal | "First move, then turn." |
| 2. Repeat | Month 3 to 4 | Uses a repeat block instead of copying the same block | "Repeat four times." |
| 3. Events | Month 5 to 7 | Makes a sprite react to a click or a key | "When I press space, it jumps." |
| 4. A small game | Month 8 to 12 | Builds a short game with a start and an end | "You win when the cat finds the fish." |

### For parents

Look for the child explaining, not just clicking. A six year old who can tell you why the cat stopped has learnt more than one who made it move faster.
Progress at this age is uneven. A slow month is often followed by a jump; keep the routine and let it come.

### For your six year old

If the cat goes the wrong way, that is a clue, not a mistake. Look at the blocks and find the one that sent it there.
You can always press the green flag again. Coders try things lots of times.

## Coding at age 6: what parents in Peru ask

### Are the classes in Spanish?

No. Every class is live, online and taught in English only, by mentors who speak slowly and clearly and adjust to your child's pace and accent. At six, most of the work is clicking, dragging and watching, and the mentor shows each step on the screen while saying it, so a child who is new to English can still follow.

### Is six too young to start coding?

No. Blocks suit a six year old very well, because a child can build a working program without reading or spelling. What is too early at six is typed code; we leave that until about nine.

### My child cannot read well yet. Can they still join?

Yes. Scratch blocks have colours and shapes as well as words, and the mentor reads each one aloud. Many six year olds in our classes are still learning to read in Spanish and in English, and coding often gives reading a push.

### Does my child need to know how to use a mouse?

It helps, but it is not required. In the first classes the mentor practises clicking and dragging with your child. A small mouse is much easier for six year olds than a trackpad.

### How long is a class for a six year old?

Classes are kept short and active for this age, with a hands-on task in the middle so the child is not staring at the screen the whole time. Your mentor will explain the exact length for your group or one-to-one plan at the free class.

### What time are the classes in Peru?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. Because the gap never changes, 17:00 for your child is always 03:30 the following morning for the mentor in India.

### Does first grade in Peru already teach coding?

No, not as a subject of its own. MINEDU's national curriculum gives 1° de primaria periods of Matemática, Comunicación and Inglés among others, but no computing area; digital skills are treated as a competency shared by all subjects. Some private schools add their own computing classes.

### Will my six year old use ChatGPT?

No. OpenAI's terms say users must be at least 13, and Google sets 13 for Gemini on personal and school accounts. Six year olds in our classes learn what AI is through games with the mentor, never through adult chatbots.

### Can siblings of different ages share a class?

Not in a group: we form groups by age so a six year old is with other six year olds. Brothers and sisters can each have their own group, or share a one-to-one plan if the mentor agrees it will work for both.

### Are you connected with MINEDU or a Peruvian school?

No. We are an independent online school, our mentors work from India, and we have no link to MINEDU, a UGEL or any school in Peru. We give no Peruvian grades; children who finish a course get a completion certificate from us.
