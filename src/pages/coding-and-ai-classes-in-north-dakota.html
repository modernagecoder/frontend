North Dakota, United States

Coding, cybersecurity and AI classes in North Dakota

Is computer science a graduation requirement in North Dakota?

Yes, with cybersecurity as an equal option. After HB 1398, passed by the 68th Legislative Assembly in 2023, students graduating after July 31, 2025 take one unit of computer science or cybersecurity, which can count as one of their three science units, unless they completed their school’s integration plan. Modern Age Coders teaches both subjects live online, from kindergarten AI literacy to picoCTF practice.

Most states that write computer science into law treat it alone. North Dakota pairs it with cybersecurity at every level: in the century code that approves schools, in the content standards the department rewrote in 2025, and in the unit a student needs to graduate. The pairing changes what a class should look like. A ninth grader in Fargo or Minot is expected to understand how a network moves data and how that same network is attacked and defended. This page lays out HB 1398 year by year as the Department of Public Instruction (NDDPI) summarizes it, explains the May 2025 standards and how they bring artificial intelligence in from kindergarten, reads the published access figures with the caveat they need, and shows a sampling project on a North Dakota dataset of more than a hundred thousand mapped landmarks.

HB 1398

North Dakota’s computer science and cybersecurity law, year by year

NDDPI published a Bill Breakdown of HB 1398 on 26 April 2023, and its current Computer Science and Cybersecurity page names the sections the 68th Legislative Assembly amended. We read both on 17 October 2026.

The waiver

NDDPI’s breakdown says the graduation unit is waived for a student who has completed the school’s computer science and cybersecurity integration plan, so the plan is a second route to the same goal.

From math to science

Before HB 1398, the breakdown notes, one unit of computer science could stand in for one of three math requirements. The new law moves the substitution to science.

Either subject counts

A unit of cybersecurity meets the requirement just as a unit of computer science does. Families can choose the route that suits the student rather than the one a neighboring state would pick.

The department’s guidance on integration plans argues that a single exposure in the upper grades is not sufficient to build a foundation, and asks schools to map the standards across every grade. Schools may choose to require an approved course as part of the plan, or have a qualified staff member, such as a licensed library media specialist, help classroom teachers deliver the standards. One question NDDPI tells schools to answer is what happens to a student who transfers in during eleventh grade after everyone else took the ninth grade course.

The May 2025 standards

Five concepts, written grade by grade, with AI from kindergarten

NDDPI’s committee began in July 2024 and finished the revised Computer Science and Cybersecurity Content Standards in May 2025, after five multi-day sessions. The department asks schools to fully implement them in 2026 to 2027.

Grade by grade to eighth

Kindergarten through grade 8 each have their own standards; high school is written in two spans, grades 9-10 and 11-12. A code such as 2.NI.1 means grade 2, Networks and Internet, standard 1.

AI starts at five

The first AI standard is for kindergarten: K.NI.3 Understand that while AI may respond like a human, it is a machine. By 9-10.NI.3, students explain how generative AI models process inputs and produce outputs.

No device required

The committee wrote the standards so that districts are not required to use any specific hardware or software, which keeps them workable in large and very small districts alike.

In the department’s words

The State Superintendent’s foreword to the May 2025 standards describes North Dakota as one of the first states in the nation to require all students to learn computer science and cybersecurity throughout their K-12 experience. We quote that as the department’s own statement from 2025, not as a ranking we have checked.

The 2025 document replaces the 2019 standards, and NDDPI publishes a crosswalk between the two. A student in grade 6 this year may therefore meet AI and security content their older sibling never saw at that age; classes outside school can fill the gap for students who started under the earlier version.

Signature project

Can 400 random points describe 104,829? A sampling project on North Dakota data

The state GIS hub publishes Landmarks NDDOT, a point layer of mapped features across North Dakota. It is big enough that sampling becomes a real question, and the server can still give the full count to check against.

Our seeded random samples against the full count, Landmarks NDDOT downloaded 17 October 2026 (sample results are ours; full counts from a server group-by)
Feature typeFull countShare of allSample of 100Sample of 400Sample of 1,600
Farm Unit33,90432.34%30.00%31.50%33.25%
Wind Turbine1,2181.16%0.00%1.25%1.38%
Grain Elevator1820.17%0.00%0.00%0.19%

1. Draw the sample

Students fetch all 104,829 object IDs, then use random.Random(2026).sample to pick 1,600 of them, so anyone rerunning the code gets the same draw.

2. Estimate, then check

They estimate each feature type’s share from the first 100, 400 and 1,600 picks, and compare with the true share from a group-by over all 223 feature types.

3. Spot the rare-case trap

Farm units are close at every size. A sample of 100 finds no wind turbines and 400 finds no grain elevators at all, which is how rare things vanish from small surveys.

What these numbers are

The sample percentages are ours, from one seeded draw on one day. A different seed gives different numbers, and that variation is the lesson. The state says the layer follows conventional cartographic standards, is not a final or formal publication, and is not guaranteed free from errors.

For an extension, students list feature types that look like data-entry quirks, such as a type called Asterisk with 3,334 records and a lower-case railway crossing label, and discuss whether to clean or keep them. Estimation, error and messy categories are the Algorithms and Computational Thinking and Impacts of Computing concepts of the 2025 standards, practised on a map of the student’s own state.

Coding and AI

North Dakota writes AI into security, so students should too

The 2025 standards treat AI as part of computing devices, algorithms, impacts, citizenship and security at once.

An AI model can summarize a dataset in a sentence. Ask it what share of North Dakota landmarks are wind turbines and it may give a confident figure from nowhere. A student who has drawn a sample of 100 and found zero turbines knows exactly how a plausible number can be badly wrong.

The same instinct protects systems. The 2025 standards note that AI can strengthen threat detection while adding risks such as adversarial attacks and privacy concerns. Students who build and test their own code learn to treat an AI answer as a claim to verify, which is the core habit of security work.

North Dakota asks graduates to understand both how computers work and how they are defended; writing code and checking AI output against real data covers both. The longer argument is in is learning to code still worth it.

FAQ

North Dakota coding, cyber and AI questions

Answers for North Dakota families, drawn from NDDPI’s own pages.

Do North Dakota students need computer science to graduate?

NDDPI says students graduating after July 31, 2025 must take one unit of computer science or cybersecurity, which may count as one of three science units, unless they completed the school integration plan.

Does cybersecurity count instead of computer science?

Yes. Under NDCC 15.1-21-02.2 as amended by HB 1398, a unit of either computer science or cybersecurity meets the graduation requirement.

What is a computer science and cybersecurity integration plan?

A school-board-approved plan, required from 2024 to 2025, that maps the standards across grades. NDDPI says completing it waives the separate graduation unit.

When do the new North Dakota standards take effect?

NDDPI finished the revised standards in May 2025 and asks schools to fully implement them in 2026 to 2027. They replace the 2019 standards.

Do the North Dakota standards include AI?

Yes. The first is K.NI.3, which asks kindergartners to understand that AI may respond like a human but is a machine. High school standards cover generative AI models.

How many North Dakota high schools offer computer science?

Code.org (2025) reports 148 of 176 high schools in 2023 to 2024, a figure that includes cybersecurity for North Dakota only, so it is not comparable with other states.

Which time zone are classes in?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. We confirm times in Central or Mountain Time, whichever your part of North Dakota uses.

Can your classes satisfy the graduation unit?

No. The unit comes from courses your school offers or makes available, and NDDPI lists approved routes. Our classes build skills beside them; assessed school work is the student's own.

Is the cybersecurity course safe for teens?

It is defensive and ethical by design, matching the Security concept of the 2025 standards: protecting networks, devices and data, never attacking systems the student does not own.

What does the sampling project teach?

Using 104,829 records from the state Landmarks NDDOT layer, students see how sample estimates compare with the full count, and why a sample of 100 can miss rare features entirely.
