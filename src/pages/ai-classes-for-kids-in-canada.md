# AI classes for kids in Canada

Children in Canada hear about AI every day, and many have watched an older sibling or a parent type into a chatbot. What they rarely get is an explanation of what is happening inside, or a chance to build a small one and watch it fail. This page sets out what the AI companies themselves allow at each age, where AI already appears in Canadian school documents, what a child of 8 to 12 can genuinely build, and one complete project on the City of Victoria's tree data in which a learned guesser loses to a child's rule, and why that teaches more than any win.

## AI tool age rules, in the companies' own words

Parents are often told that a child "can use" a chatbot. The companies' terms are more specific. These lines are copied from each company's current terms or help pages, read on 22 October 2026. Where a page gave no clear age, we say nothing.

**Age rules for AI tools a child may hear about, from each company's own pages (read 22 October 2026)**

| Tool | What the company says | What it means for a child under 13 |
|---|---|---|
| ChatGPT (OpenAI) | "ChatGPT is not meant for children under 13, and we require that children ages 13 to 18 obtain parental consent before using ChatGPT." | Not for them. In a classroom for under-13s, OpenAI says "the actual interaction with ChatGPT must be conducted by an adult." |
| OpenAI services generally | "You must be at least 13 years old"; "If you are under 18 you must have your parent or legal guardian's permission to use the Services." (terms effective January 1, 2026) | No account of their own |
| Claude (Anthropic) | "You must be at least 18 years old or the minimum age required to consent to use the Services in your location, whichever is higher." (effective Oct 8, 2025) | Not for children or teenagers at all |
| Google Account | "If you're under the age required to manage your own Google Account, you must have your parent or legal guardian's permission to use a Google Account." (effective July 30, 2026) | A parent decides, and supervises |
| Gemini Apps with a Google AI plan | "To use Gemini Apps with a Google AI plan in most countries, you must be 13 (or the applicable age in your country)" | The paid plan is not for under-13s |

### What we do with these rules

We follow them, and our courses are written around them. The AI Literacy for Kids syllabus says it never asks a child to create an account on a service that does not permit their age. Younger children see adult chatbots only when the teacher runs them on screen, which is exactly the arrangement OpenAI describes for classrooms with children under 13.

We never recommend Claude; that is our rule as much as Anthropic's. It is an 18-and-over product, and no page or class of ours for children or teens suggests it.

### Why the rules help, not hinder

A child who cannot open a chatbot is not missing the important part. The important part of AI, for a ten year old, is the idea that a machine learns a pattern from examples, and that the pattern can be wrong. That idea can be taught with a spreadsheet, a few photos and some patience.

Children who understand it are better protected later, when they are old enough for the tools: they know to ask where an answer came from and how it was checked.

A good AI class for children is mostly making, testing and arguing about results. The projects below come from our own course syllabuses; the age bands are our guidance.

| Age | Project | The idea underneath |

### Learn

How a machine turns examples into a pattern, taught with cards, drawings and tiny datasets before any screen.

### Doubt

Why an AI can be confident and wrong: missing examples, lopsided examples, and questions it was never trained on.

### Explain

Every child presents a project and answers two questions: how did you test it, and when does it fail?

## Garry oak or Douglas fir? A tree guesser on Victoria's data

The City of Victoria publishes a database of its trees under a licence that allows commercial reuse, with each tree's species, height and crown width. With 10 to 12 year olds we turn it into the simplest kind of machine learning there is: look at examples, find the closest one, copy its answer. Then we test it fairly. We wrote the program; each result comes from running it on the city's copy fetched 22 October 2026.

The question: given only a tree's height and width in metres, can the computer tell a Garry oak from a Douglas fir? The file has 5,200 Garry oaks and 1,300 Douglas firs, all with both measurements. A typical Garry oak in the file is 9 metres tall; a typical Douglas fir, 16.

**Our program · tree_guesser.py** (python)
```python
# Our tree guesser: Garry oak or Douglas fir? (Modern Age Coders, written for this page)
import csv

oaks, firs = [], []
with open("victoria-trees.csv", encoding="utf-8-sig") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        try:
            tree = (float(row["Height"]), float(row["Width"]))
        except ValueError:
            continue                       # skip trees with a missing measurement
        if row["CommonName"] == "Garry oak":
            oaks.append(tree)
        elif row["CommonName"] == "Douglas fir":
            firs.append(tree)

# Examples to learn from, and different trees to test on. Same number of each kind.
train = [(t, "oak") for t in oaks[:600]] + [(t, "fir") for t in firs[:600]]
test = [(t, "oak") for t in oaks[600:900]] + [(t, "fir") for t in firs[600:900]]

def guess(tree):
    # Find the training tree whose height and width are closest, and copy its answer.
    closest = min(train, key=lambda ex: (ex[0][0] - tree[0]) ** 2 + (ex[0][1] - tree[1]) ** 2)
    return closest[1]

def my_rule(tree):
    return "fir" if tree[0] > 12 else "oak"   # a rule a child writes by hand

for name, method in [("nearest neighbour", guess), ("hand rule", my_rule)]:
    right = sum(1 for tree, answer in test if method(tree) == answer)
    print(name, right, "of", len(test), "right")
print("test on the training trees:", sum(1 for t, a in train if guess(t) == a), "of", len(train))
```

**Output, Victoria tree file** (text)
```text
nearest neighbour 351 of 600 right
hand rule 394 of 600 right
test on the training trees: 930 of 1200
```
The learned guesser gets 351 of 600 new trees right. The child's one-line rule gets 394. On trees it has already seen, the guesser looks far better: 930 of 1200.

**Every run we made on the same 600 test trees (our results)**

| Method | Right out of 600 | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| Always guess "oak" | 300 | The test set is half oaks and half firs, so guessing one answer gets exactly half right |
| Nearest neighbour, oaks listed first | 351 | It guessed oak 537 times, because many trees share the same measurements and ties went to whichever tree came first |
| Nearest neighbour, firs listed first | 373 | Same method, different order, different score: a sign the method is fragile |
| Vote of the 31 nearest trees | 383 | Asking many neighbours smooths out the ties |
| Hand rule: taller than 12 m means fir | 394 | A child who looked at the data and thought about it beat every learned version |

### Why the computer lost, and why that is good

Heights and widths in the file are recorded in whole metres, so the 1,200 training trees have only 242 different pairs of measurements. Lots of oaks and firs look identical to the program. When two examples are equally close, our first version simply took the one it met first, and the oaks were listed first.

Children usually expect the "AI" to win. Watching it lose to their own rule, and then finding the reason, teaches more about machine learning than any winning demo: a model only knows what its examples can show, and a fair test is the only way to find out.

### The trap every adult falls into too

Tested on its own training trees, the guesser scores 930 of 1200, which looks impressive. On new trees it drops to 351 of 600. That gap is why the program keeps the test trees separate, and why we ask children, "would you trust a spelling test where you saw the answers first?"

The same lesson sits behind every serious AI system: report the score on examples the model has never seen, next to a simple rule it has to beat.

## A learn-from-examples game for the kitchen table

No screen needed. This is our own activity, and it contains the whole idea of the Victoria project in twenty minutes.

1. Collect examples. Cut out or draw 20 pictures of animals. On the back of each, write one fact: "lives in water" or "lives on land".
2. Split them. Put 12 pictures in a "learning" pile and 8 in a "test" pile. Do not peek at the test pile.
3. Learn a pattern. The child studies the learning pile, front and back, and writes one rule, for example "if it has fins, it lives in water".
4. Test fairly. Turn over the test pictures one at a time. The child applies the rule to the front only, then checks the back. Count how many were right out of 8.
5. Find the failure. Which test picture fooled the rule? A penguin, a frog, a whale? Change the rule and test again on new pictures, not the same ones.
6. Cheat on purpose. Now test the rule on the learning pile. It scores higher. Ask why that score should not be trusted.

Step 6 is the most important. It is the kitchen-table version of the 930 against 351 in the Victoria project, and children who have done it once rarely forget it.

## Children who understand AI, rather than children who copy from it

Our promise to families in Canada and everywhere else is that children learn to reason their own way to an answer; copying one, from a person or from an AI, is not the goal. For an AI class that means three things.

### The child does the reasoning

In every project, the child decides what the examples are, what the rule should be and how to test it. A tool can help with the counting; it never makes those choices.

### Every result is questioned

A score is never accepted on its own. "Compared to what? Tested on which examples?" are questions children learn to ask about their own models first.

### Mistakes are the material

A model that fails is the most useful thing in the room. Children are asked to make one fail on purpose and explain it.

### What we tell parents about chatbots

Under 13, the companies we checked say no account, and OpenAI says an adult must run the interaction in a classroom. Between 13 and 17, OpenAI requires a parent's consent; Claude remains closed until 18. These are the companies' rules, not ours, and they are the minimum.

If your child is curious, sit with them, ask the chatbot something you both know the answer to, and look for the mistake together. That is an AI lesson in itself.

### Why this protects a child later

A child who has trained a guesser and watched it fail understands, years before anyone else in their class, that an AI answer is a guess built from examples. That child asks better questions when they are finally old enough to use the tools.

Our teachers report each class to parents in a few plain lines: what the child built, what failed, and what the child concluded.

## Four ideas a child should own by 12

Our milestones for an AI class, used to place a child and to report progress.

**Four AI ideas for children (ours)**

| Idea | The child can say |
|---|---|
| 1. Rules or examples | "This program follows a rule a person wrote; that one learned from examples." |
| 2. Examples decide | "It got this wrong because it never saw one like it." |
| 3. Test fairly | "You have to test on examples it has not seen." |
| 4. Beat a simple rule | "It is only good if it does better than an easy guess." |

### For parents

Idea 3 is the one most adults never learn. A child who owns it will read every AI headline more carefully.
Progress is measured in what the child can explain, not in how many tools they have tried.

### For children

When your model gets something wrong, do not fix it straight away. First say why it went wrong.
Always keep some examples hidden for testing. If you peek, the test is spoiled.

## AI classes for kids in Canada: common questions

### What age can a child start learning about AI?

Our AI Literacy for Kids course takes children from 8 and needs no coding. Children from about 10 can also build simple AI programs in typed Python in our Python and AI for Kids course.

### Can my child use ChatGPT?

Not on their own if they are under 13. OpenAI says ChatGPT is not meant for children under 13 and that children aged 13 to 18 need parental consent. For under-13s in a classroom, OpenAI says an adult must conduct the interaction.

### Is Claude suitable for children?

No. Anthropic's terms say users must be at least 18 years old, or older where local law requires. We never recommend Claude to anyone under 18.

### What do children actually build in an AI class?

Small models and programs of their own: sorting examples into rule-following or learning, training an image or sound model for a game, and for older children, a Python program that learns from real data and is tested on new examples.

### Will my child need to create accounts on AI websites?

No. Our AI Literacy for Kids course never asks a child to create an account on a service that does not permit their age. Younger children see adult tools only in teacher-run demonstrations.

### Is AI part of the school curriculum in Canada?

It is starting to be. Ontario's Grade 6 science asks students to assess the impact of emerging technologies, Ontario's Grade 10 ICD2O course investigates artificial intelligence, and Alberta's draft Grades 7 to 9 curriculum includes it.

### Does my child need to know how to code first?

Not for AI Literacy for Kids, which uses hands-on activities and block coding. The Python course teaches coding from the beginning, so children aged 9 to 12 can start there too.

### How do you keep AI lessons safe for children?

By following each company's age rules, using tools and demonstrations chosen for children, and keeping chatbots on the teacher's screen. Children spend most of the class building and testing their own small models.

### What times are classes for Canadian families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Every time is confirmed in your own province's zone.

### How will I know my child is learning?

After classes the teacher sends a short note: what your child built, where it failed and what they concluded. By the end, a child should be able to explain why a model must be tested on examples it has never seen.
