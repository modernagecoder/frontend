## Six questions that separate a strong class from a weak one

Use these with any provider in Houston, including us. A good class answers every one of them without hesitation.

**A checklist for judging a coding class for a child (our criteria)**

| Ask this | A strong answer | A warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Who reads my child's code? | A named teacher looks at the actual program in every class | Videos with a quiz at the end, and no person checking |
| How is the level set? | A trial lesson with a real task, then a recommendation | Placement by age alone |
| What does my child finish? | A game, an app or a data project they can show and explain | Badges, streaks and certificates without a working program |
| How big is the group? | A stated number, with children at the same stage | No number given, or mixed levels in one room |
| What happens when they get stuck? | The teacher asks questions until the child finds the bug | The answer is typed in for them |
| Is there a path after this? | A next course that builds on the last one | One short camp and nothing after it |

We wrote this table from what we watch go right and wrong in first lessons. Price matters, but it is the last question, not the first: a cheap class that never reads a child&rsquo;s code is expensive in lost months. In our classes the batch is fifteen to twenty children at the same level, or one child with one teacher, and every project is the student&rsquo;s own.

### Ages 6 to 8

Short sessions, blocks rather than typing, and lots of talking. The goal is a child who can say what their program will do before they press go.

### Ages 9 to 11

Bigger Scratch builds, the first variables and loops that count, and an early look at typed Python for children who are ready.

### Ages 12 and up

Typed code as the default: Python, JavaScript, or Luau for Roblox, with data and game projects that take several weeks.

## How big is a Houston park? A histogram from city data

The City of Houston&rsquo;s GIS office (COHGIS) publishes a map layer of city parks. We downloaded its attribute table on 18 October 2026 and sorted the park sizes into bins, the way a child would in a first data project.

**Houston park records by Park_Acres, in bins we chose (our calculation from the COHGIS Houston Parks layer, downloaded 18 October 2026)**

| Size band | Park records |
|---|---|
| Under 1 acre | 91 |
| 1 to under 5 acres | 106 |
| 5 to under 10 acres | 72 |
| 10 to under 25 acres | 59 |
| 25 to under 50 acres | 27 |
| 50 to under 100 acres | 6 |
| 100 to under 500 acres | 13 |
| 500 acres and over | 6 |

### 1. Count first

The layer held 380 park records on download day: 378 in Harris County and 2 in Fort Bend. A child checks that number before drawing anything, because a chart of the wrong rows is worse than no chart.

### 2. Try equal bins

With bins of 1,000 acres, almost every Houston park lands in the first bar and the chart says nothing. The largest single record, listed at 10,488.7 acres, stretches the axis so far that the small parks vanish.

### 3. Choose better bins

Bins that widen as they go (1, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 500) show the real shape: most parks are small, a few are enormous. Our median is about 4.75 acres, far below the average a big park drags upward.

### The answer is ours, not the city&rsquo;s

These bins and the median are our own calculation from one download. Most acreage values carry the source label GIS Value (367 records), with a handful from a survey, a parcel or a deed, and the city states that COHGIS data is for general reference and comes with no warranty. A child who learns to write that sentence under a chart has learned something most adults skip.

A second question uses the Park_Type column: 167 records are labelled Neighborhood Park, 22 Pocket Park, and 30 have no type at all. Counting the blanks before charting the rest is the habit we want. The first version of this project takes one or two classes in Python for a ten- or eleven-year-old; younger children do the same counting with sticky notes and a Scratch bar chart.

## Should a Houston child learn to code if AI can write programs?

Yes, and the park histogram shows why in one picture.

Ask an AI tool for a chart of Houston park sizes and it will happily draw the first, useless version with equal bins, one tall bar and a long empty tail. It will not notice that 30 parks have no type, or that the biggest record is a lease. A child who has built the chart by hand spots both in seconds.

That is the skill coding teaches young children: checking. They learn that a computer does exactly what it is told, so every result needs a reason. With that habit in place, AI tools become something a child can question later rather than something they simply believe.

We teach the checking first, with blocks and then Python, and bring in AI tools only when a child is old enough to use them under the provider&rsquo;s own age rules. The longer argument is in [is coding still worth learning in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
