One district alone is a city

San Juan de Lurigancho, with 1,176,422 people in 2025, has more people than the whole department of San Martín, which INEI counts at 967,938. San Martín de Porres and Ate each pass 700,000. A coding academy in Miraflores or San Isidro is a long way from most of these families.

The smallest district, Santa María del Mar on the southern beaches, has 994 people. Between those two extremes sit 41 other districts, each with its own schools, traffic and timetable.

Why the map matters for a class

A weekly class that needs a trip across the city costs an afternoon, not an hour. For a working parent with two children in different grades, it often means choosing which child goes.

Live online classes remove the map from the decision. A child in Carabayllo, a teenager in Chorrillos and an adult in La Molina join the same quality of class from the room they are already in.

Online or across town

Is a live online class really as good as going to an academy in Lima?

For coding, yes, as long as it is live and the learner's hands are on the keyboard. A recorded video is not the same thing, and we do not sell recordings as classes.

What a live class keeps

The learner shares their screen, writes the code and gets stuck in front of a real person. The mentor asks what they expected to happen, waits for the answer and only then points to the line that needs changing. That conversation is the class, and it works the same on a video call as across a desk.

What disappears is the journey: no waiting in the car outside the academy, no rush from school to a second building, and no lost class because of a strike, rain or a late bus.

What a Lima home needs

INEI reports that in the second quarter of 2026, 84.7% of households in Lima Metropolitana had an internet service, against 64.5% across the country and 27.1% in rural areas. Most Lima families already have the connection a video class needs.

The rest is simple: a laptop or desktop computer for typed code (a tablet is enough for a six year old's first Scratch weeks), headphones with a microphone, and a quiet corner. We check all three in the free first class.

  1. Test the connection at class time. Run a short video call at the hour you plan to use. Evening use at home is often heavier than the morning.
  2. Put the computer where a parent passes by. Younger children concentrate better, and you see what they build without hovering.
  3. Keep the same slot each week. A fixed habit matters more than the length of any one class.
  4. Ask the learner to explain one thing. After class, a two-minute "show me what you made" tells you more than any report.

Primaria: play first, then type

A first grader drags blocks to make a character jump; by fourth or fifth grade many children want to type real words. We move each child when they are ready, not on a fixed date, and never put a seven year old with a twelve year old.

Secundaria: depth, not gadgets

Teenagers in Lima often have tried a short workshop already. What they rarely get is a year of steady programming: loops, functions, files, testing, then a second language. That is what turns a curious fourteen year old into one who can build something real.

University and adults

Students at Lima universities take Java, Python and data structures alongside their degree; working adults learn Python, data analysis or AI agents around their job. Our oldest learners are in their sixties, and the class waits for them as patiently as for a child.

Families at international and bilingual schools have their own page, English-medium coding classes in Lima, and families with children in primaria can read the Spanish page on clases de programación para niños en inglés. Adults over fifty may prefer the guide to coding after 50 in Peru.

What school covers

Do schools in Lima teach programming?

Not as a timetabled subject. Lima's public and private schools follow the national curriculum, the CNEB, whose secundaria week has no computing area. A few pilots have started, and Lima has the first of them.

The 35-hour week

Under the regular school day, each secundaria grade has 35 teaching hours a week, and each teaching hour lasts 45 minutes. Matemática has 4 of them and Inglés 3. Digital skills sit in competency 28, about working in virtual environments built with ICT, and that competency is transversal: every area is meant to use it, and no area owns it.

So a Lima teenager can spend five years of secundaria using computers in class and never be asked to write a program. Some private schools add their own workshops, which vary a great deal from school to school.

A Python pilot in Lima Norte

In July 2025 MINEDU started its Fábrica de Programadores: a thousand public-school pupils in the last two years of secundaria (4° and 5°) in Lima Norte learn Python towards an international certificate, and those who complete both stages may then take the PCEP exam. MINEDU presents it as a first stage it hopes to spread nationwide.

For the other districts, and for younger children, the same path exists outside school. Our PCEP and PCAP course follows that certificate, and our Python for Teens course reaches the level a student needs before it.

On 16 March 2026, 53,631 schools of Educación Básica Regular reopened across Peru for 6,225,000 students, according to MINEDU. Our classes do not follow that calendar: a family can start in any month, keep the same slot through the July break and the summer, or pause when exams take over.

After 5° de secundaria

What do Lima teenagers face after 5° de secundaria, and can coding help?

Most Lima universities admit students through their own exams, in Spanish, with maths at the centre. We do not prepare students for those exams and promise no result, but the reasoning a good programmer builds is the same reasoning the maths papers reward.

Where programming helps

A student who has written a hundred small programs is used to reading a problem slowly, naming what is known, trying a case by hand and checking an answer before trusting it. Those habits show up in any maths paper, in any language.

Negative marking, as at the Universidad de Lima, also rewards a habit we teach: knowing when you do not know. A programmer learns to test before claiming something works; an exam candidate learns to leave a question blank rather than guess wildly.

Where it does not

The papers are in Spanish and cover school content we do not teach as exam preparation, such as chemistry or humanities. Academias preuniversitarias exist for that, and we are not one.

What we offer a 4° or 5° student is a year of serious programming and mathematical reasoning in English, which also helps with a computing degree once they are in. Our page on Peru's university admission exams explains the processes in more detail.

Taught in English

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. All of our teaching is in English, always live and online, by mentors in India who speak slowly, check what the learner understood and adjust to their accent and pace.

Lima's English, by the numbers

EF's English Proficiency Index 2025 scores Lima city 555, in its "high" band, against 519 for Peru as a whole. Many Lima teenagers and adults already follow a lesson in English without trouble.

Younger children, and learners from Spanish-medium colegios, often need more time. The mentor uses short sentences, shows rather than describes, and repeats a word on screen until it sticks.

English that comes with the code

Python, Java and Scratch are written with English keywords: if, while, print, repeat. A child who learns to code in English learns those words in context, and the error messages a computer prints start to make sense.

For many Lima families this is the reason to choose us: one hour a week of coding is also one hour of listening and speaking English with a patient adult. Our page on learning coding and English together goes further.

A real Lima project

Which Lima air station reads highest, and can we trust the ranking?

SENAMHI, Peru's national weather and water service, runs automatic air-monitoring stations across Lima and shares their hourly readings openly. We use them with older students to teach a rule every analyst needs: before you compare averages, check how much of the time each one actually covers.

The data

The dataset is "Monitoreo de los contaminantes del aire en Lima Metropolitana", which SENAMHI publishes on Peru's national open-data portal with an Open Data Commons Attribution licence (ODC-By); it first appeared on 24 May 2024 and was last changed on 8 September 2025. Our copy, taken on 9 November 2026, holds 577,794 hourly rows from seven stations, from 1 January 2015 to 31 May 2024.

Each row gives a station, a date, an hour, and readings for PM10, PM2.5 (fine particles smaller than 2.5 microns) and nitrogen dioxide. We use only PM2.5, and we use it as a numbers exercise: this page draws no health conclusions from it.

The question

The stations sit in seven districts: Campo de Marte in Jesús María, Carabayllo, Santa Anita, San Borja, San Juan de Lurigancho, San Martín de Porres and Villa María del Triunfo. A student's first idea is natural: average each station's PM2.5 and sort from highest to lowest.

Version 1 does exactly that, and it also prints what share of the station's hours actually carry a PM2.5 value. That last column is the one most beginners never look at.

Look at the last column. No station reported PM2.5 for every hour, and the gaps are very uneven: Carabayllo has a value for 56% of its hours, San Juan de Lurigancho for 76%. Our year-by-year count shows why. Santa Anita and San Martín de Porres have no PM2.5 readings at all in 2020, and Carabayllo has five for the whole of 2017. Each average is taken over a different stretch of time, so the ranking compares different years and seasons, not just different places.

The fix is to compare like with like. Version 2 keeps, for each station, a dictionary from hour to reading, then uses a set intersection to find the hours when all seven stations reported. Only those hours go into the average. On the way it meets a small trap: the dictionary says the hour is written "hhmmss", but one in the morning appears both as "10000" and as "010000". Turning the hour into a number with int() makes both spellings the same key.

San Martín de Porres and San Borja both show 18.7 in Version 1; Python's sort keeps their original order when the keys tie. The fair comparison rests on 10,883 shared hours, about one hour in eight of the period, which is itself worth saying when you report it.

Two stations swap places and the gap between the top three shrinks. Nothing in the data changed; only the question became fair. That is the habit we want a fifteen year old to leave with: when two numbers are compared, ask what each one was measured over.

  1. Ages 8 to 10, on paper. Two children report their average spelling score. One took ten tests, the other two. Who would you believe more, and why?
  2. Ages 10 to 12, in Scratch or Python. Make two lists of seven daily temperatures with some days missing. Average each list, then average only the days both have.
  3. Ages 13 and over. Run Version 1, then change it to print how many PM2.5 readings each station has per year. Which year would you leave out of any comparison?
  4. Going further. Repeat Version 2 for PM10 and for nitrogen dioxide. Does the set of shared hours grow or shrink, and does the ranking hold?
  5. For adults. Write one sentence that reports the Version 2 result honestly, including how much of the period it covers.

The IANA time-zone database (zone America/Lima) shows Peru five hours behind UTC with no summer time since 1994, and INACAL publishes the official hour. India runs five and a half hours ahead of UTC, which puts it ten and a half hours ahead of Lima every day.

Coding classes in Lima: questions and answers

Are the classes in Spanish?

No. Classes are only ever in English, always live and online. Mentors speak clearly, slow down when needed and adjust to each learner's accent and pace; if a child answers with a Spanish word, they hear the English one back. Programming keywords are English everywhere anyway.

Do you have a centre in Lima?

No. We teach only live online, so a family in any of Lima's 43 districts joins in the same way, from home. That is also why nobody needs to cross the city for a class.

From what age can a child in Lima start?

Six is the youngest start, using Scratch blocks that need no typing. Most children move to typed Python around nine. A teenager with no experience begins at 13 in a teen group, and adults begin whenever they like; our oldest learners are in their sixties.

Which course should a Lima teenager take?

Python for Teens is the usual start for 13 to 18 year olds with no experience. After it, students choose Java, web development, app development, AI or the PCEP certificate. IB and IGCSE students can also take our computer science and maths courses.

Do Lima schools teach coding?

Not as a subject of its own. The national curriculum spreads digital skills across all areas, and no computing area appears in the secundaria timetable. In July 2025 MINEDU began a Python pilot, Fábrica de Programadores, for a thousand public-school pupils in Lima Norte.

What time are the classes?

Any day, any time, flexible: choose a batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at the hour that suits your family. Lima never changes its clocks and the mentor's day in India runs ten and a half hours later, so 08:00 in Lima is 18:30 for the mentor.

Will this help with the UNI, PUCP or Universidad de Lima exams?

We do not prepare students for those exams, which are in Spanish, and we promise no admission result. A year of programming builds careful reasoning and checking, which helps in any maths paper, and the university always decides its own admissions.

Can university students and adults in Lima join?

Yes. University students take Java, Python and data structures; working adults usually start with Python and move to data analysis or AI agents. Adults are always grouped with adults, at hours around work.

How are fees charged, and in which currency?

Each month is billed in US dollars, the currency printed on this page, and your bank handles the change into soles. Someone browsing from India is shown rupees. Nothing is charged for the first class, and no card details are taken for it.

Is there a certificate at the end?

Where a course includes one, the learner receives a Modern Age Coders completion certificate. MINEDU, universities and schools play no part in it and it carries no Peruvian academic value; it simply records the projects the learner finished.
