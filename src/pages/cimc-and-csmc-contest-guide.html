CIMC and CSMC contest guide, November 2026

Every November, Waterloo's CEMC runs two contests side by side on the same morning: the Canadian Intermediate Mathematics Contest for Grades 9 and 10 and the Canadian Senior Mathematics Contest for Grades 11, 12 and CÉGEP. They share one shape, a two-hour paper split into six boxed answers and three written solutions, and one awkward fact printed on the cover: the first written question is usually easier than the last few boxed ones. This guide is built from CEMC's contest page, the covers of the 2025 booklets and CEMC's 2025 results booklet. It sets out what the paper asks, how far students got on each question in 2025, an order of attack built on that, a complete model solution of ours, seven practice problems of ours and a four-week plan to Wednesday, November 18, 2026.

One shipping rule, two answers

CEMC has split its shipping rule by contest for schools in Canada. CSMC question booklets are no longer posted; instead the school prints them from a CEMC download area roughly a day ahead of the contest.

CIMC question booklets are still shipped to schools in Canada as before. Either way, the student sees a printed booklet on the desk, and the finished papers travel back to CEMC together for marking.

Official participants differ too

At a school in Canada, a CSMC writer counts as official when the Student Information Form is filled in properly, the student was born in 2008 or later, and the student lives in Canada.

For the CIMC the test is grade, not birth date: a writer is official if they are in Grade 10 or below and complete the form. Unofficial writers are still marked, and CEMC says they remain eligible for certificates and medals.

Two parts, one cover

Part A boxes, Part B arguments, and the line that links them

The front covers of CEMC's 2025 CSMC and CIMC booklets are word for word the same apart from the contest name. They describe a paper in two halves, and one sentence on them should change how a student spends the two hours.

The 2025 CSMC and CIMC booklet covers, quoted (no problems reproduced)

The sentence that matters sits just above Part A: "The early problems in Part B are likely easier than the later problems in Part A." In other words the paper is not one ladder but two, and the bottom rung of the second ladder is lower than the top rungs of the first. A student who works straight through from question A1 will often arrive at Part B with twenty minutes left, having spent forty on two boxed questions worth 5 marks each, while a Part B question worth 10 marks, whose first part is close to routine, waits unread.

Why the parts of a Part B question matter

Part B questions are usually split into (a), (b) and (c) or more, climbing as they go. The early parts are often short and checkable, and each one earns marks on its own even if the last part is never finished.

So "attempt Part B" does not mean "solve Part B". It means collect the parts that are within reach, write them properly, and leave the final part for whatever time remains.

Calculators, briefly

The cover allows calculating devices without internet access, the ability to communicate with other devices, stored notes or programs, a computer algebra system or dynamic geometry software.

On a written solution, a number from the screen is not an argument. If a calculator gave the answer, the solution still has to show where it came from.

Order of attack

A 120-minute plan that respects both ladders

This is the order our students practise with from their first timed paper. It is ours, not CEMC's, and it is built on the cover sentence and the 2025 averages above.

Our order of attack for a two-hour CIMC or CSMC paper (ours)
MinutesWork onRule
0 to 30A1 to A4Box each answer; write a line of working under any answer you are not sure of
30 to 55B1, every partWritten properly the first time, with a closing sentence
55 to 80B2, as many parts as possibleEach part finished before the next is started
80 to 100A5 and A6Ten minutes each, no more; working shown in case it earns part marks
100 to 115B3 part (a), or the unfinished B2 partWhichever is closer to a complete answer
115 to 120EverythingEvery box filled, every written part ending in a sentence that answers it

Why A5 and A6 move to the middle

They are worth 5 marks each and, on 2025 evidence, rarely earned. B1 and B2 are worth 10 each and earn marks part by part. Doing B1 and B2 before A5 and A6 protects the larger, more reliable marks first.

The boxed questions are not abandoned. They are simply taken when a stuck student can no longer lose a written part by lingering.

When to break the plan

A student who reads A5 and sees the idea at once should take it there and then. The plan is for the student who would otherwise stare at it.

A Grade 9 student writing a first CIMC may never reach A5 at all, and that is fine: A1 to A4 plus B1 and part of B2 is a good first paper.

Students resist this at first, because finishing Part A feels tidy. We run the same past paper twice, a week apart, once straight through and once in this order, and compare the two marks. That comparison usually settles the argument better than anything a teacher says.

A written solution of ours

A Part B question with three parts, solved the way markers want

The question is our own wording of a well-known fact about sums of consecutive integers, and the solution is written by us for our CSMC students; none of it comes from a CEMC paper. It shows a feature of good Part B writing that students miss: the parts do not have to be written in the order they are asked, as long as each one is labelled.

What each piece of the write-up is doing (our classroom notes)
PieceIts job
(a) as three sums on one lineA short-answer style part needs the answer, cleanly. Three examples are all part (a) asks for
Writing (c) before (b), and saying soThe proof in (c) makes (b) a one-line consequence. A clear label lets the marker follow the order
The formula 2N = k(2a + k - 1)Turns a sentence about runs into an equation that can be argued about
The parity stepTwo numbers whose sum is odd cannot both be even: this is the idea of the whole proof, stated with its reason
"Both factors are at least 2"Without it, the odd factor might be 1 and the proof would prove nothing. This is the line most first drafts lack
The closing sentenceAnswers all three parts in words, so nothing is left for the marker to assemble

Where the marks hide

Part (b) asked only to "explain why". A student who tests every run length from 2 to 10 and finds nothing has not explained anything, because the list could go on. A student who points to (c) has.

Part (c) is where the 2025 averages say most Part B marks are lost. A proof does not need to be long; it needs every step to have a reason a stranger can check.

A follow-up for strong students

The converse is also true: every positive integer that is not a power of 2 is a run number. Proving it means choosing a and k from an odd factor, and it makes a good extension once (c) feels easy.

We set the converse as homework only after a student can write (c) cleanly from memory in under ten minutes.

Seven practice problems

Original CIMC and CSMC practice, with solutions

All seven are our practice problems, not CEMC problems, and none is adapted from a past paper. Problems 1 to 5 are boxed-answer questions at roughly Part A level; the last two are three-part written questions at roughly B1 and B2 level. Every answer was checked in Python.

Problem 1. Ours, written for this page, not from CEMC. Part A level. A rectangle has perimeter 34 and a diagonal of length 13. What is its area?

Solution. Let the sides be l and w. Then l + w = 17 and l2 + w2 = 169. Since (l + w)2 = l2 + w2 + 2lw, we get 289 = 169 + 2lw, so lw = 60. The area is 60. The sides happen to be 5 and 12, but the question never needs them.

Problem 2. Ours, written for this page, not from CEMC. Part A level. How many positive divisors does 720 have?

Solution. 720 = 24 x 32 x 5. A divisor chooses an exponent of 2 from 0 to 4, of 3 from 0 to 2 and of 5 from 0 to 1, so there are 5 x 3 x 2 = 30 divisors. Listing them one by one is how a student loses ten minutes.

Problem 3. Ours, written for this page, not from CEMC. Part A level. A box holds 3 green and 5 yellow counters. Two counters are taken out at random, one after the other, without putting the first back. What is the probability that the two match in colour?

Solution. Count unordered pairs: 8 x 7 / 2 = 28 equally likely pairs in all. Green pairs: 3 x 2 / 2 = 3. Yellow pairs: 5 x 4 / 2 = 10. The probability is (3 + 10) / 28 = 13/28.

Problem 4. Ours, written for this page, not from CEMC. Part A level. A square is drawn inside a circle of radius 5 with all four corners on the circle. What is the area of the region inside the circle but outside the square? Give an exact answer.

Solution. The diagonal of the square is a diameter, 10, so the area of the square is 102 / 2 = 50. The circle has area 25π, so the region has area 25π - 50. Writing 28.54 would lose the box: the exact form is the answer.

Problem 5. Ours, written for this page, not from CEMC. Late Part A level. How many integers from 1 to 1000 inclusive are divisible by 6 or by 10, but not by 15?

Solution. Divisible by 6: 166. By 10: 100. By both, that is by 30: 33. So 166 + 100 - 33 = 233 are divisible by 6 or 10. A number divisible by 6 and by 15 is divisible by 30, and so is a number divisible by 10 and by 15, so the ones to remove are exactly the 33 multiples of 30. The answer is 233 - 33 = 200. The trap is subtracting the multiples of 15 directly, which counts numbers like 45 that were never in the list.

Problem 6. Ours, written for this page, not from CEMC. Part B level, written solution. A triangle has side lengths 13, 14 and 15. (a) Determine its area. (b) Determine the length of the altitude to the side of length 14. (c) Determine the radius of the circle that touches all three sides.

Problem 7. Ours, written for this page, not from CEMC. Part B level, written solution. Let f(x) = x2 - 6x + 13. (a) Write f(x) in the form (x - p)2 + q. (b) Determine the smallest value of f(x) and the value of x where it occurs. (c) Determine all real numbers x for which f(x) + f(x + 2) = 20.

Solution. (a) x2 - 6x + 13 = (x - 3)2 + 4. (b) A square is never negative, so f(x) is at least 4, with equality exactly when x = 3. The smallest value is 4, at x = 3. (c) Using (a), f(x + 2) = (x - 1)2 + 4. The equation becomes (x - 3)2 + (x - 1)2 + 8 = 20, so 2x2 - 8x + 10 = 12, which is x2 - 4x - 1 = 0. The quadratic formula gives x = 2 + √5 or x = 2 - √5. Both are real numbers and both satisfy the equation, so both are answers.

How to use the set

Sit problems 1 to 5 in 30 minutes with the boxes only, then compare. Any wrong answer with no working shown would have earned nothing on the day.

Write problems 6 and 7 as full solutions, timed at 25 minutes each, then check every line against ours. A missing reason is a missing mark.

The habit underneath

Problems 1 and 7 both reward reusing an earlier result instead of starting again. Problem 5 rewards reading the condition exactly. Problem 4 rewards exact forms.

Those habits are worth more on a November morning than any single formula, and they carry straight into the April contests.

Four weeks

A four-week plan from 22 October to 18 November 2026

For a student whose school has ordered the contest, four weeks is enough to change how the two hours are spent, even if it is not enough to learn new mathematics. Our plan, with two live classes a week:

  1. Week 1, 22 to 28 October 2026: one past paper straight through, untimed. Mark it together. Find out which Part A questions were lost to mathematics and which to time.
  2. Week 2, 29 October to 4 November 2026: Part B writing. Two B1 questions from past papers and our problem 6, written in full and returned marked for completeness, clarity and presentation.
  3. Week 3, 5 to 11 November 2026: the order of attack. One full paper timed at 120 minutes using the plan above, then the same paper reviewed for which marks the order saved.
  4. Week 4, 12 to 18 November 2026: rehearsal and rest. One last timed paper early in the week, a short review of written-solution habits, and nothing heavy the evening before the contest.

For parents

Stage 1 is where most good school students begin, because school tests rarely ask for an argument written for a stranger. The jump to stage 3 is a writing habit, and habits take weekly practice.

A November paper is a useful checkpoint even for a student whose real goal is April. It shows, early in the year, exactly which kind of mark is being lost.

For students

Read all three Part B questions in the first five minutes. Knowing what is waiting changes how long you will tolerate a stuck box.

Label every written part clearly, and end each one with a sentence that answers it.

CIMC and CSMC: common questions

What are the CIMC and the CSMC?

They are the Canadian Intermediate Mathematics Contest and the Canadian Senior Mathematics Contest, run by CEMC at the University of Waterloo on the same day each November. The CIMC is for Grades 9 and 10, the CSMC for Grades 11, 12 and CEGEP.

When are the CIMC and CSMC in 2026?

CEMC lists Wednesday, November 18, 2026 in North and South America and Thursday, November 19, 2026 elsewhere. The school ordering deadline was Thursday, October 22, 2026.

What does the paper look like?

Nine questions in two hours, scored out of 60. Part A has six answer-only questions worth 5 marks each; Part B has three full-solution questions worth 10 marks each, marked for completeness, clarity and presentation.

Should my child finish Part A before starting Part B?

Not necessarily. The booklet cover itself says the early Part B problems are likely easier than the later Part A problems, and in 2025 the first Part B question averaged about 7 out of 10 in both contests. We teach an order that takes the first two Part B questions before the last two boxes.

What were the 2025 averages?

CEMC lists a 2025 mean of 28.6 out of 60 on the CSMC and 29.1 on the CIMC, with a Certificate of Distinction cutoff of 35 on both. There were 15 153 CSMC and 13 039 CIMC contestants.

Can a student register for the CIMC or CSMC alone?

No. CEMC contests are ordered by schools, and the student writes at school under supervision. If your school does not take part, CEMC suggests asking another local school whether your child can join its writers.

Is the CSMC required for University of Waterloo admission?

No. CEMC says the CSMC is not required for admission to the Faculty of Mathematics, and the Faculty says writing the CSMC or the Euclid is required for some scholarship decisions. We make no promises about admissions or scholarships.

Are calculators allowed?

Some are. The 2025 cover allows calculating devices without internet access, communication with other devices, stored notes or programs, a computer algebra system or dynamic geometry software. Written solutions still have to show the reasoning.

What if my child missed the 2026 order?

The next CEMC full-solution contests are the Fryer, Galois and Hypatia Contests on Wednesday, April 7, 2027 for Grades 9 to 11, and the Euclid on Tuesday, April 6, 2027 for the final year. Schools order both by Thursday, March 11, 2027.

When do your CIMC and CSMC classes run?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible: choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays. Times are set in Eastern time and confirmed in your own zone, which matters in November because the eastern provinces have just changed their clocks.
