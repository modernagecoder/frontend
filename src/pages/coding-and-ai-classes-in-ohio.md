# Coding and AI classes in Ohio

**How many Ohio high schools offer computer science, and how many students take it?** In 2023 to 2024, 635 of 1,045 Ohio high schools (60.8%) offered computer science, but only 15,883 of 535,556 high school students, about 2.97%, were enrolled in it, according to Code.org (2025). Ohio is one of the larger school systems in the country, with more than a thousand high schools in the Code.org count, and the course list is not the main problem: most schools have a computer science class somewhere on the timetable. This page sets out the published access and enrollment figures for Ohio, compares them with the national numbers, shows what a student can do with real Columbus city data, and explains how our live online classes fit around the school day from Toledo to Cincinnati.

## Why a course on the timetable can still leave a student out

The figures do not explain themselves, and the sources give no reason for the gap, so we do not invent one. What we can do is list the practical questions an Ohio family can ask.

### Is it at my student&rsquo;s school?

A state percentage hides the school in front of you. Six in ten schools offering a course means four in ten do not, and the published Ohio figures do not tell you which group your building falls in. The counselor or the course catalog will.

### Does it fit the schedule?

An elective that clashes with band, a language or a required course is offered on paper and closed in practice. Asking which period it runs in, and whether it runs every year, settles that quickly.

### Is it the right level?

One introductory class is a start, not a pathway. Students who want AP Computer Science A, or data and AI work, often find that the next step is not on their school&rsquo;s list at all.

**Questions worth asking at an Ohio school, and what to do with the answer (this column of advice is ours)**

| Ask the school | If the answer is no |
|---|---|
| Is there a computer science course in grades 9 to 12 here? | Start with an outside course at the matching level so the skills are in place if a class opens later |
| Is AP Computer Science A or Principles taught, and in which grade? | Study the course content outside school; the College Board explains on its own site how students register for an exam |
| Can a middle schooler take an introductory class? | Begin with Scratch or Python at home so ninth grade is not the first contact with code |
| Is there a coding club or a competition team? | Look at national contests such as the Congressional App Challenge, which a student can enter from home |

None of this replaces the school. Our classes are an addition for students who want more than one course can hold, or who cannot get into the course their school offers. We make no claim that a class with us earns Ohio high school credit; that decision belongs to the school district.

## How many trees does Columbus actually look after? A record is not a tree

The City of Columbus publishes its Public Owned Trees layer as open data under a CC0 public domain dedication. A first count looks easy. The lesson is in discovering how many rows are not trees at all.

**From raw records to named trees, step by step (our counts from the City of Columbus Public Owned Trees layer, queried 17 October 2026)**

| Step | Filter applied | Records left |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Every record in the layer | 316,529 |
| 2 | Drop records with a RETIRED date (55,904 of them) | 260,625 |
| 3 | Drop vacant planting sites, codes VSL, VSM and VSS (86,152 left after step 2) | 174,473 |
| 4 | Drop the catch-all code labelled &ldquo;Unlnown or other live tree&rdquo; (2,936) | 171,537 |

**The most common named species after step 2, by the city&rsquo;s own SP_CODE labels (our counts)**

| City label | Records without a RETIRED date |
|---|---|
| Maple, Red | 13,296 |
| Honeylocust | 8,602 |
| Pear, Callery | 8,552 |
| Maple, Sugar | 7,278 |
| Maple, Freeman | 6,507 |
| Planetree, London | 5,761 |

### Coded values

Species arrive as short codes such as ACRU and GLTR. The layer&rsquo;s own domain lists 306 codes with their names; 287 of them appear in the records, and three codes covering six records are missing from the list entirely, which a careful script has to report rather than drop silently.

### Categories inside categories

There is no single maple code. Students match every label that begins with Maple and get 46,387 records without a RETIRED date, about 27.0% of the 171,537 named trees: a genus count the city does not publish, built by the student.

### Filters change rankings

Ash shows the danger of skipping step 2. Across all records, labels beginning with Ash number 18,598; once retired records are removed, 2,274 remain. The data does not say why records were retired, so neither do we.

In Python the core of step 3 fits on one line: live = [r for r in rows if r["RETIRED"] is None and r["SP_CODE"] not in ("VSL", "VSM", "VSS")]. Students write it, then test it against a count the server returns, so they learn to trust a filter only after checking it. The typo in &ldquo;Unlnown&rdquo; is left exactly as the city published it, a small reminder that real labels are typed by people.

### These counts are ours, not the city&rsquo;s

Every number in this section is our own query or arithmetic on one day&rsquo;s copy of the layer. The City of Columbus does not publish a tree total in this form, and a record count is not an official inventory. Saying precisely which filter produced which number is the habit the project exists to build.

## Ohio is on Eastern Time, and our teachers are in India

The IANA time zone database lists Ohio among the Eastern Time states, kept under the America/New_York zone.

### An example after school

A 4:30 p.m. class in Dayton during daylight saving starts at 2:00 a.m. the next day in India; after the clocks go back it is 3:00 a.m. in India. Our teachers work those hours so the class sits in the Ohio afternoon or evening.

### Why the gap changes

India keeps one offset all year, UTC plus 5 hours 30 minutes. Ohio moves between UTC minus 4 in summer and UTC minus 5 in winter, so the difference is 9 hours 30 minutes or 10 hours 30 minutes.

### The two Sundays to watch

Under the US rule in the IANA data, clocks fall back at 2 a.m. on Sunday, November 1, 2026 and spring forward on Sunday, March 14, 2027. We confirm every class time in Eastern Time around both dates.

**A bug worth meeting early** A reminder app that stores the offset as a fixed number of hours drifts by one hour twice a year in Ohio. Students who write their own scheduler with the America/New_York zone name, rather than a typed offset, have learned something many working programmers learn the hard way.

## Could an AI tool have counted the Columbus trees?

It could have produced a number. Whether the number meant anything is a different question.

Ask a chatbot to count trees in a downloaded file and it will happily return 316,529, the number of rows. Nothing in that answer warns that more than eighty thousand of those rows are empty planting sites, or that tens of thousands are retired. The tool did what it was told; the instruction was the problem.

A student who has written the filters by hand knows which questions to ask before trusting any total, from their own script or from an AI assistant. That is the skill we teach alongside the syntax: read the field descriptions, check the codes, compare against a second count, and say exactly what the number is.

Ohio students who learn to question a result will use AI tools well; students who only learn to prompt will repeat whatever the first answer said. The longer argument is in [whether coding is still worth learning in 2026](/blog/is-coding-worth-learning-2026).
