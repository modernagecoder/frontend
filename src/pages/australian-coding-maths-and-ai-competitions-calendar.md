# Australian coding and maths competitions

Australian students have more coding, maths and computational thinking competitions open to them than most families realise, and they are spread across the whole school year. Some take forty-five minutes in a classroom; others ask for months of project work. The trouble is that dates live on a dozen different websites, often in PDFs, and change every year. This page gathers the 2026 calendar in one place, with every date checked at the organiser's own site, explains which competitions suit which students, and shows how to plan preparation backwards from the day so nothing is crammed. We are not affiliated with any of these organisers. We help students get ready.

## Matching a competition to a student

The right first competition depends on what a student enjoys and how they like to work, not only on their year level.

**A starting guide, by stage and interest**

| Stage | Likes puzzles and logic | Likes numbers | Likes building things |
|---|---|---|---|
| Years 3 and 4 | Bebras | Kangourou sans Frontières, Maths Challenge | A first Scratch project, ahead of later team events |
| Years 5 and 6 | Bebras, CAT | APSMO Maths Olympiad, AMC | Raspberry Pi Foundation Coding Challenge, Young ICT Explorers |
| Years 7 to 10 | CAT | AMC, AIMO | Australian Informatics Olympiad, Young ICT Explorers |
| Years 11 and 12 | Bebras senior division | AMC senior division | Australian Informatics Olympiad, Young ICT Explorers |

A short first competition is usually wiser than an ambitious one. Bebras and the CAT need no coding experience at all, according to the AMT, and they show quickly whether a student enjoys algorithmic puzzles. The AIO is different: the AMT says students need some programming experience and must be able to write code.

For younger students, the point of a first competition is the experience of thinking hard for a sustained period. A result is information, not a verdict.

Project competitions such as Young ICT Explorers suit students who like making something over weeks. They reward planning, teamwork and explaining a project to judges, which a timed paper never tests.

Many students enjoy one maths contest and one computing contest in the same year. The calendar above shows how they can be spaced so preparation for one does not crowd out the other.

## Backward planning from the competition date

Preparation goes better when it is planned backwards from the day, so the hardest practice happens early and the final week is about calm and timing.

### 1. Fix the date

Start from the competition day or window. Mark it, then count back in weeks. Everything else hangs from that one date.

### 2. Place the stages

Put new skills furthest from the date, mixed practice in the middle, and timed practice close to the end. Leave the last few days light.

### 3. Check progress

At each stage, one honest question: can the student now do the thing this stage was for? If not, move the next stage, not the date.

**A worked plan, counting back from the Australian Informatics Olympiad on 27 August 2026**

| Weeks before | Approximate dates | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 12 to 9 | Early June to late June | Core skills: loops, arrays, reading input carefully |
| 8 to 5 | Early July to late July | Problem patterns: searching, sorting, simple dynamic programming |
| 4 to 2 | Early August to mid August | Timed practice on past-style problems, reviewing every mistake |
| Final week | 20 to 26 August | Light practice, rest, and a plan for reading every task first |

The same backward plan works for a primary student and Bebras, only shorter: two weeks of puzzle play, one week of timed sets, then a quiet day before. What matters is the direction of planning. Students who plan forwards from today tend to discover the date too late; students who plan backwards from the date know each week what the week is for.

## Four rungs from first contest to olympiad

Most students climb gradually, and the rungs matter more than the year.

**From a first competition to olympiad-level work**

| Around | Rung | The sign it is secure |
|---|---|---|
| Years 3 to 5 | 1. Enjoying puzzles | Sticks with a hard puzzle for twenty minutes |
| Years 5 to 7 | 2. Explaining reasoning | Can say why an answer is right, not just that it is |
| Years 7 to 9 | 3. Writing algorithms | Turns a method into steps, then into working code |
| Years 9 to 12 | 4. Olympiad problems | Attacks unfamiliar problems with a plan and checks the result |

### If a result disappoints

One paper on one day is a narrow sample. Look at which questions were attempted and how, not only at the score.
A student who keeps entering, with steady preparation between, usually sees more change than one who crams once.

## Weekly classes, fitted around the school term

Teaching happens from India, where the clock trails Australia: Adelaide is four hours ahead of India time in winter, Perth two and a half all year. That gap puts Australian after-school sessions in the Indian working afternoon, which is why weekly competition practice is easy to schedule.

- **Weekday after school** Steady weekly practice through the term.
- **Weekend morning** Longer problem-solving sessions.
- **Before a competition** A short timed-practice block in the last weeks.

- **Problem solving first** Students learn to read, plan and check, not to memorise answers.
- **Our own problems** Practice written by us, in the style competitions use, never copied papers.
- **Squads of five to ten** Competitors at a similar standard, arguing over methods until one is clearly right.
- **Individual coaching** For an olympiad hopeful or a student repairing one weak area.
- **Planned backwards** Each student's plan is built from their competition date.
- **No promises** No score, medal or selection is promised.

## What Australian families ask

### What is the Bebras challenge?

A computational thinking challenge for students in Years 3 to 12. In Australia it is delivered by the Australian Maths Trust; in 2026 it ran from 9 February to 8 March. No coding is needed.

### What is the Australian Informatics Olympiad?

A programming competition run by the Australian Maths Trust for Years 7 to 12. In 2026 it was held on 27 August and ran for 3 hours. The AMT says students need some programming experience and must be able to write code.

### When was the AMC in 2026?

The Australian Mathematics Competition ran from 4 to 6 August 2026, for Years 3 to 12, with 60 minutes for primary and 75 minutes for secondary students.

### Is there a coding competition for primary students?

Yes. Bebras and the CAT suit primary students and need no coding, and the Raspberry Pi Foundation Coding Challenge is open from Year 5 with divisions by skill level.

### What is backward planning?

Planning preparation from the competition date back to today: new skills furthest from the date, mixed practice in the middle, timed practice near the end, and a light final week.

### How do students enter?

Usually through their school. Some organisers accept individual or team registrations. Each organiser's page sets out who can enter and how.
