In short

A good coding class for an elementary student matches the grade. In grades 1 and 2, children give exact step-by-step instructions, use loops and events in block code, and learn to describe a bug. In grades 3 to 5 they add conditions and variables, build Scratch games, and around age 9 or 10 can start typed Python. Keep classes live and short. Grades and ages

What counts as elementary school in the US, and how old the children are

Families searching for coding classes for a third grader or a fifth grader are really asking about a grade band, so it helps to start with how US schools define one.

The federal definition

The National Center for Education Statistics, which collects school data for the US Department of Education, defines an elementary school as "A school that offers more of kindergarten through grade 4 than grades 5 through 8 and no grades 9 through 12."

That wording explains a common source of confusion. Many districts run kindergarten to grade 5 under one roof, while others move fifth graders, and sometimes fourth graders, into a middle or intermediate school. A fifth grader can therefore be in an elementary school in one town and a middle school in the next.

Why the grade matters more than the building

For learning to code, what matters is reading level, typing comfort and attention span, which track the grade far better than the name on the school sign. We place children by grade and by a short look at what they can already do.

Our classes start at age 6, which is first grade for most children. Kindergarten-age children are usually better served by unplugged games at home, a few of which appear further down this page.

By grade 2 and by grade 5

What US students are expected to understand by second and fifth grade

The K-12 Computer Science Framework is the shared reference behind much US computer science teaching: its site says it informs state- and district-level decisions, and it was developed by the Association for Computing Machinery, Code.org, the Computer Science Teachers Association and other partners working with states and districts. It describes what students should understand at the end of grade 2 and at the end of grade 5.

Grade 2 and grade 5 endpoints in the framework’s own words, taken from k12cs.org on 17 October 2026
IdeaEnd of grade 2End of grade 5
Algorithms"People follow and create processes as part of daily life. Many of these processes can be expressed as algorithms that computers can follow."Comparing different ways to reach the same result
Control"Computers follow precise sequences of instructions that automate tasks."Loops, event handlers and conditionals (see the program below)
Troubleshooting"Clearly describing a problem is the first step toward finding a solution."Common strategies such as "checking that power is available, checking that physical and wireless connections are working, and clearing out the working memory by restarting programs or devices"
Breaking tasks down"Complex tasks can be broken down into simpler instructions, some of which can be broken down even further."Programs split into smaller parts that are easier to design and review
CybersecurityUsing authentication such as strong passwords to protect devices and information"Information can be protected using various security measures. These measures can be physical and/or digital."
Working with dataShowing data as charts and picture graphs"People select aspects and subsets of data to be transformed, organized, clustered, and categorized" to give different views of it

What this means at home

The grade 2 expectations are about precision and patience: a child who can tell a friend exactly how to draw a square, step by step, and then spot the step that went wrong, is doing the work the framework describes.

By grade 5 the list grows up. Children are expected to choose how to organize data, keep accounts safe and work through a broken device or program methodically instead of giving up after the first try.

What the framework is not

It is not a test and it does not set a lesson for each grade. It gives grade-band endpoints, and states may use it to develop their own standards, so your child’s school may cover these ideas earlier, later or inside another subject.

The framework itself says it can be taught as standalone courses or folded into other subjects, "particularly at the elementary and middle school levels". That is why some children meet coding in a library period and others in a math lesson.

Where these lines come from: the grade-band statements on k12cs.org and its questions and answers page, both checked on 17 October 2026. Useful facts from the catalogue

Code.org describes the series as "Six comprehensive courses, one for each elementary grade." It says each course is approximately a month long and lists computers, Chromebooks and tablets as supported devices.

A separate Code.org pathway, Computer Science Connections, has 11 units for grades 3 to 5 that link coding with math, language arts, science and social studies, for example coding an interactive book cover or a geometric star quilt.

Where a live class fits alongside it

A month-long course inside a busy school year gives a good taste, and then often stops. Children who enjoyed it tend to ask for more, and that is where a weekly class with a regular teacher helps: the same ideas, taken further, with someone who notices exactly where your child gets stuck.

When a new student tells us they did Course C or Course D at school, we start from there rather than repeating it. We have no tie to Code.org or CodeAI; we simply find their catalogue a clear record of what US classrooms cover.

Try it tonight

A fifteen-minute unplugged activity for any elementary grade

You do not need a computer to see whether your child enjoys this kind of thinking. This is the grid-robot game we use in first lessons, written out so you can run it at the kitchen table with paper, a pencil and a coin.

  1. Draw a 4 by 4 grid on paper. Put a coin, the robot, in the bottom-left square, facing up the page. Draw a star in any other square.
  2. Agree on four commands and nothing else: F (forward one square), L (turn left), R (turn right) and P (pick up).
  3. Your child writes the program as a row of letters on a strip of paper, without touching the coin.
  4. You are the computer. Follow the strip exactly, even when it is obviously wrong. A computer does not guess what you meant, and this is the moment children understand that.
  5. If the robot misses the star, debug together. Ask your child to point at the first letter where the robot went somewhere unexpected, and describe the problem in one sentence before fixing it.
  6. Make it harder with a wall square the robot must go around, or a rule that the program can be no longer than eight letters.

What to listen for

In first and second grade, the win is a child who says "the robot turned the wrong way at the third letter" instead of "it does not work". That sentence is the whole skill of debugging in miniature.

In third grade and up, listen for "I could say F three times" on puzzle 3. That is a child inventing the idea of a loop before anyone names it.

If it goes badly

Some children find it frustrating to be followed literally. Swap roles: let them be the computer while you write a slightly wrong program, and enjoy watching them catch you out.

Keep it short. Fifteen minutes that ends with a solved puzzle beats forty minutes that ends in tears, and the game is just as good tomorrow.

Grades 4 and 5

A first typed program for fourth and fifth graders

Somewhere around age 9 or 10, many children who have built Scratch games are ready to type real code. This is the kind of first Python program we write with them: short, readable, and built from the same three ideas they already know from blocks.

A variable

fuel remembers a number and changes as the program runs, just like a score in Scratch.

A loop

for second in range(10, 0, -1) counts down from 10 to 1, the typed version of a repeat block.

A condition

if second <= 3 decides what to print, like an if block that checks the timer.

How we teach it

Children type it themselves, line by line, and run it after every two or three lines. Typing slowly is fine; copying and pasting is not, because the typing is where they notice the colon, the indentation and the brackets.

Then we change one thing at a time. What happens if the count starts at 20? What if fuel goes up by 5? A child who can predict the new last line before running it has understood the loop.

Is my child ready for typed code?

Three signs: they read instructions without help, they can type a short sentence in under a minute, and they have finished at least one block-coding project of their own design. Age matters less than those three.

If they are not there yet, Scratch has plenty of depth left. Our Python course for kids is built for ages 9 to 12, and nobody is rushed into it.

Across the school day

How coding shows up in reading, math and science lessons

Elementary teachers often weave coding into other subjects rather than teaching it alone, which is also how the framework imagines it. These are the links we draw in class, so a child sees that the same thinking works everywhere.

Why the links help

A seven-year-old who has just counted five moves and replaced them with one repeat block has learned something about multiplication as well as loops. Children enjoy noticing that two subjects are secretly the same idea.

It also helps children who are not yet confident readers. A story told in code, scene by scene, gives them a reason to read short sentences carefully and in order.

What we avoid

We do not turn coding into extra homework. If a school project already needs a chart or a story, a child can choose to build it in code with us, but nothing is assigned on top of school.

We also keep screens in proportion. Some lessons for first and second graders are half unplugged, with paper, cards and the grid robot, because the thinking matters more than the device.

Grade by grade

Five stages through the elementary years

A rough route, not a timetable. Children arrive at different stages and move at their own speed.

Stages of an elementary coder (ours)
StageYou will notice
1. Instruction giverGives exact steps aloud or with arrows, fixes a wrong step when shown
2. Block builderSnaps together sequences, repeats and events to make a sprite move
3. Game makerBuilds a Scratch game with a score, a timer and a win or lose rule
4. PlannerSketches a project on paper first, breaks it into parts, tests each one
5. First typistWrites and changes a short Python program, predicts what it will print

For parents

Stages 1 and 2 often take longer than parents expect, and that is healthy. Precision is the foundation everything else stands on.

Ask to see the project, and ask your child to explain one part of it. Explaining is where the learning sticks.

For kids

Every coder writes programs that do not work the first time. Finding the broken step is the fun part.

Make something you would actually want to play, then show it to someone at home.

Questions from parents

Coding for elementary students: common questions

What grade should a child start coding?

First grade, around age 6, is a comfortable start for block coding and puzzles. Typed code such as Python usually suits fourth or fifth grade, around age 9 or 10, once a child reads and types with some ease.

What should an elementary student learn in coding?

The K-12 Computer Science Framework expects children by the end of grade 2 to follow and create step-by-step processes, use sequences, loops and events, and describe a problem clearly. By the end of grade 5 they add conditionals, variables and breaking programs into parts.

Is Scratch good for elementary students?

Yes for most children from second or third grade. It teaches events, loops, conditions and variables with blocks, so children can build real games before they can type quickly.

Can a 3rd grader learn Python?

Some can, but most third graders do better finishing Scratch projects first. We usually introduce Python in fourth or fifth grade, and our Python course for kids is built for ages 9 to 12.

What coding do elementary schools teach?

It varies by state and district. Many US classrooms use Code.org CS Fundamentals, a free series with one course for each elementary grade from kindergarten to grade 5, each about a month long.

Does my child need a computer for coding classes?

Yes, a laptop, desktop or Chromebook with a keyboard. Tablets can run some block-coding apps, but a keyboard makes typed code and longer projects much easier.

How many children are in a class?

Batches hold fifteen to twenty children of a similar grade with one regular teacher. Private 1-on-1 lessons are the other option.

What times are classes for US families?

Weekdays or weekends, flexible. Choose your batch, or for a very personalised plan choose 1-on-1 at your convenience, weekend mornings or weekdays.

Can we try coding at home before signing up?

Yes. Try the grid-robot game on this page: draw a 4 by 4 grid, agree on four commands, and let your child write a program for you to follow exactly. You can also book a free first lesson.

How much do coding classes for elementary students cost?

Nothing for the first lesson. Fees are then paid month by month, and the fees section further up shows the monthly amount in US dollars.
